Chapter 0: Introduction: components and exponential smoothing (1/2)

Slide 9 in the PDF

A series decomposes into a trend \(T_t\), a seasonal component \(S_t\) and a remainder \(R_t\). In the additive model, \(y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t\), the seasonal amplitude is constant in the units of the series; in the multiplicative model, \(y_t = T_t S_t R_t\), it grows with the level. The logarithm turns the product into a sum, so a multiplicative model for \(y_t\) is an additive model for \(\ln y_t\). For this reason almost all the macroeconomic series of the course are analysed in logs.

Simple exponential smoothing (SES) updates a level with a weighted average of the new observation and the previous level:

\[\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1 - \alpha)\ell_{t-1}, \qquad \alpha \in (0, 1).\]
(15.1)

An \(\alpha\) close to 1 follows the latest observation closely, and a small \(\alpha\) smooths heavily. Accuracy is measured by the MASE (mean absolute scaled error): the mean absolute error (MAE) of the forecast divided by the in-sample MAE of the seasonal naive method. A value below 1 means the method beats the naive benchmark; the scaling makes series with different units comparable.

In brief

  • Key formulas
    • additive \(y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t\) or multiplicative \(y_t = T_t S_t R_t\); the logarithm turns one into the other
    • SES \(\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1 - \alpha)\ell_{t-1}\); MASE \(=\) MAE \(/\) MAE of the in-sample seasonal naive method
  • Notation
    • \(T_t\), \(S_t\), \(R_t\): the trend, the seasonal component and the remainder (the irregular part)
    • \(\ell_t\): the smoothed level at time \(t\); \(\alpha \in (0, 1)\): the weight of the newest observation; a large \(\alpha\) follows the data closely
    • MAE: the mean absolute error; MASE below 1: better than the seasonal naive method

Chapter 0: Introduction: components and exponential smoothing (2/2)

Slide 10 in the PDF

Romanian GDP has grown 2.3 times since 1995, that is 2.8% a year, and the seasonal component is large: the first quarter is about 23.8% below the trend and the fourth quarter 20.7% above it. For EUR/RON, the estimated smoothing parameter is \(\hat\alpha = 1.00\), so the best forecast is the last observed value, the behaviour of a random walk. For electricity consumption, the Holt–Winters method, which adds trend and seasonality, has a MASE of 0.51, against 0.82 for the naive method.

The first common mistake is judging a forecast on the data used for estimation: in sample, a more complex model almost always fits better, which says nothing about the future. The second is using the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) for series that pass through zero, such as monthly inflation: dividing by values close to zero makes the error arbitrarily large. The third is an additive model for a seasonal swing that grows with the level, where the logarithm or the multiplicative model is the right choice.

Chapter 1: Stochastic processes and stationarity (1/2)

Slide 11 in the PDF

A process is weakly stationary if its mean is constant, \(E X_t = \mu\), and its autocovariance \(\mathrm{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+h}) = \gamma(h)\) depends only on the distance \(h\), not on the time \(t\). Stationarity makes estimation possible: if the properties of the series do not change over time, observations from different periods inform about the same parameters. The sample autocorrelation \(\hat\rho(h)\) is compared with the band \(\pm 1.96/\sqrt{T}\), the approximate 95% band for white noise.

The Ljung–Box test (Ljung and Box, 1978) combines the first \(m\) autocorrelations into one statistic:

\[Q^*(m) = T(T + 2)\sum_{h=1}^{m}\frac{\hat\rho(h)^2}{T - h} \sim \chi^2(m).\]
(15.2)

A large value, that is a small p-value, rejects white noise. The random walk \(X_t = X_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) is the basic example of a non-stationary series: its variance, \(\mathrm{Var}(X_t) = t\sigma^2\), grows linearly in time, so there is no constant variance to estimate.

In brief

  • Key formulas
    • weak stationarity: \(E X_t = \mu\), \(\mathrm{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+h}) = \gamma(h)\); \(\hat\rho(h)\) against \(\pm 1.96/\sqrt{T}\)
    • Ljung–Box \(Q^*(m) = T(T + 2)\sum_{h=1}^{m}\hat\rho(h)^2/(T - h) \sim \chi^2(m)\) Ljung and Box (1978); random walk \(\mathrm{Var}(X_t) = t\sigma^2\)
  • Notation
    • \(\mu\): the constant mean; \(\gamma(h)\): the autocovariance at lag \(h\), which depends only on \(h\); \(\hat\rho(h)\): the sample autocorrelation; \(T\): the number of observations
    • \(Q^*(m)\): the Ljung–Box statistic on the first \(m\) lags; a large value (a small p-value) rejects white noise
    • \(\sigma^2\): the variance of the random-walk shocks; \(\mathrm{Var}(X_t) = t\sigma^2\) grows with \(t\), so a random walk is not stationary

Chapter 1: Stochastic processes and stationarity (2/2)

Slide 12 in the PDF

Daily BET returns have a first-order autocorrelation of 0.11, and absolute returns have 0.40, both against a band of \(\pm 0.024\). The gap between the two numbers is one of the central ideas of the course: a series can be almost uncorrelated without being independent. The size of returns is predictable even when their sign is not, and this observation prepares the GARCH models of Chapter 5.

For Romanian GDP, the Box–Cox transformation has the estimated parameter \(\hat\lambda = 0.02\), practically the logarithm. One difference too many applied to a series that is already stationary gives a first-order autocorrelation of \(-0.54\): over-differencing introduces an artificial negative autocorrelation, the sign of a unit root in the MA part. The other common mistake is reading a single bar outside the band as a signal: at 20 lags, one falls outside the band by chance, even for white noise.

  • Empirical facts

Chapter 2: ARMA models (1/2)

Slide 13 in the PDF

The ARMA\((p, q)\) model is written with the lag operator \(L\), defined by \(LX_t = X_{t-1}\):

\[\phi(L)(X_t - \mu) = \theta(L)\varepsilon_t,\]
(15.3)

where \(\phi(L)\) and \(\theta(L)\) are the AR and MA polynomials, of orders \(p\) and \(q\), and \(\varepsilon_t\) is white noise. The two simple cases give the identification patterns: an AR(1) has autocorrelations \(\rho(h) = \phi^h\), which decay geometrically, and an MA(1) has a single non-zero autocorrelation, \(\rho(1) = \theta/(1 + \theta^2)\), and cuts off after lag 1.

Among the candidate models we choose with information criteria. The Akaike criterion (Akaike, 1974), \(\mathrm{AIC} = -2\ln\hat{\mathcal{L}} + 2k\), and the Schwarz criterion (Schwarz, 1978), \(\mathrm{BIC} = -2\ln\hat{\mathcal{L}} + k\ln T\), start from the maximised log-likelihood and penalise the number \(k\) of parameters; smaller is better. The BIC penalty exceeds the AIC penalty as soon as \(\ln T > 2\), that is for \(T \ge 8\), so the BIC picks smaller models. After estimation, the Ljung–Box test on the residuals uses \(m - p - q\) degrees of freedom, because the estimated parameters absorb part of the autocorrelation.

In brief

  • Key formulas
    • \(\phi(L)(X_t - \mu) = \theta(L)\varepsilon_t\); AR(1) \(\rho(h) = \phi^h\); MA(1) \(\rho(1) = \theta/(1 + \theta^2)\)
    • AIC \(= -2\ln\hat{\mathcal{L}} + 2k\) Akaike (1974), BIC \(= -2\ln\hat{\mathcal{L}} + k\ln T\) Schwarz (1978); residual check \(Q^*(m) \sim \chi^2(m - p - q)\)
  • Notation
    • \(L\): the lag operator, \(LX_t = X_{t-1}\); \(\phi(L)\), \(\theta(L)\): the AR and MA polynomials of orders \(p\) and \(q\); \(\varepsilon_t\): white noise
    • \(\ln\hat{\mathcal{L}}\): the maximised log-likelihood; \(k\): the number of estimated parameters; a smaller AIC or BIC is better
    • BIC penalises parameters more than AIC, since \(\ln T > 2\) for \(T \ge 8\)

Chapter 2: ARMA models (2/2)

Slide 14 in the PDF

For Romanian annual GDP growth, the BIC chooses an MA(3). The explanation is the construction of the series: growth on the same quarter of the previous year is computed over overlapping four-quarter windows, and the overlap creates a dependence that stops after three lags. The residuals of the model have a Ljung–Box p-value of 0.37, so the test does not separate the remaining autocorrelation from noise. Romanian inflation fits an AR(2) with \(\hat\phi_1 + \hat\phi_2 = 0.976\), very close to a unit root, and sunspots have an AR(2) cycle of 10.6 years.

The first common mistake is reading the AR order from the ACF: for an AR process it is the PACF that cuts off, not the ACF. The second is the Ljung–Box test on residuals with \(m\) degrees of freedom instead of \(m - p - q\), which makes the test too lenient. The third is a common factor in \(\phi(L)\) and \(\theta(L)\): if the two polynomials share a root, it cancels, and the model has parameters that cannot be identified.

In brief

  • Empirical facts
    • annual growth of Romanian GDP: MA(3) by BIC, a product of overlapping quarters; residual Ljung–Box \(p = 0.37\)
    • Romanian inflation: AR(2) with \(\hat\phi_1 + \hat\phi_2 = 0.976\), close to a unit root; sunspots: an AR(2) cycle of 10.6 years
  • Common mistakes
    • reading the AR order from the ACF (it is the PACF that cuts off for an AR)
    • Ljung–Box on residuals with \(m\) instead of \(m - p - q\) degrees of freedom; a common factor in \(\phi(L)\) and \(\theta(L)\)

Chapter 3: Unit roots and ARIMA models (1/2)

Slide 15 in the PDF

The augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test (Dickey and Fuller, 1979) estimates the regression

\[\Delta y_t = c + bt + \gamma y_{t-1} + \sum_{j}\delta_j\Delta y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t\]
(15.4)

and tests \(H_0\): \(\gamma = 0\), that is a unit root. The lagged differences, with coefficients \(\delta_j\), remove the autocorrelation of the errors. Under \(H_0\) the \(t\) statistic of \(\gamma\) does not follow the Student distribution but a distribution of its own, with more negative critical values. The KPSS test (Kwiatkowski et al., 1992) reverses the hypotheses: \(H_0\) is stationarity. Used together, the two tests give four combinations of decisions, two of them clear and two inconclusive.

The ARIMA\((p, d, q)\) model applies an ARMA to the series differenced \(d\) times:

\[\phi(L)(1 - L)^d y_t = c + \theta(L)\varepsilon_t.\]
(15.5)

When \(d \ge 1\), the variance of the forecast error grows with the horizon \(h\) without bound, because shocks have a permanent effect on the level. This is the practical difference between a stochastic and a deterministic trend: around a deterministic trend, forecast intervals keep a bounded width.

In brief

  • Key formulas
    • ADF: \(\Delta y_t = c + bt + \gamma y_{t-1} + \sum_j\delta_j\Delta y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t\), \(H_0\): \(\gamma = 0\) Dickey and Fuller (1979); KPSS: \(H_0\) stationarity Kwiatkowski et al. (1992)
    • ARIMA\((p,d,q)\): \(\phi(L)(1 - L)^d y_t = c + \theta(L)\varepsilon_t\); forecast variance grows with \(h\) when \(d \ge 1\)
  • Notation
    • \(\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}\); \(c\): the constant; \(bt\): the deterministic trend; \(\gamma = 0\) means a unit root
    • \(\delta_j\): the coefficients of the lagged differences, which remove the autocorrelation of the errors
    • \(d\): the number of differences needed for stationarity; \((1 - L)^d\): the difference of order \(d\); \(h\): the forecast horizon

Chapter 3: Unit roots and ARIMA models (2/2)

Slide 16 in the PDF

For the log of Romanian GDP, the ADF statistic is \(\tau = -2.31\), with p-value 0.43, and the 5% critical value is \(-3.45\); the unit root is not rejected. For growth, \(\tau = -10.77\) rejects clearly. The resulting model is an ARIMA(0,1,0) with a drift of 0.81% per quarter: a random walk with average growth. The experiment of Granger and Newbold (1974) shows the danger of regressions between integrated series: for two independent random walks, the \(t\) statistic of the slope is significant in 90% of regressions.

The ADF test has low power: for \(\phi = 0.95\) and \(T = 100\), it rejects the unit root in only 12.5% of cases. Non-rejection therefore does not prove a unit root; it only shows that the data cannot separate it from a root close to 1. The other common mistakes are Student or Normal critical values for the ADF statistic and a regression in levels between \(I(1)\) series without a cointegration test.

In brief

  • Empirical facts

Chapter 4: Seasonality and forecasting (1/2)

Slide 17 in the PDF

The SARIMA model multiplies an ordinary ARIMA by a seasonal one, written in \(L^s\):

\[\phi(L)\Phi(L^s)(1 - L)^d(1 - L^s)^D y_t = \theta(L)\Theta(L^s)\varepsilon_t,\]
(15.6)

where \(s\) is the length of the season, 12 for monthly data, \(\Phi\) and \(\Theta\) are the seasonal polynomials and \(D\) is the number of seasonal differences \((1 - L^s)\). The airline model, \((0,1,1)(0,1,1)_s\), with one ordinary difference, one seasonal difference and one MA term in each part, is the classic starting point for monthly series with trend and seasonality.

To compare two forecasts we use the Diebold–Mariano test (Diebold and Mariano, 1995). For each period the loss differential \(d_t = L(e_{1t}) - L(e_{2t})\) is computed, for example the difference of squared errors, and the statistic is the mean \(\bar d\) divided by its standard error, \(\bar d/\sqrt{\hat V/n}\), with \(\hat V\) the long-run variance of \(d_t\). Under equal accuracy the statistic is approximately \(N(0, 1)\), so \(|\mathrm{DM}| > 1.96\) rejects at 5%. The correction of Harvey, Leybourne and Newbold (1997) (HLN) adjusts the statistic for small samples and compares it with the Student distribution.

In brief

  • Key formulas
    • SARIMA \(\phi(L)\Phi(L^s)(1 - L)^d(1 - L^s)^D y_t = \theta(L)\Theta(L^s)\varepsilon_t\); airline \((0,1,1)(0,1,1)_s\)
    • Diebold–Mariano \(\bar d/\sqrt{\hat V/n}\), \(d_t = L(e_{1t}) - L(e_{2t})\) Diebold and Mariano (1995), with the HLN correction Harvey, Leybourne and Newbold (1997)
  • Notation
    • \(s\): the season length (12 for monthly data); \(\Phi(L^s)\), \(\Theta(L^s)\): the seasonal AR and MA polynomials; \(D\): the number of seasonal differences \((1 - L^s)\)
    • \(e_{1t}\), \(e_{2t}\): the errors of the two forecasts; \(L(\cdot)\): the loss, e.g. the square; \(d_t\): the loss differential; \(\bar d\): its mean; \(\hat V\): its estimated long-run variance; \(n\): the number of forecasts
    • under equal accuracy DM is approximately \(N(0, 1)\): \(|\mathrm{DM}| > 1.96\) rejects at 5%

Chapter 5: Conditional volatility: ARCH and GARCH (1/2)

Slide 19 in the PDF

The GARCH(1,1) model (Engle, 1982; Bollerslev, 1986) writes the conditional variance as a function of yesterday's shock and yesterday's variance:

\[\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2,\]
(15.7)

with \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha \ge 0\), \(\beta \ge 0\). The parameter \(\alpha\) measures the reaction to shocks and \(\beta\) the persistence. If \(\alpha + \beta < 1\), the variance reverts to the long-run level \(\bar\sigma^2 = \omega/(1 - \alpha - \beta)\), and a volatility shock halves in \(h_{1/2} = \ln 0.5/\ln(\alpha + \beta)\) periods. The closer \(\alpha + \beta\) is to 1, the more persistent the volatility.

The ARCH-LM test checks whether the variance changes: \(\varepsilon_t^2\) is regressed on \(q\) of its lags, and \(nR^2\) is \(\chi^2(q)\) under constant variance. The model is then used for the risk of loss. With the conditional mean \(\mu\), the forecast volatility \(\sigma_{t+1}\) and the 1% quantile \(q_{0.01}(z)\) of the standardised innovations, VaR 1% is

\[\mathrm{VaR}_{1\%} = -\bigl(\mu + \sigma_{t+1}\,q_{0.01}(z)\bigr),\]

a positive loss, exceeded on average on one day in a hundred.

In brief

  • Key formulas
    • GARCH(1,1) \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2\) Engle (1982), Bollerslev (1986); \(\bar\sigma^2 = \omega/(1 - \alpha - \beta)\), \(h_{1/2} = \ln 0.5/\ln(\alpha + \beta)\)
    • ARCH-LM \(= nR^2 \sim \chi^2(q)\); VaR 1% \(= -(\mu + \sigma_{t+1}q_{0.01}(z))\)
  • Notation
    • \(\sigma_t^2\): the conditional variance; \(\varepsilon_{t-1}\): yesterday's shock; \(\omega > 0\): the constant; \(\alpha \ge 0\): the reaction to shocks; \(\beta \ge 0\): the persistence
    • \(\bar\sigma^2\): the long-run variance, defined only if \(\alpha + \beta < 1\); \(h_{1/2}\): the half-life of a volatility shock
    • ARCH-LM: \(R^2\) of the regression of \(\varepsilon_t^2\) on \(q\) of its lags, \(n\) observations; \(q_{0.01}(z)\): the 1% quantile of the standardised innovations \(z_t\); \(\mu\): the conditional mean

Chapter 6: VAR models and Granger causality (1/2)

Slide 21 in the PDF

A VAR\((p)\) (Sims, 1980) models \(K\) variables together, each regressed on \(p\) lags of all of them:

\[\bY_t = \bc + \bA_1\bY_{t-1} + \dots + \bA_p\bY_{t-p} + \bepsilon_t.\]
(15.8)

The matrices \(\bA_i\) are \(K \times K\). Written as a VAR(1) of dimension \(Kp\), through the companion matrix, the model is stable if all eigenvalues of that matrix lie inside the unit circle; then the effect of any shock dies out over time.

Granger causality (Granger, 1969) asks whether the lags of a variable \(x\) improve the forecast of \(y\), given the history of \(y\). The test compares the residual sum of squares without the lags of \(x\), \(RSS_R\), with the one with the lags of \(x\), \(RSS_U\):

\[F = \frac{(RSS_R - RSS_U)/p}{RSS_U/(T - Kp - 1)}.\]

Impulse response functions (IRF) and the forecast error variance decomposition (FEVD) need uncorrelated shocks, usually obtained by a Cholesky decomposition, which depends on the ordering of the variables.

In brief

  • Key formulas
    • VAR\((p)\): \(\bY_t = \bc + \bA_1\bY_{t-1} + \dots + \bA_p\bY_{t-p} + \bepsilon_t\) Sims (1980); stable if all eigenvalues of the companion matrix are inside the unit circle
    • Granger \(F = [(RSS_R - RSS_U)/p]\,/\,[RSS_U/(T - Kp - 1)]\) Granger (1969); IRF and FEVD with a Cholesky ordering
  • Notation
    • \(\bY_t\): the vector of the \(K\) variables; \(\bc\): the constants; \(\bA_i\): the \(K \times K\) coefficient matrices of lag \(i\); \(\bepsilon_t\): the vector of shocks
    • the companion matrix writes the VAR(\(p\)) as a VAR(1) of dimension \(Kp\)
    • \(RSS_R\), \(RSS_U\): the residual sums of squares without and with the lags of the tested variable; \(p\): the number of restrictions

Chapter 7: Cointegration and VECM (1/2)

Slide 23 in the PDF

Two \(I(1)\) series are cointegrated if a linear combination of them is stationary. The Engle–Granger method (Engle and Granger, 1987) estimates the relation by OLS in levels, then applies an ADF test to the residuals, with MacKinnon critical values, more negative than the Dickey–Fuller ones, because the residuals come from an estimated regression. In the error correction model, the coefficient \(\gamma < 0\) on last period's disequilibrium is the speed of adjustment, and the half-life of a deviation is \(\ln 0.5/\ln(1 + \gamma)\).

The vector error correction model (VECM) writes the differences as functions of last period's disequilibria and of the short-run dynamics:

\[\Delta\mathbf y_t = \alpha\beta^\top\mathbf y_{t-1} + \sum_i\Gamma_i\Delta\mathbf y_{t-i} + \mathbf u_t.\]
(15.9)

The columns of \(\beta\) are the equilibrium relations, and \(\alpha\) says how fast each variable reacts to disequilibrium. The trace test of Johansen (1991), with the statistic \(-T\sum_{i>r}\ln(1 - \hat\lambda_i)\), tests the ranks \(r = 0, 1, \dots\) in turn; the first rank that is not rejected is the cointegration rank.

In brief

  • Key formulas
    • Engle–Granger: OLS in levels, then ADF on residuals with MacKinnon critical values Engle and Granger (1987); ECM half-life \(\ln 0.5/\ln(1 + \gamma)\)
    • VECM \(\Delta\mathbf y_t = \alpha\beta^\top\mathbf y_{t-1} + \sum_i\Gamma_i\Delta\mathbf y_{t-i} + \mathbf u_t\); trace \(-T\sum_{i>r}\ln(1 - \hat\lambda_i)\) Johansen (1991)
  • Notation
    • ECM: \(\gamma < 0\) is the speed of adjustment towards equilibrium
    • \(\beta\): the cointegrating vectors (the equilibrium relations); \(\alpha\): the adjustment coefficients; \(\Gamma_i\): the short-run dynamics; \(\mathbf u_t\): the shocks
    • \(\hat\lambda_i\): the estimated eigenvalues, in decreasing order; \(r\): the tested cointegration rank; a trace above its critical value rejects rank \(r\)

Chapter 8: Long memory and ARFIMA (1/2)

Slide 25 in the PDF

Between \(d = 0\), a stationary short-memory process, and \(d = 1\), a unit root, there are fractional values of \(d\) (Granger and Joyeux, 1980; Hosking, 1981). The operator \((1 - L)^d\) expands into an infinite series of lags, with weights given by the recursion

\[\pi_k = \pi_{k-1}\,\frac{k - 1 - d}{k}, \qquad \pi_0 = 1.\]
(15.10)

For \(0 < d < 1/2\) the process is stationary, but the autocorrelations decay hyperbolically, \(\rho(k) \sim Ck^{2d-1}\), not exponentially as for an ARMA: a shock has an effect over hundreds of periods.

The Hurst exponent is \(H = d + 1/2\), above 0.5 for long memory; in the course it is estimated with the rescaled range (R/S) statistic. The process is stationary for \(d < 1/2\) and mean-reverting for \(d < 1\), even though its variance is then no longer finite. The GPH and local Whittle estimators use only the lowest \(m\) frequencies of the periodogram, where long memory leaves its mark; the choice of \(m\) changes the estimate.

In brief

  • Key formulas
    • \((1 - L)^d\) with \(\pi_k = \pi_{k-1}(k - 1 - d)/k\) Granger and Joyeux (1980), Hosking (1981); \(\rho(k) \sim Ck^{2d-1}\); \(H = d + 1/2\)
    • stationary for \(d < 1/2\), mean-reverting for \(d < 1\); GPH and local Whittle use the lowest \(m\) frequencies
  • Notation
    • \(d\): the order of fractional integration; \(\pi_k\): the weight of lag \(k\) in the expansion of \((1 - L)^d\)
    • \(C > 0\): a constant; \(\rho(k) \sim Ck^{2d-1}\): the ACF decays hyperbolically, not exponentially
    • \(H\): the Hurst exponent, above 0.5 for long memory, estimated here by the rescaled-range (R/S) statistic; \(m\): the number of low frequencies used by GPH and local Whittle

Chapter 9: Machine learning for time series (1/2)

Slide 27 in the PDF

Machine-learning methods usually forecast directly: for each horizon \(h\) a separate function is estimated of the last \(p\) values and of regressors known in advance, such as the calendar,

\[\hat y_{T+h} = \hat f_h(y_T, \dots, y_{T-p+1}, \mathbf z_{T+h}).\]

Penalised regressions control complexity: ridge adds the term \(\lambda\sum\beta_j^2\) to the sum of squared errors and shrinks all coefficients, while lasso adds \(\lambda\sum|\beta_j|\) and sets some of them to zero, so it selects predictors.

The bias–variance decomposition explains why a more flexible model is not automatically better: the mean squared forecast error for a new observation is

\[E(y_0 - \hat f)^2 = \text{bias}^2 + \mathrm{Var}(\hat f) + \sigma^2.\]

Flexibility reduces the bias and raises the variance, and \(\sigma^2\) cannot be removed by any model. Validation must respect the order of time: walk-forward validation estimates on the past and evaluates on the period right after, then moves forward.

In brief

  • Key formulas
    • direct forecast \(\hat y_{T+h} = \hat f_h(y_T, \dots, y_{T-p+1}, \mathbf z_{T+h})\); ridge \(+\lambda\sum\beta_j^2\), lasso \(+\lambda\sum|\beta_j|\)
    • bias–variance \(E(y_0 - \hat f)^2 = \text{bias}^2 + \mathrm{Var}(\hat f) + \sigma^2\); walk-forward validation only
  • Notation
    • \(\hat f_h\): the estimated function for horizon \(h\); \(p\): the number of lags used; \(\mathbf z_{T+h}\): known regressors, e.g. the calendar
    • \(\lambda \ge 0\): the strength of the penalty; \(\beta_j\): the coefficients; ridge shrinks them, lasso sets some exactly to 0
    • \(y_0\): a new observation; \(\sigma^2\): the noise variance, which no model can remove

Chapter 10: State space models, Kalman filter and Markov switching (1/2)

Slide 29 in the PDF

A state space model has two equations: a measurement equation, which links the observation to a hidden state, and a transition equation, which describes how the state evolves:

\[y_t = Z\alpha_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \alpha_{t+1} = T\alpha_t + R\eta_t.\]
(15.11)

The Kalman filter (Kalman, 1960) updates the state estimate when a new observation arrives: \(a_{t|t} = a_t + K_tv_t\), where \(v_t = y_t - Za_t\) is the prediction error and the gain \(K_t = P_tZ'F_t^{-1}\) is the weight of this surprise. When the predicted state is uncertain, \(P_t\) large, the gain is large and the filter relies more on the observation.

In the Markov switching model (Hamilton, 1989), the parameters depend on an unobserved regime that moves from one state to another with probabilities \(p_{ij}\). The expected duration of regime \(i\) is \(1/(1 - p_{ii})\), and with two regimes the long-run share of regime 1 is

\[\pi_1 = \frac{1 - p_{22}}{2 - p_{11} - p_{22}}.\]

In brief

  • Key formulas
    • \(y_t = Z\alpha_t + \varepsilon_t\), \(\alpha_{t+1} = T\alpha_t + R\eta_t\); \(K_t = P_tZ'F_t^{-1}\), \(a_{t|t} = a_t + K_tv_t\) Kalman (1960)
    • Markov switching: durations \(1/(1 - p_{ii})\), ergodic \(\pi_1 = (1 - p_{22})/(2 - p_{11} - p_{22})\) Hamilton (1989)
  • Notation
    • \(\alpha_t\): the hidden state (e.g. the level); \(Z\), \(T\), \(R\): known system matrices; \(\varepsilon_t\), \(\eta_t\): the measurement and state noise
    • \(a_t\), \(P_t\): the predicted state and its variance; \(v_t = y_t - Za_t\): the prediction error, with variance \(F_t\); \(K_t\): the Kalman gain, the weight of the surprise
    • \(p_{ii}\): the probability of staying in regime \(i\); \(\pi_1\): the long-run share of regime 1

Chapter 12: Spectral analysis (self-study)

Slide 32 in the PDF

Spectral analysis describes a stationary series by the frequencies that make it up. The Fourier transform decomposes the series into sinusoids, the periodogram \(I(\nu_j)\) measures how much of the variance falls on each Fourier frequency \(\nu_j\), and the spectral density is the theoretical version of the periodogram. The raw periodogram fluctuates a lot, so it is smoothed, for example by Welch's method. Filters, such as Hodrick–Prescott, keep some frequencies and remove the others, which turns the business cycle into a band of frequencies.

The spectrum holds the same information as the autocovariance function of Chapter 1, ordered by frequency instead of by lag. The long memory of Chapter 8 appears as a pole of the spectral density at frequency zero. Coherence measures the link between two series at each frequency, and wavelets allow the study of frequencies that change over time.

Chapter 13: Speculative bubbles: LPPL models (self-study)

Slide 33 in the PDF

Chapter 13 describes speculative bubbles as a super-exponential growth of prices, faster than any exponential growth. The LPPLS (log-periodic power law singularity) model combines a power law leading to a critical time with ever faster log-periodic oscillations. The link with the core is the Dickey–Fuller test of Chapter 3, used the other way round: explosive-root tests look for an autoregressive root above 1, in the right tail. The second link is the volatility before crashes, studied with the tools of Chapter 5.

Chapter 14: Multivariate GARCH models (self-study)

Slide 34 in the PDF

Chapter 14 moves from the volatility of one series to the covariance matrix of several series, which changes over time. The main problem is the number of parameters, which grows very fast with the number of series. The VEC and BEKK models model the covariances directly, with many restrictions, while the CCC (constant conditional correlation) and DCC (dynamic conditional correlation) models separate the individual volatilities from the correlations. The DCC is estimated in two steps: first a GARCH for each series, then the correlation dynamics on the standardised residuals.

The results are dynamic correlations, the variance of a portfolio and the portfolio's VaR 1%. The link with the core is direct: each series has its own GARCH from Chapter 5, and the multivariate view, with coefficient matrices and a number of parameters that grows fast, is that of the VAR model of Chapter 6.

Conventions used throughout the course

Slide 37 in the PDF

Transformations are always written in the same form. The logarithm is multiplied by 100, so that differences are approximately percentages: \(100\Delta\ln y_t\) is the growth on the previous period, and \(100\Delta_{12}\ln y_t\) for monthly data and \(100\Delta_4\ln y_t\) for quarterly data are the annual rates. In EViews output, "DLOG(IPC,1,12)" means the ordinary difference of the seasonal difference of the logarithm, \(\Delta\Delta_{12}\ln\) IPC.

Every test is reported the same way: the null hypothesis, the statistic, its distribution under \(H_0\), the critical value or the p-value and the decision at 5%, followed by one sentence on what the decision means for the series. The information criteria AIC, AICc and BIC are compared only on the same sample and for the same \(d\) and \(D\), and forecasts are judged out of sample, against the naive and seasonal naive methods. For risk, the level is the tail probability: VaR 1% is a positive loss, \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha = -q_\alpha\), where \(q_\alpha\) is the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the return.

Box–Jenkins from start to finish: Romanian inflation

The section applies the Box–Jenkins method, in six steps, to the Romanian HICP. Each step uses tools from earlier chapters, and each decision rests on a reported result.

The method in six steps

Slide 38 in the PDF

The method of Box, Jenkins and Reinsel (2008) is a loop, not a linear recipe. The first step is the plot and the transformation: the logarithm to stabilise the variance, the ordinary difference \(\Delta\) for the trend and the seasonal difference \(\Delta_{12}\) for seasonality. The second step applies the ADF and KPSS tests to each transformation. The third identifies the orders from the ACF and PACF of the stationary series, and the fourth estimates several candidate SARIMA models by maximum likelihood and compares them with AICc and BIC.

The fifth step checks the residuals: autocorrelation with Ljung–Box, Normality with Jarque–Bera, and outliers. If the check fails, the analysis goes back to step 3. The sixth step forecasts and evaluates: a test sample kept aside, the comparison with the naive benchmarks and only then the forecast intervals. The data are the Romanian HICP, from Eurostat, from January 2005 to August 2026, \(T = 260\) months.

  1. Plot and transform: logarithm, \(\Delta\), \(\Delta_{12}\)

  3. Identify: ACF and PACF of the stationary series
  4. Estimate: candidate SARIMA models by maximum likelihood; AICc and BIC
  5. Check: residuals (Ljung–Box, Jarque–Bera, outliers); back to step 3 if they fail
  6. Forecast and evaluate: a test sample, benchmarks, then intervals

                                                        Problem 1: a trend with AR(1) errors

                                                        Slide 55 in the PDF

                                                        The series is a linear trend, \(Y_t = 10 + 0.5t + u_t\), with AR(1) errors on top, \(u_t = 0.6u_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\), where \(\varepsilon_t\) is white noise with variance 1.                                                         In brief

                                                        • Context

                                                        Problem 1: solution

                                                        Slide 56 in the PDF

                                                        The mean \(E(Y_t) = 10 + 0.5t\) depends on time, so \(Y_t\) is not stationary. The variance is that of the AR(1) process, \(\mathrm{Var}(u_t) = 1/(1 - 0.6^2) = 1.5625\), constant over time. The series is trend-stationary: after removing the trend, a stationary process remains. Differencing gives \(\Delta Y_t = 0.5 + \Delta u_t\), and

                                                        \[(1 - 0.6L)\,\Delta u_t = (1 - L)\,\varepsilon_t,\]

                                                        that is, an ARMA(1,1) with \(\theta = -1\). Its autocovariances are \(\gamma(0) = 1.25\) and \(\gamma(1) = -0.25\), so \(\rho(1) = -0.20\). The root of the MA polynomial is 1, and the differenced process is not invertible. A negative first-order autocorrelation and an estimated MA coefficient close to \(-1\) are the signs of overdifferencing.

                                                        The forecast uses the correct model, the trend plus AR(1). The deviation from the trend at time \(T\) is \(u_T = 62 - 10 - 50 = 2.0\), and it dies out geometrically, by the factor 0.6 per period:

                                                        \[\begin{aligned} \hat Y_{T+1} &= 10 + 50.5 + 0.6 \cdot 2 = 61.70, \\ \hat Y_{T+10} &= 10 + 55 + 0.6^{10} \cdot 2 = 65.01. \end{aligned}\]

                                                        The ten-step error variance is \((1 - 0.6^{20})/(1 - 0.36) = 1.562\), practically the variance of \(u_t\): after ten periods only 0.012 of the deviation remains, and the uncertainty stays bounded. The treatment follows the type of trend: a trend-stationary series is detrended, a series with a unit root is differenced (Chapter 3).

                                                        Slide 68 in the PDF

                                                        RMSE is the square root of the mean squared error, MAE the mean absolute error, and MASE divides the test-sample MAE by the in-sample MAE of the seasonal naive method, 0.6. A MASE below 1 shows that the method has, on average, smaller errors than the naive benchmark.

                                                        The Diebold–Mariano test (Diebold and Mariano, 1995) works with the loss differentials, \(d_t = e_{A,t}^2 - e_{B,t}^2\), with \(A\) the SARIMA and \(B\) the seasonal naive method: \(-0.16\), \(-0.24\), \(-0.32\), \(0.20\), \(-0.65\) and \(-0.08\). Their mean is \(\bar d = -0.208\) and their standard deviation \(s_d = 0.281\), so

                                                        \[\mathrm{DM} = \frac{\bar d}{s_d/\sqrt{n}} = \frac{-0.208}{0.281/\sqrt 6} = -1.82.\]

                                                        Since \(|-1.82| < 2.571\), equal accuracy is not rejected: SARIMA beats the seasonal naive method on average, but six months cannot separate this gain from noise.

Walk-forward validation, which respects the order of time, gives \(R^2 = -0.39\) for the same model (Chapter 9). Only out-of-sample evaluation, in time order, is evidence of forecasting ability.

                                                        Problem 8: state space, regimes and long memory

                                                        Slide 69 in the PDF

                                                        The problem has three independent parts. The first concerns the local level model, with observation-noise variance \(\sigma^2_\varepsilon = 4\) and level-shock variance \(\sigma^2_\eta = 1\); the predicted level is \(a_t = 10\), with variance \(P_t = 2\), and the observation is \(y_t = 13\). The tasks are one Kalman update and prediction step, the steady-state gain and the weight of the equivalent simple exponential smoothing (SES).

                                                        The second part concerns Romanian GDP growth with two regimes, with probabilities of staying in the regime \(p_{11} = 0.9108\) for the volatile regime and \(p_{22} = 0.9341\) for the stable one (Chapter 10); the tasks are the expected durations of the regimes and the long-run share of the volatile regime. The third part concerns monthly Romanian inflation, modelled as ARFIMA(0,\(d\),0) with \(\hat d = 0.31\) and standard error 0.05 (Chapter 8); the tasks are the Hurst exponent \(H\) and \(\rho(1)\), and whether the series is stationary and mean-reverting.

                                                        In brief

                                                        • Context
                                                          • Local level model: \(\sigma^2_\varepsilon = 4\), \(\sigma^2_\eta = 1\); prediction \(a_t = 10\), \(P_t = 2\); observation \(y_t = 13\)
                                                          • Romanian GDP growth, two regimes: \(p_{11} = 0.9108\) (volatile), \(p_{22} = 0.9341\) (stable) (Chapter 10)
                                                          • Romanian monthly inflation: ARFIMA(0,\(d\),0) with \(\hat d = 0.31\) (SE 0.05) (Chapter 8)
                                                        • Tasks
                                                          1. Run one Kalman update and prediction; compute the steady-state gain and the equivalent SES weight.
                                                          2. Compute the expected durations of the two regimes and the long-run share of the volatile regime.
                                                          3. Compute \(H\) and \(\rho(1)\) for the ARFIMA model and say whether inflation is stationary and mean-reverting.

                                                        Problem 8: solution

                                                        Slide 70 in the PDF

                                                        The Kalman filter (Kalman, 1960) compares the observation with the prediction. The variance of the prediction error is \(F_t = P_t + \sigma^2_\varepsilon = 2 + 4 = 6\), the gain is \(K_t = P_t/F_t = 0.333\), and the surprise is \(13 - 10 = 3\). The update gives \(a_{t|t} = 10 + 0.333 \times 3 = 11.00\) and \(P_{t|t} = 2(1 - 0.333) = 1.333\); in the local level model the forecast level stays \(a_{t+1} = 11.00\), while the variance grows by \(\sigma^2_\eta\), to \(P_{t+1} = 2.333\). With the signal-to-noise ratio \(q = \sigma^2_\eta/\sigma^2_\varepsilon = 0.25\), the steady-state variance and gain are

                                                        \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\bar P}{\sigma^2_\varepsilon} &= \frac{q + \sqrt{q^2 + 4q}}{2} = 0.6404, \\ \bar K &= \frac{0.6404}{1.6404} = 0.390, \end{aligned}\]

                                                        and \(\bar K\) is exactly the weight \(\alpha\) of simple exponential smoothing.

                                                        In a Markov-switching model (Hamilton, 1989), the duration of a regime is geometric, with mean \(1/(1 - p_{ii})\): \(E(D_1) = 1/(1 - 0.9108) = 11.2\) quarters for the volatile regime and \(E(D_2) = 15.2\) quarters for the stable one. The long-run share of the volatile regime is \(\pi_1 = (1 - p_{22})/(2 - p_{11} - p_{22}) = 0.425\): Romanian growth is in the volatile regime about 42% of the time, periods that cover the transition, 2009 and 2020.

                                                        For ARFIMA (Granger and Joyeux, 1980; Hosking, 1981), \(H = d + 0.5 = 0.81\) and \(\rho(1) = d/(1 - d) = 0.45\). Since \(0 < d < 0.5\), inflation is stationary, with long memory, and mean-reverting. Its shocks die out slowly, hyperbolically, and forecasts return to the mean more slowly than those of an AR model.

                                                        In brief

                                                        1. \(F_t = 2 + 4 = 6\), \(K_t = 2/6 = 0.333\); \(a_{t|t} = 10 + 0.333 \times 3 = 11.00\), \(P_{t|t} = 2(1 - 0.333) = 1.333\); \(a_{t+1} = 11.00\), \(P_{t+1} = 2.333\)
                                                        2. \(q = \sigma^2_\eta/\sigma^2_\varepsilon = 0.25\): \(\bar P/\sigma^2_\varepsilon = (q + \sqrt{q^2 + 4q})/2 = 0.6404\), \(\bar K = 0.6404/(1 + 0.6404) = 0.390 = \alpha_{SES}\)
                                                        3. \(E(D_1) = 1/(1 - 0.9108) = 11.2\) quarters, \(E(D_2) = 15.2\) quarters; \(\pi_1 = (1 - 0.9341)/(2 - 0.9108 - 0.9341) = 0.425\)
                                                        4. \(H = d + 0.5 = 0.81\); \(\rho(1) = d/(1 - d) = 0.45\); \(0 < d < 0.5\): stationary with long memory, mean-reverting
                                                        • Interpretation
                                                          • The filter moves the level one third of the way to the surprise; in the long run the weight is that of exponential smoothing
                                                          • In the long run Romanian growth is in the volatile regime about 42% of the time: transition, 2009 and 2020
                                                          • Inflation shocks fade slowly, hyperbolically: forecasts return to the mean more slowly than those of an AR model

                                                        Key takeaways

                                                        Slide 77 in the PDF

                                                        The analysis of a series starts with the data: the plot, the components, the right transformation and the correlogram. In the project what counts is a clear question and an honest answer, including when the answer is that the data cannot decide.

                                                        Self-assessment

                                                        Slide 78 in the PDF

                                                        The first question combines the two unit-root tests. If ADF does not reject the unit root and KPSS rejects stationarity, both tests point to a unit root, so the series is differenced. This is exactly the situation of the HICP level in step 2.

                                                        The second question applies the Ljung–Box test to the residuals of an ARMA(1,1), with \(Q(10) = 16\). The degrees of freedom are \(10 - 2 = 8\), and the 5% critical value of the \(\chi^2(8)\) distribution is 15.51. The statistic exceeds it, so autocorrelation remains in the residuals and the model is not adequate.

                                                        The third question concerns two cointegrated \(I(1)\) series. A VAR in differences is not suitable, because it omits the error correction term of (15.9) and loses the information about the long-run relation; the right model is the VECM.
