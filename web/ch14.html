Why this chapter

Slide 2 in the PDF

The question of the chapter is how the risks of several assets move together. In Chapter 5 the conditional variance of a single return series was enough to describe its risk. For a portfolio of two or more assets this is no longer enough: two portfolios with the same individual volatilities can have very different risk, depending on whether their components tend to fall on the same days or on different days. The quantity that captures this is the covariance, or its standardised version, the correlation, and the chapter treats both as variables that change from day to day.

The route goes from the general to the practical. It starts from the conditional covariance matrix \(\mathbf{H}_t\) and the two conditions it must satisfy, continues with the VEC and BEKK models, which describe all elements of \(\mathbf{H}_t\) at once, and arrives at the CCC and DCC models, which estimate volatilities and correlations separately. The last sections use these models for three applications: correlations in crises, the VaR of a portfolio and hedge ratios.

The chapter builds directly on three earlier ones. The univariate GARCH model of Chapter 5 is the building block of every multivariate model; the vector autoregression (VAR) of Chapter 6 supplies the multivariate notation and the idea of spillovers between series; cointegration, from Chapter 7, is the long-run counterpart of the short-run links studied here.

In brief

  • Question: how do the risks of several assets move together? Chapter 5 modelled the volatility of one series at a time
    • the risk of a portfolio, a hedge or the spread of a crisis depends on covariances and correlations, and these change in time
  • Route of the chapter
    • the conditional covariance matrix \(\mathbf{H}_t\) and its conditions
    • the VEC and BEKK models; the number of parameters
    • CCC and DCC: volatilities and correlations estimated in two steps
    • applications: correlations in crises, portfolio VaR 1%, hedge ratios
  • Self-study chapter
    • read the slides in order, redo the worked examples, run the notebook, then answer the self-assessment and the quiz
    • prerequisites: Chapter 5 (GARCH), Chapter 6 (VAR models), Chapter 7 (cointegration)

Learning outcomes

Slide 3 in the PDF

The first outcomes are conceptual. The reader should be able to explain why the variance of a portfolio, a hedge ratio and the question of contagion all depend on covariances that change over time, and to write the main multivariate GARCH models in matrix form. The other outcomes are computational and empirical. Chapter 5 modelled the volatility of one return series at a time with generalised autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH) models. Most questions of risk management, however, concern several assets at once. The risk of a portfolio depends on how its components move together, a hedge works only as well as the link between the asset and the hedging instrument, and the spread of a crisis from one market to another is a statement about co-movement. All three questions are answered by covariances and correlations, and these change over time.

This chapter extends GARCH to \(N\) series. The object of interest is the conditional covariance matrix \(\mathbf{H}_t\), which holds the variances of all assets on its diagonal and their covariances off the diagonal. The chapter first sets out the conditions that any model for \(\mathbf{H}_t\) must respect, then presents the general VEC and BEKK models and the reason they become unusable beyond a few assets. The constant conditional correlation (CCC) and dynamic conditional correlation (DCC) models solve this by separating volatilities from correlations and estimating them in two steps.

The applications use daily data on the S&P 500, the German DAX index, the Romanian BET index, the EUR/RON exchange rate and Bitcoin. The correlation between the S&P 500 and the DAX is not constant: the likelihood ratio test against constant correlation gives 131.8, far above the 5% critical value of 5.99. Correlations rise in crises, most of all for the assets that are weakly linked to the large markets in calm times. The same conditional covariances give a portfolio Value at Risk (VaR) at 1% that adapts to the state of the market and dynamic hedge ratios that remove more risk out of sample than a static ratio.

Why multivariate volatility

The section shows why a model for a single volatility is not enough. Portfolio risk, hedging and contagion all depend on correlations, and the correlations observed in real markets move a great deal over time. The section ends with a practical trap of daily data: markets that close at different hours.

Three questions that need covariances

Slide 5 in the PDF

The first question is the risk of a portfolio. If the vector \(\mathbf{w}\) holds the weights of the assets and \(\mathbf{H}\) is the covariance matrix of their returns, the variance of the portfolio return is a quadratic form, as in Markowitz (1952):

\[\begin{gathered} \sigma_p^2 = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{H}\mathbf{w}, \\ \text{for two assets: } \sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2. \end{gathered}\]
(14.1)

The first two terms come from the individual volatilities; the third is the only place where the joint behaviour enters. With positive weights, a lower correlation \(\rho\) means a lower portfolio variance, and this is the whole mechanism of diversification.

The second question is hedging: how many units of one asset to sell in order to offset the risk of another. Section 7 shows that the answer is the ratio of a covariance to a variance, so a hedge ratio moves whenever the covariance moves. The third question is contagion: do markets move more closely together in a crisis? Longin and Solnik (2001) found that correlations between international equity markets are higher in bear markets. If correlations rise exactly when volatility rises, diversification weakens at the moment it is needed most.

A univariate GARCH model gives the volatilities \(\sigma_{1,t}\) and \(\sigma_{2,t}\) day by day. Formula (14.1) also needs the correlation, and the aim of this chapter is to add a correlation \(\rho_t\) that changes over time in the same way.

In brief

  • Portfolio risk
    • the variance of a portfolio with weights \(\mathbf{w}\) is \(\sigma_p^2 = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{H}\mathbf{w}\) Markowitz (1952)
    • \(\mathbf{w}\): the vector of portfolio weights; \(\mathbf{H}\): the covariance matrix of the returns; \(\sigma_p^2\): the portfolio variance
    • two assets: \(\sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2\), with volatilities \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\) and correlation \(\rho\)
    • the correlation \(\rho\) decides how much diversification helps
  • Hedging
    • how many units of an asset to sell to offset the risk of another
    • the answer is a ratio of a covariance to a variance (Section 7)
  • Contagion
    • do markets move more closely together in a crisis?
    • if correlations rise exactly when volatility rises, diversification fails when it is needed most Longin and Solnik (2001)
  • A univariate GARCH (Chapter 5) gives \(\sigma_{1,t}\) and \(\sigma_{2,t}\); this chapter adds the time-varying \(\rho_t\)

Worked example: correlation and portfolio risk

Slide 6 in the PDF

Take two assets with an annual volatility of 20% each and a portfolio split 50/50. With volatilities in percent, \(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = 400\) and \(w_1^2 = w_2^2 = 0.25\), so the first two terms of (14.1) add up to 200 whatever the correlation. With \(\rho = 0.2\) the covariance term is \(2 \cdot 0.25 \cdot 0.2 \cdot 400 = 40\), the variance is 240 and the volatility \(\sqrt{240} = 15.5\%\). With \(\rho = 0.8\) the covariance term is 160, the variance 360 and the volatility 19.0%.

Nothing changed except the correlation: the same assets, the same weights, the same individual volatilities. Yet the risk of the portfolio rises by more than a fifth. The practical consequence is that a risk model which keeps \(\rho\) at the value it had in a calm period understates the risk when correlations rise, and the data below show that they do rise in crises.

In brief

  • Two assets with annual volatility 20% each, weights 50% and 50%
  • \(\rho = 0.2\): \(\sigma_p^2 = 0.25 \cdot 400 + 0.25 \cdot 400 + 2 \cdot 0.25 \cdot 0.2 \cdot 400 = 240\), so \(\sigma_p = 15.5\%\)
  • \(\rho = 0.8\): \(\sigma_p^2 = 200 + 2 \cdot 0.25 \cdot 0.8 \cdot 400 = 360\), so \(\sigma_p = 19.0\%\)
  • The same assets and weights: the risk of the portfolio rises by more than a fifth only because the correlation rose
    • a risk model that keeps \(\rho\) fixed at its calm-period value underestimates the risk in a crisis

        Interpreting the diversification curve

        Slide 8 in the PDF

        Three points of the curve are worth remembering. At \(\rho = 1\) the two assets move as one, there is no diversification and the portfolio has the 20% volatility of each asset. At \(\rho = 0\) the covariance term disappears and the volatility is \(20/\sqrt{2} \approx 14.1\%\). At \(\rho = -1\) the movements of the two assets cancel exactly and the risk disappears, a case that does not occur with real equity indices.

        Because the curve is concave, the same increase of the correlation adds more volatility at low correlations than at high ones. For portfolios that combine weakly correlated assets, the very portfolios built for diversification, a rise in correlation is therefore most costly. This leads to the empirical question of the chapter: does \(\rho\) stay put in real markets, or does it move with the state of the market?

        In brief

        • \(\rho = 1\): no diversification, \(\sigma_p = 20\%\); \(\rho = 0\): \(\sigma_p = 20/\sqrt{2} \approx 14.1\%\); \(\rho = -1\): the risk disappears

            Asynchronous trading and the bias of daily correlations

            Slide 11 in the PDF

            Daily returns of different markets do not cover the same hours. Bucharest closes at 16:45 Central European Time (CET), 17:45 local time, Frankfurt at 17:30 CET and New York at 22:00 CET, while Bitcoin trades all day, every day. News that arrives after the European close enters European prices only on the next trading day. This is asynchronous trading (non-synchronous trading), and it splits the reaction to one event across two daily returns.

            The tariff announcement of 9 April 2025 shows the effect. The pause of the United States (US) tariffs was announced in the afternoon in New York, after the European close: on 9 April the S&P 500 rose by 9.1% and the DAX fell by 3.1%; on 10 April the S&P 500 fell by 3.5% and the DAX rose by 4.4%. The same news produced returns of opposite sign on the same calendar day, and this pulls the daily correlation towards zero.

            Weekly returns are much less affected, because a one-day delay matters little over five days. Since 2015 the correlation of daily against weekly returns is 0.32 against 0.46 for the S&P 500 and the BET, 0.56 against 0.68 for the S&P 500 and the DAX, and 0.42 against 0.47 for the BET and the DAX. The gap is small for the last pair, whose markets close almost together. The remedies are weekly returns or returns measured between synchronous times; the daily models of this chapter keep the bias in mind when reading transatlantic correlations.

            The multivariate GARCH framework

            The section defines the multivariate GARCH model in general form: the vector of returns, the conditional covariance matrix \(\mathbf{H}_t\), the conditions of symmetry and positive definiteness, and estimation by maximum likelihood.

            The model for \(N\) return series

            Slide 13 in the PDF

            The model for \(N\) returns has the same structure as the univariate GARCH model, written with vectors and matrices:

            \[\begin{gathered} \mathbf{r}_t = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t, \qquad \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t = \mathbf{H}_t^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\eta}_t, \\ \mathbf{H}_t = \Var(\mathbf{r}_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}). \end{gathered}\]
            (14.2)

            Here \(\mathbf{r}_t\) is the vector of the \(N\) returns on day \(t\), \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) the vector of means (or the forecast of a VAR model, as in Chapter 6), and \(\boldsymbol{\eta}_t\) a vector of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) innovations with mean zero and covariance matrix \(\mathbf{I}_N\). The matrix \(\mathbf{H}_t^{1/2}\) is a square root of \(\mathbf{H}_t\), for instance the Cholesky factor \(\mathbf{L}_t\) with \(\mathbf{L}_t\mathbf{L}_t^\top = \mathbf{H}_t\); any such root gives the shocks \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t\) the conditional covariance matrix \(\mathbf{H}_t\).

            The conditional covariance matrix (conditional covariance matrix) \(\mathbf{H}_t\) is computed from the information \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\) available up to day \(t-1\). Its diagonal holds the conditional variances \(h_{ii,t} = \sigma_{i,t}^2\), exactly the quantities of Chapter 5. Off the diagonal are the conditional covariances \(h_{ij,t}\), and the conditional correlation follows from them:

            \[\rho_{ij,t} = \frac{h_{ij,t}}{\sqrt{h_{ii,t}\,h_{jj,t}}}.\]
            (14.3)

            A multivariate GARCH (MGARCH) model is a rule that computes \(\mathbf{H}_t\) from the past shocks \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}\) and from \(\mathbf{H}_{t-1}\). The models of the following sections differ only in this rule.

            In brief

            • \(\mathbf{r}_t = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t\),   \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t = \mathbf{H}_t^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\eta}_t\),   \(\boldsymbol{\eta}_t\) i.i.d. with mean \(\mathbf{0}\) and covariance \(\mathbf{I}_N\)
              • \(\mathbf{r}_t\): the vector of \(N\) returns on day \(t\); \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\): the means (or a VAR model for the mean, Chapter 6)
              • \(\mathbf{H}_t^{1/2}\): a square root of \(\mathbf{H}_t\), e.g. the Cholesky factor \(\mathbf{L}_t\) with \(\mathbf{L}_t\mathbf{L}_t^\top = \mathbf{H}_t\)
            • \(\mathbf{H}_t = \Var(\mathbf{r}_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1})\): the conditional covariance matrix, given the information \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\) up to day \(t-1\)
              • diagonal: the conditional variances \(h_{ii,t} = \sigma_{i,t}^2\) (Chapter 5)
              • off-diagonal: the conditional covariances \(h_{ij,t}\); the conditional correlation is \(\rho_{ij,t} = h_{ij,t}/\sqrt{h_{ii,t}h_{jj,t}}\)
            • A multivariate GARCH (MGARCH) model is a rule that computes \(\mathbf{H}_t\) from past shocks \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}\) and past \(\mathbf{H}_{t-1}\)

            Two conditions on \(\mathbf{H}_t\)

            Slide 14 in the PDF

            The first condition is symmetry: the covariance of asset \(i\) with asset \(j\) is the covariance of \(j\) with \(i\), so \(h_{ij,t} = h_{ji,t}\) and only the \(N(N+1)/2\) elements on and below the diagonal are distinct. The operator \(\mathrm{vech}\) stacks these elements column by column in a vector; for two assets, \(\mathrm{vech}(\mathbf{H}) = (h_{11}, h_{12}, h_{22})^\top\). The number of distinct elements grows quickly: 3 for \(N = 2\), 55 for \(N = 10\) and 1 275 for \(N = 50\).

            The second condition is positive definiteness (positive definiteness): \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{H}_t\mathbf{w} > 0\) for every non-zero vector \(\mathbf{w}\). By (14.1) the left side is the variance of the portfolio with weights \(\mathbf{w}\), so the condition says that every portfolio has a positive variance. For two assets it reduces to \(h_{11,t} > 0\) and \(h_{11,t}h_{22,t} - h_{12,t}^2 > 0\), which is the same as \(|\rho_{12,t}| < 1\).

            The example on the slide shows what goes wrong without this condition. With \(h_{11} = 4\), \(h_{22} = 1\) and \(h_{12} = 2.5\) the determinant is \(4 - 6.25 < 0\), and (14.3) gives a "correlation" of 1.25, which no pair of random variables can have. A model that computes each element of \(\mathbf{H}_t\) separately can produce such matrices. A good MGARCH model guarantees both conditions for every \(t\), whatever the shocks, and does so with few parameters.

            In brief

            • Symmetry
              • \(h_{ij,t} = h_{ji,t}\), so only \(N(N+1)/2\) elements are distinct
              • the operator \(\mathrm{vech}\) stacks the lower triangle: \(\mathrm{vech}\begin{pmatrix} h_{11} & h_{12}\\ h_{12} & h_{22}\end{pmatrix} = (h_{11}, h_{12}, h_{22})^\top\)
              • \(N = 2\): 3 elements; \(N = 10\): 55; \(N = 50\): 1 275
            • Positive definiteness
              • \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{H}_t\mathbf{w} > 0\) for every \(\mathbf{w} \neq \mathbf{0}\), i.e. every portfolio has a positive variance
              • \(N = 2\): \(h_{11,t} > 0\) and \(h_{11,t}h_{22,t} - h_{12,t}^2 > 0\), i.e. \(|\rho_{12,t}| < 1\)
              • example: \(h_{11} = 4\), \(h_{22} = 1\), \(h_{12} = 2.5\) gives \(4 - 6.25 < 0\): an impossible “correlation” of 1.25
            • A good MGARCH model guarantees both conditions for every \(t\), with few parameters

            Estimation by maximum likelihood

            Slide 15 in the PDF

            If the innovations \(\boldsymbol{\eta}_t\) follow the multivariate Normal distribution, the shocks \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t\) are conditionally Normal with covariance matrix \(\mathbf{H}_t\), and the log-likelihood of the sample is

            \[\ell(\theta) = -\frac12\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(N\log 2\pi + \log|\mathbf{H}_t| + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t^\top\mathbf{H}_t^{-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t\right),\]
            (14.4)

            where \(|\mathbf{H}_t|\) is the determinant and \(\theta\) collects all parameters. For \(N = 1\) the determinant is \(\sigma_t^2\), the quadratic form is \(\varepsilon_t^2/\sigma_t^2\), and (14.4) becomes the GARCH likelihood of Chapter 5. The two terms play the same roles as there: the determinant penalises large variances, the quadratic form penalises shocks that are large relative to the predicted covariance.

            Daily returns have fat tails, so the Normal assumption is not literally true. The Normal likelihood is then used as a quasi-maximum likelihood (QML): the estimates of the parameters of \(\mathbf{H}_t\) remain consistent as long as \(\mathbf{H}_t\) is correctly specified, but the usual standard errors are wrong and need a robust (sandwich) correction.

            The practical difficulty is computational. Each evaluation of \(\ell(\theta)\) requires the inverse and the determinant of \(\mathbf{H}_t\) for every day of the sample, and a numerical optimiser evaluates \(\ell(\theta)\) many times. The cost grows quickly with \(N\) and, above all, with the number of parameters, which is the subject of the next section.

            In brief

            • If \(\boldsymbol{\eta}_t\) follows the multivariate Normal distribution, the log-likelihood is
              • \(\ell(\theta) = -\frac12\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(N\log 2\pi + \log|\mathbf{H}_t| + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t^\top\mathbf{H}_t^{-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t\right)\)
              • \(|\mathbf{H}_t|\) is the determinant; for \(N = 1\) this is the GARCH likelihood of Chapter 5
            • Returns have fat tails, so the Normal likelihood is used as quasi-maximum likelihood (QML)
              • the estimates stay consistent; the standard errors need a robust (sandwich) correction
            • Every evaluation of \(\ell(\theta)\) needs \(\mathbf{H}_t^{-1}\) and \(|\mathbf{H}_t|\) for all \(t\): the cost grows fast with \(N\) and with the number of parameters

            Section summary

            The framework leaves open the rule that updates \(\mathbf{H}_t\). The next section presents the two classic answers, VEC and BEKK, which model all elements of the matrix at once, and shows the price they pay in parameters.

            VEC and BEKK models

            The section presents the most general linear MGARCH model, VEC, and the BEKK model, which guarantees positive definiteness by construction. Counting their parameters shows why neither can be used for more than a handful of assets.

            The VEC model

            Slide 17 in the PDF

            Bollerslev, Engle and Wooldridge (1988) wrote the most general linear extension of GARCH(1,1): every distinct element of \(\mathbf{H}_t\) depends on every past squared shock and cross-product and on every element of \(\mathbf{H}_{t-1}\). With the \(\mathrm{vech}\) operator, the VEC(1,1) model is

            \[\begin{aligned} \mathrm{vech}(\mathbf{H}_t) = \mathbf{c} &+ \mathbf{A}\,\mathrm{vech}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}^\top) \\ &+ \mathbf{B}\,\mathrm{vech}(\mathbf{H}_{t-1}). \end{aligned}\]
            (14.5)

            The matrix \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}^\top\) holds yesterday's squared shocks on its diagonal and their cross-products off it. The vector \(\mathbf{c}\) holds constants, \(\mathbf{A}\) measures the reaction to shocks and \(\mathbf{B}\) the persistence, as \(\omega\), \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) do in a univariate GARCH.

            The generality is expensive. With \(k = N(N+1)/2\) distinct elements, \(\mathbf{c}\) has \(k\) entries and \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are \(k \times k\), so the model has \(k + 2k^2\) parameters: 21 for two assets and 6 105 for ten. Worse, nothing in (14.5) keeps \(\mathbf{H}_t\) positive definite; the restrictions that would do so are complicated and hard to impose during estimation.

            The diagonal VEC keeps only the diagonal of \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\), so that each element follows its own GARCH(1,1):

            \[h_{ij,t} = c_{ij} + a_{ij}\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\varepsilon_{j,t-1} + b_{ij}h_{ij,t-1}.\]

            This leaves \(3k\) parameters, 9 for \(N = 2\), but positive definiteness is still not guaranteed, because each element is updated without regard to the others.

            In brief

            • Bollerslev, Engle and Wooldridge (1988): every element of \(\mathbf{H}_t\) depends on every past squared shock, cross-product and element of \(\mathbf{H}_{t-1}\)
              • VEC(1,1): \(\mathrm{vech}(\mathbf{H}_t) = \mathbf{c} + \mathbf{A}\,\mathrm{vech}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}^\top) + \mathbf{B}\,\mathrm{vech}(\mathbf{H}_{t-1})\)
              • \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}^\top\): the matrix of yesterday's squared shocks and cross-products; \(\mathbf{c}\): a vector of constants; \(\mathbf{A}\): the reaction to shocks; \(\mathbf{B}\): the persistence
              • with \(k = N(N+1)/2\): \(\mathbf{c}\) has \(k\) elements, \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are \(k \times k\): in total \(k + 2k^2\) parameters
            • \(N = 2\): \(k = 3\) and 21 parameters; \(N = 10\): 6 105
              • and positive definiteness of \(\mathbf{H}_t\) is not guaranteed: it needs complicated restrictions
            • Diagonal VEC
              • \(h_{ij,t} = c_{ij} + a_{ij}\varepsilon_{i,t-1}\varepsilon_{j,t-1} + b_{ij}h_{ij,t-1}\)
              • each element is a GARCH(1,1) of its own; \(3k\) parameters (9 for \(N = 2\)); still no guarantee of positive definiteness

            The BEKK model

            Slide 18 in the PDF

            Engle and Kroner (1995) proposed a form named after Baba, Engle, Kraft and Kroner (BEKK) that is positive definite by construction:

            \[\begin{aligned} \mathbf{H}_t = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^\top &+ \mathbf{A}^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}^\top\mathbf{A} \\ &+ \mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{H}_{t-1}\mathbf{B}, \end{aligned}\]
            (14.6)

            with \(\mathbf{C}\) lower triangular and \(\mathbf{A}\), \(\mathbf{B}\) full \(N \times N\) matrices. \(\mathbf{A}\) describes the reaction to yesterday's shocks and \(\mathbf{B}\) the persistence of \(\mathbf{H}_{t-1}\); the elements of the constant matrix \(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^\top\) are written \(c_{ij}^*\).

            The guarantee comes from the form of each term, a "square" \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{M}\mathbf{X}\) of a positive semidefinite matrix \(\mathbf{M}\): the identity in the first term (with \(\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{C}^\top\)), the outer product \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}^\top\) in the second and \(\mathbf{H}_{t-1}\) in the third. For any vector \(\mathbf{w}\),

            \[\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{M}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} = (\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w})^\top\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}) \ge 0,\]

            and a constant term \(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^\top\) of full rank makes the sum strictly positive. The number of parameters is \(N(N+1)/2 + 2N^2\): 11 for two assets, 255 for ten.

            The off-diagonal elements of a full \(\mathbf{A}\) have an economic meaning: they measure volatility spillovers (volatility spillovers), the effect of a shock to asset 1 on the variance of asset 2. Restricting \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) to be diagonal gives the diagonal BEKK, in which the covariance follows

            \[\begin{aligned} h_{12,t} = c_{12}^* &+ a_{11}a_{22}\,\varepsilon_{1,t-1}\varepsilon_{2,t-1} \\ &+ b_{11}b_{22}\,h_{12,t-1}. \end{aligned}\]

            The scalar BEKK goes further, with \(\mathbf{A} = a\mathbf{I}\) and \(\mathbf{B} = b\mathbf{I}\), so that all elements share the same dynamics. Both restrictions save parameters and remove the spillovers.

            In brief

            • Engle and Kroner (1995): \(\mathbf{H}_t = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^\top + \mathbf{A}^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}^\top\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}^\top\mathbf{H}_{t-1}\mathbf{B}\)
              • BEKK: Baba, Engle, Kraft and Kroner; \(\mathbf{C}\) lower triangular, \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are \(N \times N\)
              • \(\mathbf{A}\): the reaction to yesterday's shocks; \(\mathbf{B}\): the persistence of \(\mathbf{H}_{t-1}\); \(c_{ij}^*\): the elements of \(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^\top\)
            • Positive definite by construction
              • each term is a “square” \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{M}\mathbf{X}\) of a positive (semi)definite matrix \(\mathbf{M}\)
              • \(\mathbf{M}\): \(\mathbf{I}\), \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}^\top\) or \(\mathbf{H}_{t-1}\); \(\mathbf{X}\): \(\mathbf{C}^\top\), \(\mathbf{A}\) or \(\mathbf{B}\)
              • parameters: \(N(N+1)/2 + 2N^2\), i.e. 11 for \(N = 2\) and 255 for \(N = 10\)
            • Diagonal BEKK (\(\mathbf{A}\), \(\mathbf{B}\) diagonal)
              • \(h_{12,t} = c_{12}^* + a_{11}a_{22}\,\varepsilon_{1,t-1}\varepsilon_{2,t-1} + b_{11}b_{22}\,h_{12,t-1}\)
              • scalar BEKK: \(\mathbf{A} = a\mathbf{I}\), \(\mathbf{B} = b\mathbf{I}\), the same dynamics for all elements
            • The off-diagonal elements of a full \(\mathbf{A}\) measure volatility spillovers: a shock to asset 1 raises the variance of asset 2

            Slide 19 in the PDF

The given values are \(c_{12}^* = 0.02\), \(a_{11} = 0.30\), \(a_{22} = 0.25\), \(b_{11} = 0.94\), \(b_{22} = 0.95\), yesterday's covariance \(h_{12,t-1} = 0.5\) and yesterday's shocks \(\varepsilon_{1,t-1} = -2\) and \(\varepsilon_{2,t-1} = -1.5\).             The cross-product of the shocks is \((-2)(-1.5) = 3.000\), positive because both assets fell. Today's covariance is

            \[\begin{aligned} h_{12,t} &= 0.020 + 0.075 \cdot 3.000 + 0.893 \cdot 0.5 \\ &= 0.020 + 0.225 + 0.4465 = 0.6915. \end{aligned}\]

            The example also shows what the diagonal model cannot do. Because \(a_{12} = a_{21} = 0\), a shock to asset 1 affects the variance of asset 2 only through the covariance, never directly: the diagonal BEKK has no volatility spillovers.

            In brief

            • Step 2: the cross-product of shocks \(\varepsilon_{1,t-1}\varepsilon_{2,t-1} = 3.000\) (both negative: a joint fall)
            • Step 3: \(h_{12,t} = 0.020 + 0.075 \cdot 3.000 + 0.893 \cdot 0.5 = 0.020 + 0.225 + 0.4465 = 0.6915\)

                        Section summary

                        VEC and BEKK model the whole matrix \(\mathbf{H}_t\) in one equation, and this is the source of their cost. The next section takes a different route: it splits \(\mathbf{H}_t\) into volatilities, which univariate GARCH models already handle, and a correlation matrix with very few dynamic parameters.

                        Constant and dynamic conditional correlations

                        The section decomposes the covariance matrix into volatilities and correlations, presents the constant conditional correlation model and Engle's dynamic conditional correlation model, explains their estimation in two steps and the test of DCC against CCC.

                        Splitting volatilities and correlations

                        Slide 24 in the PDF

                        Any covariance matrix can be written as volatilities times correlations:

                        \[\begin{gathered} \mathbf{H}_t = \mathbf{D}_t\mathbf{R}_t\mathbf{D}_t, \\ \mathbf{D}_t = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1,t}, \dots, \sigma_{N,t}), \end{gathered}\]
                        (14.7)

                        where \(\mathbf{R}_t\) is the conditional correlation matrix, with ones on the diagonal. Element by element, \(h_{ij,t} = \rho_{ij,t}\,\sigma_{i,t}\sigma_{j,t}\), which is (14.3) read the other way. Each volatility follows a univariate GARCH(1,1) model (Bollerslev, 1986):

                        \[\sigma_{i,t}^2 = \omega_i + \alpha_i\varepsilon_{i,t-1}^2 + \beta_i\sigma_{i,t-1}^2.\]

                        Dividing each shock by its own volatility gives the standardised residuals (standardised residuals) \(z_{i,t} = \varepsilon_{i,t}/\sigma_{i,t}\). Each has conditional variance one, and the conditional covariance of \(z_{i,t}\) and \(z_{j,t}\) is \(h_{ij,t}/(\sigma_{i,t}\sigma_{j,t}) = \rho_{ij,t}\). The conditional covariance matrix of the vector \(\mathbf{z}_t\) is therefore exactly \(\mathbf{R}_t\): once the volatilities are removed, only the correlations are left to model.

                        The simplest choice is the constant conditional correlation (CCC) model of Bollerslev (1990), with \(\mathbf{R}_t = \mathbf{R}\) for all \(t\). \(\mathbf{H}_t\) is then positive definite whenever \(\mathbf{R}\) is and the GARCH variances are positive, and \(\mathbf{R}\) is estimated by the sample correlation matrix of the \(z_{i,t}\). In CCC the covariances still move, but only through the volatilities. The rolling correlations of the first section contradict this assumption.

                        In brief

                        • Decomposition: \(\mathbf{H}_t = \mathbf{D}_t\mathbf{R}_t\mathbf{D}_t\),   \(\mathbf{D}_t = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1,t}, \dots, \sigma_{N,t})\)
                          • \(\mathbf{R}_t\): the conditional correlation matrix (ones on the diagonal); element by element: \(h_{ij,t} = \rho_{ij,t}\,\sigma_{i,t}\sigma_{j,t}\)
                          • each \(\sigma_{i,t}\) is a univariate GARCH(1,1) Bollerslev (1986): \(\sigma_{i,t}^2 = \omega_i + \alpha_i\varepsilon_{i,t-1}^2 + \beta_i\sigma_{i,t-1}^2\)
                        • Standardised residuals
                          • \(z_{i,t} = \varepsilon_{i,t}/\sigma_{i,t}\)
                          • their conditional covariance matrix is exactly \(\mathbf{R}_t\)
                        • CCC (constant conditional correlation, Bollerslev (1990))
                          • \(\mathbf{R}_t = \mathbf{R}\) for all \(t\)
                          • \(\mathbf{H}_t\) is positive definite whenever \(\mathbf{R}\) is; \(\mathbf{R}\) is estimated by the sample correlation of the \(z_{i,t}\)
                          • covariances still move, but only through the volatilities; the rolling correlations of Section 1 reject this

                        Robert Engle and the DCC model

                        Slide 25 in the PDF

                        Robert F. Engle, born in 1942, received the Nobel Prize in economics in 2003 for methods of analysing economic time series with time-varying volatility. Engle (2002) proposed letting the correlations follow a recursion of the same form as GARCH. An auxiliary matrix \(\mathbf{Q}_t\), with elements \(q_{ij,t}\), is updated each day from yesterday's vector of standardised residuals:

                        \[\begin{aligned} \mathbf{Q}_t &= (1 - a - b)\,\bar{\mathbf{Q}} + a\,\mathbf{z}_{t-1}\mathbf{z}_{t-1}^\top \\ &\quad + b\,\mathbf{Q}_{t-1}, \\ \rho_{ij,t} &= \frac{q_{ij,t}}{\sqrt{q_{ii,t}\,q_{jj,t}}}. \end{aligned}\]
                        (14.8)

                        The rescaling in the second part is needed because \(\mathbf{Q}_t\) is a weighted average of matrices whose diagonals are not exactly one; dividing by the square roots of the diagonal turns it into a proper correlation matrix with ones on the diagonal.

                        \(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\) is the unconditional correlation matrix of \(\mathbf{z}_t\), and it is set to its sample value before the optimisation. This is correlation targeting (correlation targeting): the recursion is anchored at the observed long-run correlation, and only \(a\) and \(b\) remain to be estimated. The parameter \(a \ge 0\) is the reaction to yesterday's joint shock, \(b \ge 0\) the persistence, and \(a + b < 1\) ensures that \(\mathbf{Q}_t\) reverts to \(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\).

                        With \(a = b = 0\) the recursion gives \(\mathbf{Q}_t = \bar{\mathbf{Q}}\) on every day, which is CCC. DCC therefore contains CCC as a special case, and this nesting is what makes a likelihood ratio test between the two possible.

                        In brief

                        Robert F. Engle (b. 1942), Nobel Prize 2003 Photo: Jon Demske (2022); CC BY-SA 4.0; Wikimedia Commons
                        • Engle (2002): let the correlations follow a GARCH-like recursion
                          • \(\mathbf{Q}_t = (1 - a - b)\,\bar{\mathbf{Q}} + a\,\mathbf{z}_{t-1}\mathbf{z}_{t-1}^\top + b\,\mathbf{Q}_{t-1}\)
                          • \(\mathbf{Q}_t\): an auxiliary matrix with elements \(q_{ij,t}\); \(\mathbf{z}_{t-1}\): yesterday's vector of standardised residuals
                          • \(\rho_{ij,t} = q_{ij,t}/\sqrt{q_{ii,t}\,q_{jj,t}}\): the rescaling puts ones on the diagonal
                        • \(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\): the unconditional correlation of \(\mathbf{z}_t\), set to its sample value (correlation targeting)
                          • \(a \ge 0\): reaction to yesterday's joint shock; \(b \ge 0\): persistence; \(a + b < 1\): mean reversion to \(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\)
                        • \(a = b = 0\) gives back CCC

                        Two-step estimation

                        Slide 26 in the PDF

                        Substituting (14.7) into the log-likelihood (14.4) splits it into two parts, \(\ell = \ell_V(\theta_1) + \ell_C(\theta_1, a, b)\). The volatility part \(\ell_V\) depends only on \(\theta_1\), the GARCH parameters of all series, and is the sum of the univariate GARCH likelihoods. The correlation part \(\ell_C\) depends on \(\theta_1\) through the standardised residuals and on \(a\) and \(b\).

                        The split suggests estimating in two steps. In step 1 a GARCH(1,1) is fitted to each series separately, as in Chapter 5, and the fitted volatilities \(\hat\sigma_{i,t}\) and standardised residuals \(\hat z_{i,t} = \hat\varepsilon_{i,t}/\hat\sigma_{i,t}\) are kept. In step 2, \(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\) is set to the sample correlation of \(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_t\) and the correlation log-likelihood is maximised over \((a, b)\) only:

                        \[\ell_C(a, b) = -\frac12\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\log|\mathbf{R}_t| + \hat{\mathbf{z}}_t^\top\mathbf{R}_t^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{z}}_t - \hat{\mathbf{z}}_t^\top\hat{\mathbf{z}}_t\right).\]
                        (14.9)

                        The estimator is consistent, fast and works for large \(N\). Its weak point is inference: the standard errors of step 2 treat the step-1 estimates as known and ignore their estimation error, so they are too small.

                        The test against CCC compares the maximised correlation log-likelihoods: \(LR = 2(\ell_C^{DCC} - \ell_C^{CCC})\), compared with the \(\chi^2(2)\) distribution, whose 5% critical value is 5.99. A large value rejects constant correlation. Because the null hypothesis \(a = b = 0\) lies on the boundary of the parameter space, the \(\chi^2(2)\) critical value is conservative: a rejection with it is a safe rejection. Tse (2000) proposed another test of constant correlations, based on the Lagrange multiplier principle.

                        In brief

                        • The log-likelihood splits into a volatility part and a correlation part: \(\ell = \ell_V(\theta_1) + \ell_C(\theta_1, a, b)\)
                          • \(\theta_1\): the GARCH parameters of all series; \(\ell_V\) depends only on them, \(\ell_C\) also on \(a\) and \(b\)
                        • Step 1
                          • fit a GARCH(1,1) to each series separately (Chapter 5)
                          • keep \(\hat\sigma_{i,t}\) and \(\hat z_{i,t} = \hat\varepsilon_{i,t}/\hat\sigma_{i,t}\)
                        • Step 2
                          • set \(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\) to the sample correlation of \(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_t\) and maximise over \((a, b)\) only
                          • \(\ell_C(a, b) = -\frac12\sum_t\left(\log|\mathbf{R}_t| + \hat{\mathbf{z}}_t^\top\mathbf{R}_t^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{z}}_t - \hat{\mathbf{z}}_t^\top\hat{\mathbf{z}}_t\right)\)
                        • Consistent, fast, works for large \(N\); the step-2 standard errors ignore the estimation error of step 1 (they are too small)
                        • Test against CCC
                          • likelihood ratio \(LR = 2(\ell_C^{DCC} - \ell_C^{CCC})\), compared with \(\chi^2(2)\) (5% critical value 5.99)
                          • also Tse (2000)
                          • \(\ell_C^{DCC}\), \(\ell_C^{CCC}\): the maximised correlation log-likelihoods of the two models; a large \(LR\) rejects constant correlation
                          • \(a = b = 0\) lies on the boundary of the parameter space, so the \(\chi^2(2)\) critical value is conservative

                        Worked example: one step of the DCC recursion

                        Slide 27 in the PDF

                        The recursion (14.8) can be followed by hand for two assets. The given values are \(a = 0.05\), \(b = 0.93\), a long-run correlation \(\bar q_{12} = 0.5\) (with \(\bar q_{11} = \bar q_{22} = 1\)), yesterday's matrix \(\mathbf{Q}_{t-1}\) with ones on the diagonal and 0.5 off it, and yesterday's standardised residuals \(z_{1,t-1} = -2\) and \(z_{2,t-1} = -2.5\), a large joint fall. The weight of the long-run matrix is \(1 - a - b = 0.020\).

                        The off-diagonal element and, because the squared residuals enter them, the diagonal elements are

                        \[\begin{aligned} q_{12,t} &= 0.020 \cdot 0.5 + 0.05 \cdot (-2)(-2.5) \\ &\quad + 0.93 \cdot 0.5 \\ &= 0.010 + 0.250 + 0.465 = 0.725, \\ q_{11,t} &= 0.020 + 0.05 \cdot 4 + 0.93 = 1.150, \\ q_{22,t} &= 0.020 + 0.05 \cdot 6.25 + 0.93 = 1.2625. \end{aligned}\]

                        The correlation is \(\rho_{12,t} = 0.725/\sqrt{1.150 \cdot 1.2625} = 0.602\).

                        The large joint fall raises tomorrow's correlation from 0.50 to 0.602. The rescaling matters: without it, the value 0.725 of \(q_{12,t}\) would be read as the correlation and overstate the reaction. Large shocks raise the diagonal of \(\mathbf{Q}_t\) as well, and the division by \(\sqrt{q_{11,t}q_{22,t}}\) takes this into account.

                        In brief

                        • Given: \(a = 0.05\), \(b = 0.93\), \(\bar q_{12} = 0.5\) (and \(\bar q_{11} = \bar q_{22} = 1\)), \(\mathbf{Q}_{t-1} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0.5\\ 0.5 & 1\end{pmatrix}\), yesterday \(z_{1,t-1} = -2\), \(z_{2,t-1} = -2.5\)
                        • Step 1: \(1 - a - b = 0.020\)
                        • Step 2: \(q_{12,t} = 0.020 \cdot 0.5 + 0.05 \cdot (-2)(-2.5) + 0.93 \cdot 0.5 = 0.010 + 0.250 + 0.465 = 0.725\)
                        • Step 3: \(q_{11,t} = 0.020 + 0.05 \cdot 4 + 0.93 = 1.150\);   \(q_{22,t} = 0.020 + 0.05 \cdot 6.25 + 0.93 = 1.2625\)
                        • Step 4: \(\rho_{12,t} = 0.725/\sqrt{1.150 \cdot 1.2625} = 0.602\)
                          • a large joint fall raises tomorrow's correlation from 0.50 to 0.602; without step 3 the “correlation” \(q_{12,t}\) would be 0.725

                        Reading \(a\) and \(b\)

                        Slide 28 in the PDF

                        The parameter \(a\) is the news coefficient: it measures how much one day's joint shock moves the correlation. Typical daily estimates lie between 0.01 and 0.05, so a single day moves the correlation only a little, and a large change requires a run of joint shocks.

                        The sum \(a + b\) is the persistence. Taking the long-run value away from both sides of (14.8) and replacing \(\mathbf{z}_{t-1}\mathbf{z}_{t-1}^\top\) by its conditional mean, approximately \(\mathbf{Q}_{t-1}\), shows that a deviation of \(q_{ij,t}\) from \(\bar q_{ij}\) is expected to shrink by the factor \(a + b\) each day. After \(n\) days it is multiplied by \((a+b)^n\), and it is halved when \((a+b)^n = 0.5\), which gives the half-life (half-life) \(\ln 0.5/\ln(a + b)\) days. With \(a + b = 0.98\) the half-life is 34 days; with \(0.99\) it is 69 days. Near one, small changes in \(a + b\) change the half-life a great deal.

                        The reading is the same as for \(\alpha\) and \(\alpha + \beta\) in a GARCH(1,1) model in Chapter 5. Two variants address limits of the basic model: the asymmetric DCC of Cappiello, Engle and Sheppard (2006) lets joint falls move correlations more than joint rises, and the corrected DCC of Aielli (2013) modifies the recursion so that the two-step estimator remains consistent.

                        In brief

                        • \(a\) is the news coefficient
                          • how much one day's joint shock moves the correlation
                          • typical daily values 0.01–0.05
                        • \(a + b\) is the persistence
                          • a deviation of \(q_{ij,t}\) from \(\bar q_{ij}\) shrinks by the factor \(a + b\) each day
                          • half-life: \(\ln 0.5/\ln(a + b)\) days; \(a + b = 0.98\) gives 34 days, \(0.99\) gives 69 days
                        • The same reading as \(\alpha\) and \(\alpha + \beta\) in a GARCH(1,1) (Chapter 5)

                        Recap: CCC and DCC

                        Slide 29 in the PDF

                        The decomposition (14.7) writes \(\mathbf{H}_t\) as GARCH volatilities times a correlation matrix. In CCC the correlation matrix is constant; in DCC the auxiliary matrix \(\mathbf{Q}_t\) follows a GARCH-like recursion with two parameters, \(a\) and \(b\). Estimation proceeds in two steps, univariate GARCH models first and \((a, b)\) with correlation targeting second, and a likelihood ratio test compares DCC with CCC.

                        In brief

                        • \(\mathbf{H}_t = \mathbf{D}_t\mathbf{R}_t\mathbf{D}_t\): GARCH volatilities times a correlation matrix
                        • CCC: \(\mathbf{R}\) constant; DCC: \(\mathbf{Q}_t\) follows a GARCH-like recursion with parameters \(a\), \(b\)
                        • Two steps: univariate GARCH, then \((a, b)\) with correlation targeting; LR test against CCC

                                                  Portfolio VaR 1% with DCC

                                                  The section computes the Value at Risk of a portfolio from \(\mathbf{H}_t\), presents the Kupiec and Christoffersen backtests and compares DCC, CCC and a static covariance matrix on an S&P 500 and DAX portfolio from 2015 to 2026.

                                                  From \(\mathbf{H}_t\) to the VaR of a portfolio

                                                  Slide 46 in the PDF

                                                  A portfolio with weights \(\mathbf{w}\) has the return \(r_{p,t} = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{r}_t\), with conditional mean \(\mu_p = \mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and conditional volatility \(\sigma_{p,t} = \sqrt{\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{H}_t\mathbf{w}}\), by (14.1) applied to the conditional covariance matrix. The VaR 1% is the loss that is exceeded with probability 1%, minus the 1% quantile of the portfolio return:

                                                  \[\mathrm{VaR}_{0.01,t} = -q_{0.01}(r_{p,t}) = -\left(\mu_p + q\,\sigma_{p,t}\right).\]
                                                  (14.10)

                                                  The value of \(q\) depends on the assumed distribution of the standardised portfolio return. Under the Normal distribution \(q = z_{0.01} = -2.326\). Filtered historical simulation (FHS) uses instead the empirical 1% quantile of the past standardised portfolio returns \((r_{p,t} - \mu_p)/\sigma_{p,t}\), which takes the fat tails of the data into account while keeping the volatility dynamics of the model.

                                                  Three choices of \(\mathbf{H}_t\) are compared: DCC; CCC, which has GARCH volatilities but a constant correlation; and a static matrix, a single sample covariance matrix used for all days. The static VaR is the same every day, the CCC VaR moves with the volatilities only, and the DCC VaR moves with both volatilities and correlation.

                                                  In brief

                                                  • Portfolio return \(r_{p,t} = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{r}_t\); conditional mean \(\mu_p = \mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and volatility \(\sigma_{p,t} = \sqrt{\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{H}_t\mathbf{w}}\)
                                                  • VaR 1%
                                                    • the loss exceeded with probability 1%, \(\mathrm{VaR}_{0.01,t} = -q_{0.01}(r_{p,t}) = -(\mu_p + q\,\sigma_{p,t})\)
                                                    • Normal: \(q = z_{0.01} = -2.326\)
                                                    • filtered historical simulation (FHS): \(q\) = the empirical 1% quantile of the past standardised portfolio returns \((r_{p,t} - \mu_p)/\sigma_{p,t}\)
                                                  • Three choices of \(\mathbf{H}_t\): DCC; CCC (GARCH volatilities with a constant correlation); static (one sample covariance matrix for all days)

                                                  Backtesting a VaR (1/2): frequency

                                                  Slide 48 in the PDF

                                                  A VaR model is judged by backtesting: comparing its forecasts with the returns that actually followed. A violation (violation) occurs on day \(t\) when \(r_{p,t} < -\mathrm{VaR}_{0.01,t}\), that is when the loss exceeds the VaR. A correct VaR 1% has violations on 1% of the days, and these days occur at random, not in clusters. The two properties are tested separately.

                                                  The test of Kupiec (1995) checks the frequency. With \(x\) violations in \(n\) days and observed rate \(\hat p = x/n\), the statistic

                                                  \[LR_{uc} = -2\log\frac{(1-0.01)^{n-x}\,0.01^{x}}{(1-\hat p)^{n-x}\,\hat p^{x}}\]
                                                  (14.11)

                                                  compares the likelihood of the observed violations under the nominal rate of 1% with their likelihood under the observed rate. The subscript \(uc\) stands for unconditional coverage. Under a correct model \(LR_{uc}\) follows approximately a \(\chi^2(1)\) distribution, and the model is rejected at 5% if \(LR_{uc} > 3.84\). The test rejects both too many violations, a VaR that is too low, and too few, a VaR that is too high.

                                                  In brief

                                                  • A violation on day \(t\)
                                                    • \(r_{p,t} < -\mathrm{VaR}_{0.01,t}\)
                                                    • a correct VaR 1% has violations on 1% of the days, at random times
                                                  • Kupiec test Kupiec (1995) (frequency)
                                                    • \(x\) violations in \(n\) days, \(\hat p = x/n\)
                                                    • \(LR_{uc} = -2\log\dfrac{(1-0.01)^{n-x}\,0.01^{x}}{(1-\hat p)^{n-x}\,\hat p^{x}} \sim \chi^2(1)\); reject at 5% if \(LR_{uc} > 3.84\)
                                                    • \(uc\): unconditional coverage; the ratio compares the likelihood of the violations under the rate 1% with that under the observed rate \(\hat p\)

                                                  Backtesting a VaR (2/2): independence

                                                  Slide 49 in the PDF

                                                  The test of Christoffersen (1998) checks independence: is a violation more likely on the day after a violation? It compares \(\pi_{11}\), the probability of a violation given a violation yesterday, with \(\pi_{01}\), the probability of a violation given no violation yesterday. For a correct model the two are equal, because a VaR that uses all available information leaves nothing predictable in the violations.

                                                  The statistic is built from counts: \(n_{ij}\) is the number of days in state \(j\) that follow a day in state \(i\), where state 1 is a violation and 0 is none. The estimates are \(\hat\pi_{01} = n_{01}/(n_{00}+n_{01})\) and \(\hat\pi_{11} = n_{11}/(n_{10}+n_{11})\), and \(\hat\pi\) is the overall violation rate. The statistic

                                                  \[LR_{ind} = -2\log\frac{(1-\hat\pi)^{n_{00}+n_{10}}\,\hat\pi^{\,n_{01}+n_{11}}}{(1-\hat\pi_{01})^{n_{00}}\,\hat\pi_{01}^{\,n_{01}}\,(1-\hat\pi_{11})^{n_{10}}\,\hat\pi_{11}^{\,n_{11}}}\]

                                                  has a numerator that assumes \(\pi_{01} = \pi_{11}\), no clustering, and a denominator that lets the two probabilities differ. Under independence it follows approximately \(\chi^2(1)\); a small p-value rejects independence.

                                                  A backtest is meaningful only out of sample. All parameters are estimated on 2000–2014, 3 712 days, and the filters are then run forward on 2015–2026, 2 893 days, without re-estimating. Each day's VaR uses only information available on the previous day, as it would in practice.

                                                  In brief

                                                  • Christoffersen test Christoffersen (1998) (independence)
                                                    • is a violation more likely the day after a violation?
                                                    • compares \(\pi_{11} = P(\text{violation} \mid \text{violation yesterday})\) with \(\pi_{01} = P(\text{violation} \mid \text{no violation yesterday})\)
                                                  • The statistic
                                                    • \(LR_{ind} = -2\log\dfrac{(1-\hat\pi)^{n_{00}+n_{10}}\,\hat\pi^{\,n_{01}+n_{11}}}{(1-\hat\pi_{01})^{n_{00}}\,\hat\pi_{01}^{\,n_{01}}\,(1-\hat\pi_{11})^{n_{10}}\,\hat\pi_{11}^{\,n_{11}}} \sim \chi^2(1)\)
                                                    • \(n_{ij}\): the number of days in state \(j\) after a day in state \(i\) (1: violation, 0: none); \(\hat\pi_{01} = n_{01}/(n_{00}+n_{01})\), \(\hat\pi_{11} = n_{11}/(n_{10}+n_{11})\)
                                                    • \(\hat\pi\): the overall violation rate; the numerator assumes \(\pi_{01} = \pi_{11}\) (no clustering); a small \(p\)-value rejects independence
                                                  • Honest design
                                                    • estimate all parameters on 2000–2014 (3 712 days), then run the filters forward on 2015–2026 (2 893 days) without re-estimating

                                                          Dynamic hedge ratios

                                                          The section derives the minimum-variance hedge ratio and its hedging effectiveness, makes the ratio dynamic with DCC, and compares static, rolling and DCC hedges of the BET with the DAX out of sample.

                                                          The minimum-variance hedge ratio

                                                          Slide 53 in the PDF

                                                          An investor holds one unit of an asset \(s\), for example Romanian shares represented by the BET, and sells \(h\) units of a hedging asset \(f\), for example a futures contract on the DAX. The hedged return is \(r_{s,t} - h\,r_{f,t}\), and its variance is \(\sigma_s^2 - 2h\,\sigma_{sf} + h^2\sigma_f^2\), where \(\sigma_s^2\) and \(\sigma_f^2\) are the variances of the two returns and \(\sigma_{sf}\) their covariance. Setting the derivative with respect to \(h\) to zero gives the minimum-variance hedge ratio (hedge ratio) of Ederington (1979):

                                                          \[h^* = \frac{\sigma_{sf}}{\sigma_f^2} = \rho\,\frac{\sigma_s}{\sigma_f}.\]
                                                          (14.12)

                                                          The static version of \(h^*\) is the slope of the ordinary least squares (OLS) regression of \(r_s\) on \(r_f\), because the OLS slope is exactly a covariance divided by a variance. The dynamic version, proposed by Kroner and Sultan (1993), uses the elements of \(\mathbf{H}_t\) from an MGARCH model, \(h_t^* = h_{sf,t}/h_{ff,t}\), and therefore changes with the forecast covariance and variance every day.

                                                          The hedging effectiveness (hedging effectiveness) measures the share of variance removed: \(HE = 1 - \Var(r_s - h\,r_f)/\Var(r_s)\). Substituting the constant optimal ratio into the hedged variance gives \(\sigma_s^2(1 - \rho^2)\), so \(HE = \rho^2\). A hedge is only as good as the correlation between the asset and the hedging instrument.

                                                          In brief

                                                          • Hold one unit of asset \(s\) (e.g. Romanian shares, the BET) and sell \(h\) units of a hedging asset \(f\) (e.g. a DAX futures contract)
                                                            • hedged return \(r_{s,t} - h\,r_{f,t}\), with variance \(\sigma_s^2 - 2h\,\sigma_{sf} + h^2\sigma_f^2\)
                                                            • \(\sigma_s^2\), \(\sigma_f^2\): the variances of the two returns; \(\sigma_{sf}\): their covariance
                                                          • Setting the derivative in \(h\) to zero: \(h^* = \dfrac{\sigma_{sf}}{\sigma_f^2} = \rho\,\dfrac{\sigma_s}{\sigma_f}\) Ederington (1979)
                                                            • static \(h^*\): the OLS slope of \(r_s\) on \(r_f\)
                                                            • dynamic: \(h_t^* = h_{sf,t}/h_{ff,t}\) from an MGARCH model Kroner and Sultan (1993); \(h_{sf,t}\), \(h_{ff,t}\): elements of \(\mathbf{H}_t\)
                                                          • Hedging effectiveness
                                                            • \(HE = 1 - \Var(r_s - h\,r_f)/\Var(r_s)\), the share of the variance removed
                                                            • with \(h = h^*\) constant: \(HE = \rho^2\); a hedge is only as good as the correlation

                                                          Worked example: a hedge ratio

                                                          Slide 54 in the PDF

                                                          Take daily volatilities \(\sigma_s = 1.2\%\) and \(\sigma_f = 1.0\%\) and a correlation \(\rho = 0.8\). The covariance is \(\sigma_{sf} = \rho\,\sigma_s\sigma_f = 0.8 \cdot 1.2 \cdot 1.0 = 0.96\), and by (14.12) the hedge ratio is \(h^* = 0.96/1 = 0.96\): the investor sells 0.96 units of \(f\) for each unit of \(s\).

                                                          The hedging effectiveness is \(\rho^2 = 0.64\), so the hedge removes 64% of the variance. The volatility of the hedged position is \(\sigma_s\sqrt{1 - \rho^2} = 0.72\%\) instead of 1.2%. Volatility falls less than variance, by 40% instead of 64%, because it is the square root.

                                                          If tomorrow the DCC model forecasts \(\rho_t = 0.5\) and \(\sigma_{f,t} = 2\%\), with \(\sigma_s\) unchanged, the ratio becomes \(0.5 \cdot 1.2/2 = 0.3\). Both the lower correlation and the higher volatility of the hedging asset reduce the number of units to sell, and the position must be adjusted.

                                                          In brief

                                                          • Given: \(\sigma_s = 1.2\%\), \(\sigma_f = 1.0\%\) (daily), \(\rho = 0.8\)

                                                                  Links with Chapters 5, 6 and 7

                                                                  Slide 58 in the PDF

                                                                  Every MGARCH model contains univariate GARCH models: the diagonal of \(\mathbf{H}_t\) consists of conditional variances, as in Chapter 5. In DCC the link is literal, since the univariate GARCH models are step 1 of the estimation.

                                                                  A VAR model of Chapter 6 describes spillovers in the mean, for example whether yesterday's DAX return helps to forecast today's BET return. A BEKK model with a full matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) describes spillovers in the variance, whether yesterday's shock to the DAX raises today's variance of the BET. The two combine naturally: a VAR for the mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_t\) and an MGARCH model for \(\mathbf{H}_t\). Diebold and Yilmaz (2009) built spillover indices from the variance decompositions of a VAR.

                                                                  Cointegration, from Chapter 7, is a long-run link between price levels; correlation is a short-run link between returns. The two are distinct. Two prices can be highly correlated from day to day and still drift apart for ever, or be cointegrated with a low daily correlation. A vector error correction model (VECM) can carry DCC errors, with equilibrium in the mean and dynamic correlation in the shocks.

                                                                  Limits and extensions

                                                                  Slide 59 in the PDF

                                                                  DCC buys its parsimony with restrictions. A single pair \((a, b)\) drives all correlations, so pairs that adjust at different speeds are forced to the same rhythm, as the five-market model showed. The volatilities come from separate univariate GARCH models, so DCC has no spillovers between volatilities. And the Normal quasi-likelihood says nothing about the tails: for risk measures, Student \(t\) innovations or filtered historical simulation are needed, as the backtest showed.

                                                                  Correlations also react asymmetrically: they rise more after joint falls than after joint rises, which the asymmetric DCC of Cappiello, Engle and Sheppard (2006) captures. For hundreds of assets even DCC becomes hard to handle, because the sample matrix \(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\) is noisy and the step-2 likelihood requires inverting a large matrix every day; factor models, composite likelihood and shrinkage of \(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\) are the usual answers.

                                                                  Finally, correlation measures linear dependence only. Two assets can have a moderate correlation and still crash together much more often than a Normal model implies. Joint extremes are described by copulas and by tail dependence; Longin and Solnik (2001) studied the correlation of international equity markets in the tails directly.

                                                                  In brief

                                                                  Frankfurt Stock Exchange Photo: Ank Kumar (2015); CC BY-SA 4.0; Wikimedia Commons
                                                                  • One \((a, b)\) for all pairs; no spillovers between volatilities in DCC
                                                                  • Normal QML underestimates the tails: use Student \(t\) innovations or FHS for risk
                                                                  • Asymmetry: correlations react more to joint falls (asymmetric DCC, Cappiello, Engle and Sheppard (2006))
                                                                  • Hundreds of assets: factor models, composite likelihood and shrinkage of \(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\)
                                                                  • Correlation is linear dependence; for joint extremes see copulas and tail dependence Longin and Solnik (2001)

                                                                  Section summary

                                                                  DCC is the workhorse of multivariate volatility because it splits a hard problem into univariate GARCH models and a correlation recursion with two parameters. Its limits, a common speed for all pairs, no volatility spillovers and Normal tails, are known and have standard extensions.

                                                                  Section summary

                                                                  The chapter extended volatility modelling from one series to several. The central object is the conditional covariance matrix, and the central idea of DCC is to model volatilities and correlations separately, with univariate GARCH models for the first and a two-parameter recursion for the second. On real data the correlations move, rise in crises most for the weakest links, and change the risk of a portfolio and the size of a hedge.
