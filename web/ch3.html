Most macroeconomic and financial series rise over time: real gross domestic product (GDP), the price level, the exchange rate, stock indices. Their mean is not constant, so the stationary tools of the previous chapters do not apply to them directly. The question of the chapter is what kind of trend these series have. If the trend is a fixed line, a shock moves the series away from the line only temporarily. If the trend is stochastic, every shock stays in the level of the series for ever. The two cases call for different treatments: in the first the trend is removed, in the second the series is differenced.

The difference has direct practical consequences. A trend-stationary model estimated on US real GDP up to 2007Q4 expected output to return to its pre-crisis line. The return never came: in 2026Q2, observed GDP was about 30 log points below that forecast, while the half-width of the 95% interval was only 8 points. The difference-stationary model also misses, by about 25 points, but its interval, with a half-width of 23, at least signals that the uncertainty is large. The nature of the trend also decides whether a regression between two trending series makes sense: the question of Yule (1926) in the epigraph concerns exactly the correlations found between series that evolve as sums of shocks.

The chapter first treats the difference between the two kinds of trend and spurious regression. It then turns to the tests that decide between them, augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF), Phillips–Perron (PP) and KPSS, with their limits in short samples and in the presence of structural breaks. The last part builds the ARIMA\((p,d,q)\) models, which add differencing to the ARMA models of Chapter 2, and uses them to forecast Romanian GDP, inflation and the EUR/RON exchange rate.

Deterministic and stochastic trends

A trending series can be described in two ways. The first is a fixed line plus stationary deviations from it: a deterministic trend. The second is a cumulative sum of shocks, possibly with a drift: a stochastic trend. On a chart the two look almost the same; they differ in the effect of a shock, in the long-run variance and in the correct treatment. The section defines the two models, integrated processes and the unit root, then shows what happens when a series receives the wrong treatment.

    Trend-stationary series

    Slide 8 in the PDF

    A series is trend-stationary if it can be written as a deterministic function of time plus a stationary process:

    \[y_t = \alpha + \beta t + u_t, \qquad u_t \sim I(0).\]
    (3.1)

    In (3.1), \(\alpha\) is the intercept, \(\beta\) the slope, that is the average change per period, and \(u_t\) a stationary process, for example an AR(1) \(u_t = \phi u_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) with \(|\phi| < 1\) (Chapter 2). The mean \(E[y_t] = \alpha + \beta t\) depends on time, so \(y_t\) is not stationary, but the variance \(\mathrm{Var}(y_t) = \mathrm{Var}(u_t)\) is constant and \(y_t - \alpha - \beta t\) is stationary.

    A shock \(\varepsilon_t\) moves the series away from the line only temporarily. With AR(1) errors, its effect after \(h\) periods is \(\phi^h\), which goes to zero geometrically. Long-run forecasts therefore return to the line \(\alpha + \beta(T+h)\), and the forecast-error variance converges to \(\mathrm{Var}(u_t)\): forecast intervals have a bounded width. The correct treatment is to estimate the trend by ordinary least squares (OLS) and to model the residuals as an ARMA process.

    In brief

    • Definition: \(y_t = \alpha + \beta t + u_t\), with \(u_t\) a stationary process (for example a stationary AR(1), Chapter 2)
      • \(\alpha\): the intercept; \(\beta\): the slope, the average change per period; \(t\): the time index
      • \(y_t\) is trend-stationary (TS): stationary after subtracting the line \(\alpha + \beta t\)
    • Properties
      • \(E[y_t] = \alpha + \beta t\); \(\mathrm{Var}(y_t) = \mathrm{Var}(u_t)\), constant
      • a shock moves \(y_t\) away from the line only temporarily: with \(u_t = \phi u_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\), its effect after \(h\) periods is \(\phi^h\)
      • long-run forecasts return to the line; their intervals have a bounded width
    • Right treatment: estimate the trend by OLS (ordinary least squares) and model the residuals as ARMA

    Difference-stationary series

    Slide 9 in the PDF

    A series is difference-stationary if \(y_t = \beta + y_{t-1} + u_t\), that is if its first difference is stationary:

    \[\Delta y_t = \beta + u_t, \qquad u_t \sim I(0).\]
    (3.2)

    Here \(\beta\) is the drift, the average change per period. By repeated substitution, \(y_t = y_0 + \beta t + \sum_{i=1}^{t} u_i\), where \(y_0\) is the initial value. The series has two trend components: the deterministic term \(\beta t\), which comes from the drift, and the cumulative sum of shocks \(\sum_{i \le t} u_i\), called the stochastic trend.

    The mean is the same as in (3.1), \(y_0 + \beta t\), but the variance grows with time: for a random walk, with \(u_t\) white noise of variance \(\sigma^2\), \(\mathrm{Var}(y_t) = t\sigma^2\) (Chapter 1). Every shock enters the cumulative sum and stays in the level for ever; there is no line to which the series returns. As a consequence, forecast intervals widen without limit as the horizon grows. The correct treatment is to difference the series and to model \(\Delta y_t\) as ARMA, that is the ARIMA model of section 6.

    In brief

    • Definition: \(y_t = \beta + y_{t-1} + u_t\), \(u_t\) stationary; by substitution, \(y_t = y_0 + \beta t + \sum_{i=1}^{t} u_i\)
      • \(\beta\): the drift, the average change per period; \(y_0\): the starting value
      • \(y_t\) is difference-stationary (DS): \(\Delta y_t = \beta + u_t\) is stationary
      • \(\sum_{i \le t} u_i\) is the stochastic trend; \(\beta t\) is the drift
    • Properties
      • the same mean \(y_0 + \beta t\) as a TS series, but \(\mathrm{Var}(y_t)\) grows with \(t\) (Chapter 1: \(t\sigma^2\) for a random walk)
      • every shock stays in the level forever: there is no line to return to
      • forecast intervals widen without limit as the horizon grows
    • Right treatment: difference the series and model \(\Delta y_t\) as ARMA: the ARIMA model of this chapter

      Integrated processes

      Slide 12 in the PDF

      The notion that generalises a difference-stationary series is the order of integration. A process \(y_t\) is integrated of order \(d\), written \(y_t \sim I(d)\), if \(\Delta^d y_t\) is stationary and \(\Delta^{d-1} y_t\) is not. An \(I(0)\) process is stationary, with an invertible ARMA representation (Chapter 2); an \(I(1)\) process becomes stationary after one difference, and an \(I(2)\) process after two. The second difference is \(\Delta^2 y_t = \Delta y_t - \Delta y_{t-1} = y_t - 2y_{t-1} + y_{t-2}\).

      In economics and finance the typical orders are known. Log stock prices, exchange rates and log GDP are usually \(I(1)\), while returns and growth rates, their differences, are \(I(0)\). The price level in periods of high inflation may be \(I(2)\); inflation, the difference of the log price level, is then itself \(I(1)\).

      Two simple rules help with the classification. The sum of an \(I(0)\) and an \(I(1)\) process is \(I(1)\): the stochastic trend dominates the stationary component. The second rule concerns trend-stationary series: they become \(I(0)\) after detrending, not after differencing, and section 6 shows what happens if they are differenced nonetheless.

      In brief

      • Definition: \(y_t\) is integrated of order \(d\), \(y_t \sim I(d)\), if \(\Delta^d y_t\) is stationary and \(\Delta^{d-1}y_t\) is not
        • \(I(0)\): stationary (with an invertible ARMA representation, Chapter 2); \(I(1)\): stationary after one difference; \(I(2)\): after two
        • \(\Delta^2 y_t = \Delta y_t - \Delta y_{t-1} = y_t - 2y_{t-1} + y_{t-2}\)
      • Typical orders in economics and finance
        • log prices of stocks, exchange rates, log GDP: \(I(1)\); returns and growth rates: \(I(0)\)
        • price levels in high-inflation periods: possibly \(I(2)\), so that inflation itself is \(I(1)\)
      • Rules
        • \(I(0) + I(1) = I(1)\): the stochastic trend dominates; a TS series is \(I(0)\) after removing the trend, not after differencing

      The unit root

      Slide 13 in the PDF

      The order of integration has an algebraic characterisation through the autoregressive polynomial. An AR(\(p\)) is written with the lag operator \(L\), \(Ly_t = y_{t-1}\), as \(\phi(L)y_t = \varepsilon_t\), where \(\phi(z) = 1 - \phi_1 z - \dots - \phi_p z^p\). From Chapter 2, the process is stationary if all roots of \(\phi(z) = 0\) lie outside the unit circle, \(|z| > 1\).

      A unit root means that \(z = 1\) is a root of \(\phi\). The polynomial then factors as \(\phi(z) = (1 - z)\phi^*(z)\), and if the roots of \(\phi^*\) lie outside the circle, \(\phi^*(L)(1 - L)y_t = \phi^*(L)\Delta y_t = \varepsilon_t\). The difference \(\Delta y_t\) is a stationary AR(\(p-1\)), so \(y_t \sim I(1)\). For an AR(1), \(\phi(z) = 1 - \phi z\) has the root \(1/\phi\), which is a unit root exactly when \(\phi = 1\): the random walk.

      The example on the slide shows the factorisation. For \(y_t = 1.5y_{t-1} - 0.5y_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t\), the polynomial is \(1 - 1.5z + 0.5z^2 = (1 - z)(1 - 0.5z)\), with roots 1 and 2. The first is a unit root, the second lies outside the circle, so \(\Delta y_t = 0.5\Delta y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\): a stationary AR(1) in differences. The coefficients of the levels, 1.5 and \(-0.5\), sum to exactly 1, and this is the condition \(\phi(1) = 0\) used by the tests of section 3.

      In brief

      • Write an AR(\(p\)) with the lag operator \(L\) (Chapter 1): \(\phi(L)y_t = \varepsilon_t\), \(\phi(z) = 1 - \phi_1 z - \dots - \phi_p z^p\)
        • Chapter 2: stationary if all roots of \(\phi(z) = 0\) lie outside the unit circle, \(|z| > 1\)
      • Unit root: one root equals \(z = 1\), so \(\phi(z) = (1 - z)\,\phi^*(z)\) with the roots of \(\phi^*\) outside the circle
        • then \(\phi^*(L)\,\Delta y_t = \varepsilon_t\): \(\Delta y_t\) is a stationary AR(\(p-1\)), and \(y_t \sim I(1)\)
        • AR(1): \(\phi(z) = 1 - \phi z\) has the root \(1/\phi\), a unit root when \(\phi = 1\): the random walk
      • Example: \(y_t = 1.5y_{t-1} - 0.5y_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t\); \(1 - 1.5z + 0.5z^2 = (1 - z)(1 - 0.5z)\)
        • roots 1 and 2: \(\Delta y_t = 0.5\,\Delta y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\), an AR(1) in differences

      Case study: Nelson and Plosser (1982)

      Slide 14 in the PDF

      Until the early 1980s, business-cycle models usually assumed that macroeconomic series are stationary around a trend: the cycle was the temporary deviation from a line. Nelson and Plosser (1982) put this assumption to the test on 14 long annual US series, some starting in 1860: real gross national product (GNP), employment, prices, wages, interest rates and stock prices.

      The method was the Dickey–Fuller test of section 3, in the version with constant and trend, whose alternative is exactly trend stationarity. The unit root was rejected for a single series, the unemployment rate. For the other 13, the data did not allow a stochastic trend to be rejected.

      Their conclusion, that shocks to output appear permanent, opened a broad debate on the nature of recessions. It also carried a methodological warning: removing a straight-line trend from a difference-stationary series produces artefacts, illustrated in the next frame. The result was later challenged by Perron (1989), who showed that structural breaks can mimic a unit root (section 5).

      In brief

      • Question: are US macroeconomic series stationary around a trend, as the business-cycle models of the 1970s assumed?
        • Nelson and Plosser (1982): 14 long annual series (real GNP, employment, prices, wages, interest rates, stock prices), some since 1860
      • Method: the Dickey–Fuller test of Section 3, with a constant and a trend
        • the unit root was rejected for one series only, the unemployment rate
      • Consequence: shocks to output look permanent, not transitory
        • a large debate on the nature of recessions; and a warning against detrending by a straight line (next slide)
        • the answer was later challenged by structural breaks (Section 5)

        Recap: Deterministic and stochastic trends

        Slide 17 in the PDF

        The two trend models differ in the memory of shocks. In a trend-stationary series, (3.1), shocks die out and forecasts return to the line. In a difference-stationary series, (3.2), shocks are permanent and the variance grows with time. The order of integration \(I(d)\) counts the differences needed for stationarity, and a unit root of the polynomial \(\phi(z)\) means \(I(1)\).

        The wrong treatment creates artefacts in both directions. Detrending a difference-stationary series produces spurious cycles and misleading \(t\)-statistics. Differencing a trend-stationary series produces over-differencing. This is why the choice between the two treatments requires a test, not a visual inspection.

        In brief

        • TS: \(y_t = \alpha + \beta t + u_t\), shocks fade; DS: \(\Delta y_t = \beta + u_t\), shocks are permanent
        • \(I(d)\): stationary after \(d\) differences; a unit root in \(\phi(z)\) means \(I(1)\)
        • The wrong treatment creates artefacts: spurious cycles (detrending a DS series) or over-differencing (differencing a TS series)

        Section summary

        Trending economic series can be stationary around a line or can contain a stochastic trend; in the second case every shock stays in the level and the variance grows with time. The distinction is expressed algebraically by a unit root of the AR polynomial and decides the treatment: detrending or differencing. The simulations show that the two models cannot be told apart by eye and that the wrong treatment produces false cycles and false slopes. The next section extends the problem to regressions between two series with stochastic trends.

        Spurious regression

        The problem of the previous section, a regression on time that looks significant although there is no slope, also arises when one \(I(1)\) series is regressed on another \(I(1)\) series independent of it. The phenomenon is called spurious regression. The section follows its history, from Yule (1926) to Granger and Newbold (1974), reproduces it by simulation, explains why the \(t\)-test fails and illustrates it on real Romanian and US data.

        1926: nonsense correlations

        Slide 18 in the PDF

        The first to raise the problem systematically was the British statistician George Udny Yule. The paper of Yule (1926) opens with the question in its title: why do we sometimes get nonsense correlations between time series? His example: in England and Wales, between 1866 and 1911, the proportion of marriages celebrated by the Church of England and the mortality rate had a correlation of about 0.95. There is no causal link between them; both series fell over the period.

        Yule's explanation anticipates the modern theory. For series that evolve as cumulative sums of random terms, the sample correlation does not concentrate around 0, even when the series are independent. Large values often occur by chance, and the usual standard error of the correlation coefficient, built for independent observations, does not apply.

        The problem returned fifty years later, in econometric models estimated on the levels of trending macroeconomic series. The next frame describes its rediscovery.

        In brief

        1974: Granger and Newbold

        Slide 19 in the PDF

        Granger and Newbold (1974) studied by simulation the regression of one random walk on another, independent random walk: \(y_t = a + bx_t + e_t\), with \(y_t\) and \(x_t\) independent \(I(1)\) series. The true slope is \(b = 0\). The \(t\)-test of \(b = 0\) nevertheless rejected far too often, the \(R^2\) was frequently large and the Durbin–Watson (DW) statistic was close to 0.

        The Durbin–Watson statistic measures the first-order autocorrelation of the residuals: \(\mathrm{DW} \approx 2(1 - \hat\rho_1)\). A value close to 2 indicates uncorrelated residuals, while a value close to 0 indicates highly persistent, \(I(1)\)-like residuals. Hence the authors' rule of thumb: a regression should be suspected of being spurious when \(R^2 > \mathrm{DW}\), that is when the apparent fit is larger than the signal of autocorrelation in the residuals.

        Clive Granger and Paul Newbold were then at the University of Nottingham. Granger received the Nobel Prize in economics in 2003, for methods of analysing series with common trends, cointegration, covered in Chapter 7.

          Why the \(t\)-test fails

          Slide 22 in the PDF

          The explanation lies in the error term. If \(b = 0\), the regression error is \(e_t = y_t - a\), a random walk. The OLS assumptions, stationary and uncorrelated errors, do not hold: the residuals are strongly autocorrelated, and the usual standard error, computed as if they were independent, is far too small. The \(t\)-ratio is therefore far too large.

          Phillips (1986) derived the exact large-sample behaviour, using asymptotic theory for integrated processes. The estimator \(\hat b\) does not converge to 0 but to a random variable: each sample produces a different slope, however long it is. The statistic \(t_{\hat b}\) grows like \(\sqrt{T}\), so the rejection rate goes to 100%. The \(R^2\) has a non-degenerate limiting distribution, which explains the stable median of about 0.17 in the simulation, and \(\mathrm{DW} \to 0\).

          There is an important exception. If \(y_t - bx_t\) is stationary for some \(b\), the series share a common stochastic trend and are called cointegrated; the regression in levels then makes sense, and \(\hat b\) estimates the equilibrium relation (Engle and Granger, 1987). Cointegration is the subject of Chapter 7.

          In brief

          • Under \(b = 0\) the error \(e_t = y_t - a\) is itself a random walk: the OLS assumptions (stationary, uncorrelated errors) fail
            • the usual standard error is far too small
          • Phillips (1986) derived the large-sample behaviour
            • \(\hat b\) does not converge to 0: it converges to a random variable
            • \(t_{\hat b}\) grows like \(\sqrt{T}\), so the rejection rate tends to 100%
            • \(R^2\) has a non-degenerate limit distribution; \(\mathrm{DW} \to 0\)
          • Exception: if \(y_t - b x_t\) is stationary for some \(b\), the series are cointegrated and the levels regression is meaningful Engle and Granger (1987) (Chapter 7)

                The Dickey–Fuller test

                The Dickey–Fuller test turns the question "deterministic or stochastic trend?" into a hypothesis about one coefficient: is the root of the AR polynomial equal to 1? The difficulty is that, under the null, the test statistic does not have the usual distribution but one of its own, shifted to the left. The section presents the test, its origin, the three versions according to the deterministic terms, the distributions and critical values, a worked example on Romanian GDP, the augmented version for autocorrelated errors, lag choice, the power of the test and the determination of the order of integration.

                Testing \(\phi = 1\)

                Slide 26 in the PDF

                Start from an AR(1), \(y_t = \phi y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\). Subtracting \(y_{t-1}\) from both sides gives the form used by the test:

                \[\Delta y_t = \gamma y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \gamma = \phi - 1.\]
                (3.3)

                The null hypothesis is \(H_0\): \(\gamma = 0\), that is \(\phi = 1\), a unit root and \(y_t \sim I(1)\). The alternative is \(H_1\): \(\gamma < 0\), that is \(|\phi| < 1\) and a stationary series. The test is one-sided, to the left. Its statistic is the usual \(t\)-ratio from the OLS regression (3.3), \(\tau = \hat\gamma/\mathrm{SE}(\hat\gamma)\), where \(\mathrm{SE}\) is the standard error. A strongly negative value of \(\tau\) is evidence against the unit root.

                The novelty, established by Dickey and Fuller (1979), is that under \(H_0\) the statistic \(\tau\) follows neither the Student law nor the Normal distribution, not even asymptotically. The reason is that the regressor \(y_{t-1}\) is itself a random walk, not a stationary variable. The sum \(\sum y_{t-1}^2\) grows like \(T^2\), not \(T\), and the estimator \(\hat\phi\) converges to 1 at rate \(T\), not \(\sqrt{T}\): this property is called superconsistency. The limit of \(\tau\) is a functional of Brownian motion \(W\),

                \[\tau \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \frac{\tfrac12\big(W(1)^2 - 1\big)}{\big(\int_0^1 W(r)^2\,dr\big)^{1/2}},\]

                a skewed distribution, with its mass shifted to the left [Hamilton (1994), ch. 17]. It has no closed form, so the critical values come from simulation. The first tables were published by Fuller (1996); today the response surfaces of MacKinnon (2010) are used, which give the critical value for any \(T\).

                In brief

                • AR(1): \(y_t = \phi y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\); subtract \(y_{t-1}\): \(\Delta y_t = \gamma y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\), with \(\gamma = \phi - 1\)
                  • \(H_0\): \(\gamma = 0\) (unit root, \(I(1)\)); \(H_1\): \(\gamma < 0\) (stationary); a one-sided, left-tail test
                  • statistic: \(\tau = \hat\gamma/\mathrm{SE}(\hat\gamma)\), the usual OLS \(t\)-ratio; \(\mathrm{SE}\): the standard error; a very negative \(\tau\) speaks against the unit root
                • Under \(H_0\), \(\tau\) is not Student or Normal Dickey and Fuller (1979)
                  • \(y_{t-1}\) is a random walk, so the regressor is not stationary; \(\hat\phi\) converges at rate \(T\), not \(\sqrt{T}\) (superconsistency)
                  • the limit of \(\tau\) is a functional of Brownian motion, skewed to the left Hamilton (1994), Ch. 17
                  • critical values come from simulation: Fuller (1996); today from response surfaces MacKinnon (2010)

                Three Dickey–Fuller regressions

                Slide 28 in the PDF

                Regression (3.3) has as its alternative a stationary process with mean zero, rarely a realistic description of the data. The test is therefore applied in three versions, according to the deterministic terms included. The version without constant, \(\Delta y_t = \gamma y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\), has as alternative a stationary process with mean 0. The version with constant, \(\Delta y_t = c + \gamma y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\), has as null a random walk, with drift if \(c \ne 0\), and as alternative a process stationary around a non-zero mean. It suits series without a trend: interest rates, inflation, exchange rates, returns.

                The version with constant and trend is

                \[\Delta y_t = c + bt + \gamma y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t,\]

                where \(c\) is the constant and \(bt\) a linear trend with slope \(b\). The null is a random walk with drift and the alternative a trend-stationary series. This is exactly the choice between (3.2) and (3.1) in section 1, so this version is used for trending series: log GDP, log prices and log price indices.

                Each version has its own distribution of \(\tau\) and its own critical values. The deterministic terms change the distribution because the regression has to estimate them, and their estimation absorbs part of the variation of \(y_{t-1}\) that would otherwise be attributed to \(\gamma\).

                In brief

                • No constant: \(\Delta y_t = \gamma y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\)

                • Constant: \(\Delta y_t = c + \gamma y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\)
                  • \(H_0\): random walk (with drift if \(c \ne 0\)); \(H_1\): stationary around a non-zero mean
                  • for series without a trend: interest rates, inflation, exchange rates, returns
                • Constant and trend: \(\Delta y_t = c + bt + \gamma y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\)
                  • \(c\): the constant; \(bt\): a linear trend with slope \(b\)
                  • \(H_0\): random walk with drift; \(H_1\): trend-stationary: exactly the TS against DS question of Section 1
                  • for trending series: log GDP, log prices, log price indices
                • Each case has its own distribution of \(\tau\) and its own critical values

                        The augmented Dickey–Fuller test

                        Slide 33 in the PDF

                        The Dickey–Fuller regression assumes that the error \(\varepsilon_t\) is white noise. If \(\Delta y_t\) is autocorrelated, the autocorrelation passes into the error and the distribution of \(\tau\) changes: the size of the test, the probability of rejecting a true unit root, is no longer the nominal one. The remedy is to add past differences \(\Delta y_{t-j}\) as regressors, which absorb the autocorrelation. The result is the augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) regression:

                        \[\begin{aligned} \Delta y_t ={}& c + bt + \gamma y_{t-1} \\ &+ \sum_{j=1}^{k} \delta_j\,\Delta y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t. \end{aligned}\]
                        (3.4)

                        In (3.4), \(\delta_j\) are the coefficients of the past differences and \(k\) is their number, called the lag length. The statistic remains \(\tau = \hat\gamma/\mathrm{SE}(\hat\gamma)\), and the critical values are the same as in the regression without lags. For an AR(\(p\)) in levels, (3.4) is exact: any AR(\(p\)) can be rewritten as a regression of \(\Delta y_t\) on \(y_{t-1}\) and \(p-1\) past differences, with \(k = p - 1\) and \(\gamma = -\phi(1)\). The unit root, \(\phi(1) = 0\), therefore still corresponds to \(\gamma = 0\).

                        When the errors are ARMA, with an MA component, no finite \(k\) is exact. Said and Dickey (1984) showed that the test remains valid if \(k\) grows slowly with \(T\): the past differences approximate the MA part by a long AR. The ADF test is the default unit-root test in statistical software, for example the adfuller function in statsmodels.

                        In brief

                        • If \(\Delta y_t\) is autocorrelated, \(\varepsilon_t\) in the DF regression is not white noise and the size of the test is wrong
                          • the remedy: add lagged differences
                        • ADF (augmented Dickey–Fuller) regression:
                          • \(\Delta y_t = c + bt + \gamma y_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{k} \delta_j \Delta y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t\); same \(\tau\), same critical values
                          • \(\delta_j\): the coefficients of the past differences; \(k\): the number of lagged differences
                          • an AR(\(p\)) in levels becomes exactly this regression with \(k = p - 1\) and \(\gamma = -\phi(1)\)
                        • Said and Dickey (1984): with ARMA errors the test stays valid if \(k\) grows slowly with \(T\)
                          • the lags approximate the MA part by a long AR
                        • The ADF test is the default unit-root test in software: adfuller in statsmodels

                        Choosing the number of lags \(k\)

                        Slide 34 in the PDF

                        The lag length is chosen in two steps. First a maximum is set; the rule of Schwert (1989) is \(k_{\max} = 12\,(T/100)^{1/4}\), which for \(T = 105\) gives 12.15, rounded up to 13. Then \(k \le k_{\max}\) is chosen by an information criterion, AIC or BIC (Chapter 2). All the regressions being compared must be estimated on the same sample, the one left after dropping the first \(k_{\max}\) observations; otherwise the values of the criterion are not comparable.

                        The choice has a cost in both directions. With too few lags, the errors remain autocorrelated and the size of the test is wrong; with a negative MA component, the test rejects a true unit root far too often. With too many lags, the test estimates useless parameters and loses power.

                        For series with a negative MA component, Ng and Perron (2001) propose a modified Akaike information criterion (MAIC), which gives a size closer to the nominal one. Combined with the generalised least squares (GLS) detrending of Elliott, Rothenberg and Stock (1996), which increases power, it forms the DF-GLS test. Whatever the method, the result is checked with the Ljung–Box test (Ljung and Box, 1978) on the residuals of the ADF regression, and the report gives \(k\) together with \(\tau\).

                        In brief

                        • Maximum: \(k_{\max} = 12\,(T/100)^{1/4}\) Schwert (1989); for \(T = 105\), \(k_{\max} = 13\)
                          • then choose \(k \le k_{\max}\) by an information criterion, AIC or BIC (Chapter 2), on the same sample for every \(k\)
                        • Too few lags: autocorrelated errors, wrong size (with a negative MA part, far too many rejections)
                          • too many lags: lower power
                        • Ng and Perron (2001): a modified AIC (MAIC) gives a better size; GLS detrending Elliott, Rothenberg and Stock (1996) gives more power
                          • MAIC: modified Akaike information criterion; GLS: generalised least squares; both appear in the DF-GLS test
                        • Check the result: Ljung–Box on the ADF residuals Ljung and Box (1978); report \(k\) together with \(\tau\)

                                Choosing the deterministic terms

                                Slide 37 in the PDF

                                The rule for choosing the deterministic terms is that the alternative hypothesis should be a plausible description of the data. For trending series, such as log GDP and log prices, the plausible alternative is stationarity around a trend, so the regression includes constant and trend. For series that fluctuate around a level, such as interest rates, inflation, exchange rates and returns, the alternative is stationarity around a mean, so the regression includes only the constant.

                                The errors have asymmetric costs. If a needed trend is omitted, a trend-stationary series appears to have a unit root: the regression with constant can describe the slope only through a \(\hat\phi\) close to 1, and the test almost never rejects. If an unnecessary trend is added, the test remains valid but loses power, as the previous chart shows.

                                In practice, the chart of the series is examined first and the regression is chosen afterwards. For ambiguous cases, Dickey and Fuller (1981) propose joint \(F\)-type tests for the unit root and for the trend coefficient.

                                • The alternative must be a plausible description of the data

                                How many differences: \(I(1)\) or \(I(2)\)?

                                Slide 38 in the PDF

                                When a series may be \(I(2)\), the order of the tests matters. Dickey and Pantula (1987) recommend testing from the highest plausible order downwards. Step 1 applies ADF to \(\Delta y_t\); if the test does not reject, \(\Delta y_t\) has a unit root and \(y_t\) may be \(I(2)\). Step 2, only if the first step rejected, applies ADF to \(y_t\): a rejection indicates \(I(0)\), a non-rejection \(I(1)\).

                                The reason for the downward order is that the ADF test on \(y_t\) assumes at most one unit root. If \(y_t \sim I(2)\), the null of the test on levels is also wrong, and the distribution of \(\tau\) is no longer the tabulated one; the result cannot be interpreted.

                                The example is Romanian consumer prices after 2005, from the table of section 4. The log HICP, \(\ln P\), has a unit root. For 12-month inflation \(\pi\), the ADF test has p-value 0.32 and the KPSS statistic is 0.404, so neither test rejects. The change in inflation, \(\Delta\pi\), is clearly stationary. Hence \(\ln P\) is \(I(1)\) or \(I(2)\), depending on how persistent inflation is, a question to which section 7 returns.

                                Phillips–Perron, KPSS and their joint use

                                The ADF test handles autocorrelated errors through extra lags and has the unit root as its null. The section presents two alternatives. The Phillips–Perron test keeps the simple regression and corrects the statistic through an estimate of the long-run variance. The KPSS test reverses the hypotheses: stationarity is the null. Used together, tests with opposite nulls allow a more reliable classification, applied then to a table of real series.

                                The Phillips–Perron test

                                Slide 40 in the PDF

                                Phillips and Perron (1988) handle autocorrelation differently from ADF: they keep the simple Dickey–Fuller regression, without past differences, and correct the statistic \(\tau\) after estimation. The corrected statistic is

                                \[Z_\tau = \sqrt{\frac{\hat\gamma_0}{\hat\lambda^2}}\;\tau \;-\; \frac{(\hat\lambda^2 - \hat\gamma_0)\,T\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\gamma)}{2\hat\lambda\,s},\]

                                where \(\hat\gamma_0\) is the variance of the residuals, \(s^2\) their OLS variance (with the degrees-of-freedom correction) and \(\hat\lambda^2\) their long-run variance.

                                The long-run variance, \(\lambda^2 = \lim_{T\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}\big(\sum_{t=1}^{T} e_t\big)/T\), includes the autocovariances of the residuals \(e_t\) in addition to their variance. It is estimated with Bartlett weights, as in the Newey–West estimator:

                                \[\hat\lambda^2 = \hat\gamma_0 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{L}\Big(1 - \frac{j}{L+1}\Big)\hat\gamma_j,\]

                                where \(\hat\gamma_j\) are the autocovariances of the residuals and \(L\) is the number of autocovariances included, called the bandwidth. If the residuals are white noise, \(\hat\lambda^2 = \hat\gamma_0\), the second term vanishes and \(Z_\tau = \tau\). The correction therefore matters only when the residuals are autocorrelated.

                                The test has the same null and the same critical values as ADF. Its advantages are robustness to heteroskedasticity and the fact that it requires no lag choice, only a bandwidth \(L\). Its weak point, documented by Schwert (1989), is the large size distortion when the errors have a negative MA component: the test then rejects a true unit root far too often.

                                In brief

                                • Phillips and Perron (1988): keep the simple DF regression (no lagged differences) and correct \(\tau\) instead
                                  • \(Z_\tau = \sqrt{\hat\gamma_0/\hat\lambda^2}\;\tau - \dfrac{(\hat\lambda^2 - \hat\gamma_0)\,T\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\gamma)}{2\hat\lambda\,s}\)
                                  • \(\hat\gamma_0\): variance of the residuals; \(s^2\): their OLS variance; \(\hat\lambda^2\): their long-run variance
                                • Long-run variance (Newey–West, Bartlett weights): \(\hat\lambda^2 = \hat\gamma_0 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{L}(1 - \frac{j}{L+1})\hat\gamma_j\)

                                  • if the residuals are white noise, \(\hat\lambda^2 = \hat\gamma_0\) and \(Z_\tau = \tau\): the correction only matters when they are autocorrelated

                                The KPSS test: stationarity as the null

                                Slide 41 in the PDF

                                The ADF and PP tests start from the unit root and ask for evidence against it. Kwiatkowski et al. (1992) proposed the KPSS (Kwiatkowski–Phillips–Schmidt–Shin) test, which reverses the hypotheses. The model decomposes the series into a deterministic trend, a random walk and stationary noise:

                                \[y_t = \xi t + r_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad r_t = r_{t-1} + u_t.\]

                                Here \(\xi t\) is the deterministic trend, \(r_t\) a random walk, a level that wanders, with shocks \(u_t \sim \mathrm{WN}(0, \sigma_u^2)\), and \(\varepsilon_t\) stationary noise. The null is \(H_0\): \(\sigma_u^2 = 0\); the random walk is then a constant, and \(y_t\) is stationary around a level or a trend. The alternative \(H_1\): \(\sigma_u^2 > 0\) means a unit root.

                                The statistic is built from the residuals \(e_t\) of the regression of \(y_t\) on a constant, or on a constant and a trend. With the partial sums \(S_t = e_1 + \dots + e_t\),

                                \[\eta = \frac{1}{T^2\hat\lambda^2}\sum_{t=1}^{T} S_t^2,\]
                                (3.5)

                                where \(\hat\lambda^2\) is the long-run variance of \(e_t\). Stationarity is rejected for large values of \(\eta\). The 5% critical values are 0.463 for the level version and 0.146 for the trend version, and the 1% values are 0.739 and 0.216. KPSS is thus a test of stationarity, not of a unit root: the burden of proof moves from stationarity to the unit root.

                                In brief

                                • Kwiatkowski et al. (1992) (KPSS: Kwiatkowski–Phillips–Schmidt–Shin): \(y_t = \xi t + r_t + \varepsilon_t\), \(r_t = r_{t-1} + u_t\), \(u_t \sim \mathrm{WN}(0, \sigma_u^2)\)
                                  • \(\xi t\): a deterministic trend; \(r_t\): a random walk, the moving level; \(\varepsilon_t\): stationary noise
                                  • \(H_0\): \(\sigma_u^2 = 0\) (the random walk is a constant: \(y_t\) stationary around a level or a trend); \(H_1\): \(\sigma_u^2 > 0\), a unit root
                                • Statistic: regress \(y_t\) on a constant (and a trend); residuals \(e_t\); partial sums \(S_t = e_1 + \dots + e_t\)
                                  • \(\eta = \dfrac{1}{T^2\hat\lambda^2}\sum_{t=1}^{T} S_t^2\), with \(\hat\lambda^2\) the long-run variance of \(e_t\)
                                  • reject stationarity for large \(\eta\): 5% critical values 0.463 (level) and 0.146 (trend); 1%: 0.739 and 0.216
                                • A test of stationarity, not of a unit root: it reverses the burden of proof

                                          Interpreting the partial sums

                                          Slide 43 in the PDF

                                          In the stationary series, deviations from the mean offset each other quickly: a positive value is soon followed by negative ones, and the partial sum \(S_t\) stays close to 0. The sum \(\sum S_t^2\) grows like \(T^2\), like the normalisation in (3.5), so \(\eta\) stays bounded as \(T\) grows.

                                          In the random walk, the series spends long periods on the same side of the mean, and \(S_t\) moves far from 0: on the chart, up to about 300. In this case \(\sum S_t^2\) grows like \(T^4\), the statistic \(\eta\) grows with \(T\), and the test rejects with increasing probability.

                                          The idea is the same as in the cumulative sum (CUSUM) charts of quality control: cumulative deviations from a reference value reveal a level that wanders, even when the individual deviations are small.

                                          In brief

                                          • Stationary series: deviations cancel out, \(S_t\) stays close to 0
                                            • \(\sum S_t^2\) grows like \(T^2\), so \(\eta\) stays bounded
                                          • Random walk: long runs on one side of the mean, \(S_t\) makes large excursions
                                            • \(\sum S_t^2\) grows like \(T^4\): \(\eta\) grows with \(T\) and the test rejects
                                          • The same idea as the CUSUM charts of quality control: cumulated deviations reveal a drifting level

                                          ADF and KPSS together

                                          Slide 44 in the PDF

                                          The ADF and KPSS tests have opposite nulls, so their combined results give four situations. If ADF rejects and KPSS does not, both point to an \(I(0)\) series. If ADF does not reject and KPSS rejects, both point to \(I(1)\). In these two cases agreement is stronger evidence than either test alone, because each test has failed to find evidence for the other's model.

                                          The inconclusive situation arises when neither rejects: the data cannot decide. It is frequent in short samples, where both tests have low power. The decision is then made according to the purpose; for forecasting, \(d = 1\) is the prudent choice, because it produces wider intervals.

                                          The conflict arises when both reject: the series looks neither stationary nor integrated in the simple sense of the tests. The usual causes are a structural break (section 5), long memory, the fractional integration of Chapter 8, or outliers.

                                          Structural breaks

                                          Unit-root tests compare two models: a series stationary around a fixed mean or trend and a series with a stochastic trend. A third possibility is a series stationary around a mean or a trend that changes once, through a structural break. The section shows that such a break makes the usual tests fail to reject the unit root, presents the Zivot–Andrews test, which searches for the break date, and applies it to the flow of the Nile and to the EUR/RON rate.

                                          Perron (1989): a break can look like a unit root

                                          Slide 48 in the PDF

                                          Perron (1989) observed that a series stationary around a mean or a trend that changes once looks highly persistent. The Dickey–Fuller regression does not contain the break and can explain it only through a \(\hat\phi\) close to 1: after the jump, the series does not return to the old mean, which resembles a permanent shock. The test thus loses power, and the unit root is not rejected.

                                          Perron re-examined the Nelson–Plosser series with two breaks treated as known: the 1929 crash, treated as a change in level, and the 1973 oil shock, treated as a change in slope. With break dummies included in the test regression, the unit root was rejected for most series. His interpretation was that most shocks are temporary and that only a few rare events have permanent effects.

                                          The main criticism concerned the procedure: the break dates were chosen after looking at the series. Choosing the date from the same data makes the critical values computed for a date fixed in advance too lenient, and the test rejects too easily.

                                          In brief

                                          • Perron (1989): a stationary series around a mean or a trend that shifts once looks persistent
                                            • the DF regression explains the shift by \(\hat\phi\) close to 1; the test loses power
                                          • He re-examined Nelson and Plosser with two known breaks: the 1929 crash (level) and the 1973 oil shock (slope)
                                            • with dummy variables for the break, the unit root was rejected for most series
                                            • reading: most shocks are transitory; only a few rare events are permanent
                                          • The critique: the break date was chosen after looking at the data

                                              Zivot and Andrews (1992): an unknown break date

                                              Slide 51 in the PDF

                                              Zivot and Andrews (1992) answered the criticism of Perron by letting the data choose the break date \(T_B\). For each candidate date in the central 70% of the sample, the ADF regression is estimated with a break dummy:

                                              \[\begin{aligned} \Delta y_t ={}& c + \theta DU_t + bt + \gamma y_{t-1} \\ &+ \sum_{j}\delta_j\,\Delta y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t. \end{aligned}\]

                                              The dummy variable \(DU_t\) is 0 for \(t \le T_B\), before the break, and 1 for \(t > T_B\), and \(\theta\) measures the size of the level jump. The test statistic is the minimum \(t\)-ratio of \(\gamma\) over all candidate dates, the date most favourable to the stationary alternative. The ends of the sample are excluded so that each regime has enough observations.

                                              The test has three versions: a break in level, as above, a break in slope and a break in both. Because the statistic is a minimum over many dates, its distribution is shifted further to the left than that of \(\tau\), and the 5% critical values are about \(-4.81\) for the level break and \(-5.07\) for the break in both. The null is a unit root without a break, and the alternative a stationary series with one break. For several breaks the method of Bai and Perron (1998) is used. A rejection does not prove that the break is the correct explanation: it only shows that the unit-root model without a break is rejected in favour of the alternative tested.

                                              • Zivot and Andrews (1992): let the data choose the break date \(T_B\)
                                                • for each \(T_B\) in the central 70% of the sample, run the ADF regression with a break dummy:
                                                • \(\Delta y_t = c + \theta DU_t + bt + \gamma y_{t-1} + \sum_j\delta_j\Delta y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t\), \(DU_t = 1\) for \(t > T_B\)

                                                  ARIMA\((p,d,q)\) models

                                                  When a series is integrated, the forecasting model has to contain the differencing. The ARIMA (autoregressive integrated moving average) model combines differencing of order \(d\) with an ARMA\((p,q)\) model for the differenced series. The section defines the model and its special cases, adapts the Box–Jenkins cycle to series with unit roots and applies it to Romanian real GDP: identification, choice of orders by AICc, residual checks and forecasting. The second part derives the forecast-error variance and explains why intervals grow with the horizon, and the last part treats over-differencing.

                                                  The ARIMA model

                                                  Slide 55 in the PDF

                                                  A process \(y_t\) follows an ARIMA\((p,d,q)\) model if its difference of order \(d\), \(\Delta^d y_t\), is a stationary and invertible ARMA\((p,q)\) process:

                                                  \[\phi(L)\,(1 - L)^d\,y_t = c + \theta(L)\,\varepsilon_t.\]
                                                  (3.6)

                                                  In (3.6), \(\varepsilon_t \sim \mathrm{WN}(0, \sigma^2)\) is white noise, \(p\) is the order of the AR part, \(d\) the number of differences, that is the number of unit roots, and \(q\) the order of the MA part. The polynomials \(\phi(L) = 1 - \phi_1 L - \dots - \phi_p L^p\) and \(\theta(L) = 1 + \theta_1 L + \dots + \theta_q L^q\) are those of Chapter 2, with roots outside the unit circle.

                                                  The autoregressive polynomial of the levels is the product \(\phi(z)(1 - z)^d\): it has exactly \(d\) roots on the unit circle, all equal to 1, and the others outside the circle. The name "integrated" comes from the operation inverse to differencing: \(y_t\) is obtained by cumulating an ARMA process \(d\) times.

                                                  The model was popularised by Box and Jenkins in the 1970s, and their methodology is set out in Box, Jenkins and Reinsel (2008). In economic and financial applications \(d \le 2\), and the most frequent case is \(d = 1\).

                                                  In brief

                                                  • Definition: \(y_t \sim\) ARIMA\((p,d,q)\) (autoregressive integrated moving average) if \(\Delta^d y_t\) is a stationary, invertible ARMA\((p,q)\):
                                                    • \(\phi(L)\,(1 - L)^d\,y_t = c + \theta(L)\,\varepsilon_t\), \(\varepsilon_t \sim \mathrm{WN}(0, \sigma^2)\)
                                                    • \(p\): AR order; \(d\): number of differences (unit roots); \(q\): MA order; \(\phi(L)\), \(\theta(L)\) as in Chapter 2
                                                  • The AR polynomial of the levels is \(\phi(z)(1 - z)^d\): \(d\) roots exactly on the unit circle
                                                    • “integrated”: \(y_t\) is a \(d\)-fold cumulative sum of an ARMA process
                                                  • Box, Jenkins and Reinsel (2008) made the model popular; in practice \(d \le 2\), and \(d = 1\) most often

                                                  Special cases and the constant

                                                  Slide 56 in the PDF

                                                  Several well-known models are special cases of (3.6). ARIMA(0,1,0), \(\Delta y_t = c + \varepsilon_t\), is the random walk, with drift \(c\). ARIMA(1,1,0), \(\Delta y_t = c + \phi\Delta y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\), describes growth with momentum: high growth this quarter signals above-average growth in the next. ARIMA(0,1,1), \(\Delta y_t = \varepsilon_t + \theta\varepsilon_{t-1}\), generates the same forecasts as simple exponential smoothing from Chapter 0, with parameter \(\alpha = 1 + \theta\). ARIMA(0,2,2) is the model behind Holt's linear method.

                                                  The constant \(c\) changes the shape of the long-run forecast [Hyndman and Athanasopoulos (2021), ch. 9.7]. For \(d = 0\), forecasts converge to the mean of the process, \(c/\phi(1)\). For \(d = 1\), a non-zero constant is a drift: forecasts follow a line whose slope equals the average growth. Without the constant, long-run forecasts are a horizontal line.

                                                  For \(d = 2\), a non-zero constant means that the second difference has a non-zero mean, so forecasts follow a quadratic trend. Such an extrapolation is rarely reasonable, and in practice \(c = 0\) is used when \(d = 2\).

                                                  In brief

                                                  ModelEquation and meaning
                                                  ARIMA(0,1,0)\(\Delta y_t = c + \varepsilon_t\): random walk (with drift \(c\))

                                                  The Box–Jenkins cycle with unit roots

                                                  Slide 57 in the PDF

                                                  The Box–Jenkins cycle of Chapter 2 is extended by a step for choosing \(d\). Step 1 is the transformation: if the variance grows with the level of the series, the logarithm or a Box–Cox transformation is applied (Chapter 1). Step 2 chooses \(d\) from the chart, the ACF and the ADF and KPSS tests; testing starts with \(\Delta y_t\), and differencing stops at the first stationary difference.

                                                  Step 3 chooses \(p\) and \(q\) from the ACF and the partial autocorrelation function (PACF) of \(\Delta^d y_t\), then compares the candidate models by the AICc criterion, all with the same \(d\). Step 4 estimates the model by maximum likelihood and decides whether to include the constant.

                                                  Step 5 checks the residuals: the ACF, the Ljung–Box test with \(m - p - q\) degrees of freedom and the outliers. Step 6 produces forecasts of the levels, with intervals. If the checks fail, the cycle restarts from step 2 or 3.

                                                  In brief

                                                  • 1. Transform: logarithm or Box–Cox if the variance grows with the level (Chapter 1)
                                                  • 2. Choose \(d\): plot, ACF, ADF and KPSS; test \(\Delta y_t\) first; stop at the first stationary difference
                                                  • 3. Choose \(p\), \(q\): ACF and PACF of \(\Delta^d y_t\) (Chapter 2), then compare candidates by AICc on the same \(d\)
                                                  • 4. Estimate by maximum likelihood; decide on the constant
                                                  • 5. Check the residuals: ACF, Ljung–Box with \(m - p - q\) degrees of freedom, outliers
                                                  • 6. Forecast the levels with intervals; go back to step 2 or 3 if the checks fail

                                                          Forecasting with ARIMA

                                                          Slide 63 in the PDF

                                                          Point forecasts are obtained by writing the model for the levels and applying it recursively, with the observed values up to \(T\) and the future shocks replaced by their mean, \(\hat\varepsilon_{T+j} = 0\) for \(j \ge 1\). For example, ARIMA(1,1,0) without constant, \(\Delta y_t = \phi\Delta y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\), is written in levels as \(y_t = (1 + \phi)y_{t-1} - \phi y_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t\), an AR(2) with one unit root.

                                                          The forecast error at horizon \(h\) is written with the weights \(\psi_j\) of the MA(\(\infty\)) representation of the levels, defined by \(\psi(z) = \theta(z)/[\phi(z)(1 - z)^d]\):

                                                          \[y_{T+h} - \hat y_{T+h} = \sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j\,\varepsilon_{T+h-j},\]

                                                          and its variance is

                                                          \[\mathrm{Var}(y_{T+h} - \hat y_{T+h}) = \sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2.\]
                                                          (3.7)

                                                          The 95% forecast interval is \(\hat y_{T+h} \pm 1.96\,\sigma\sqrt{\sum_{j<h}\psi_j^2}\). For \(d = 0\), the weights \(\psi_j\) go to zero geometrically, and the variance in (3.7) converges to the variance of the process. For \(d \ge 1\), the factor \((1 - z)^{-d}\) prevents the weights from going to zero, and the variance grows without bound with \(h\).

                                                          Two cases have explicit formulas. For the random walk, \(\psi_j = 1\) for every \(j\), the variance is \(h\sigma^2\), and the interval grows like \(\sqrt{h}\). For ARIMA(0,1,1), \(\psi_0 = 1\) and \(\psi_j = 1 + \theta\) for \(j \ge 1\), so the variance is \(\sigma^2[1 + (h - 1)(1 + \theta)^2]\).

                                                          In brief

                                                          • Point forecasts: write the model for the levels and iterate it, with \(\hat\varepsilon_{T+j} = 0\) for \(j \ge 1\)
                                                            • ARIMA(1,1,0): \(y_t = (1 + \phi)y_{t-1} - \phi y_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t\)
                                                          • Forecast error: \(y_{T+h} - \hat y_{T+h} = \sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j\varepsilon_{T+h-j}\), with \(\psi(z) = \theta(z)/[\phi(z)(1 - z)^d]\)
                                                            • variance \(\sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2\); 95% interval \(\hat y_{T+h} \pm 1.96\,\sigma\sqrt{\sum_{j<h}\psi_j^2}\)
                                                            • for \(d \ge 1\) the \(\psi_j\) do not decay to 0: the variance grows without bound
                                                          • Two closed forms
                                                            • random walk: \(\psi_j = 1\), variance \(h\sigma^2\): the interval grows like \(\sqrt{h}\)
                                                            • ARIMA(0,1,1): \(\psi_j = 1 + \theta\) for \(j \ge 1\), variance \(\sigma^2[1 + (h - 1)(1 + \theta)^2]\)

                                                          Worked example: ARIMA(1,1,0) forecasts

                                                          Slide 64 in the PDF

                                                          The example uses the model \(\Delta y_t = 0.6\,\Delta y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\), with \(\sigma = 2\), and the last data \(y_T = 108\) and \(\Delta y_T = 5\). Point forecasts are obtained in two steps: the change is forecast, then added to the previous level. The first forecast change is \(0.6 \cdot 5 = 3\), so \(\hat y_{T+1} = 111.00\). The second is \(0.6 \cdot 3 = 1.8\), so \(\hat y_{T+2} = 112.80\), and the third \(0.6 \cdot 1.8 = 1.08\), so \(\hat y_{T+3} = 113.88\). Momentum makes the forecast changes decline geometrically towards zero, the mean of \(\Delta y_t\) in the absence of a constant.

                                                          The \(\psi\) weights of the levels are the partial sums of the powers of \(\phi\): \(\psi_0 = 1\), \(\psi_1 = 1 + \phi = 1.60\) and \(\psi_2 = 1 + \phi + \phi^2 = 1.96\). From (3.7), the forecast-error variances are 4 at one step, \(4(1 + 1.60^2) = 14.24\) at two steps and \(4(1 + 1.60^2 + 1.96^2) = 29.61\) at three steps. The half-widths of the 95% intervals, \(1.96\) times the square root of the variance, are 3.92, 7.40 and 10.66.

                                                          The interval almost triples in three steps: the ratio is \(10.66/3.92 = 2.72\). For a random walk, the same ratio would be \(\sqrt{3} = 1.73\). Momentum \(\phi = 0.6\) amplifies uncertainty, because today's shock also changes the following growth rates and therefore accumulates in the level with weights larger than 1.

                                                                            Over-differencing

                                                                            Slide 69 in the PDF

                                                                            Over-differencing means differencing a series that is already stationary or trend-stationary. The simplest case shows the effect: if \(\varepsilon_t\) is white noise, the difference \(\Delta\varepsilon_t = \varepsilon_t - \varepsilon_{t-1}\) is an MA(1) with \(\theta = -1\), a process with a unit root in its MA part (Chapter 1). The resulting process is not invertible: it has no AR(\(\infty\)) representation, and its maximum-likelihood estimation is difficult, with estimates piling up at the boundary \(\theta = -1\) (Plosser and Schwert, 1977).

                                                                            The symptoms follow from the same example. The first-order autocorrelation of the differenced series is \(\rho(1) = \theta/(1 + \theta^2) = -0.5\), so a \(\hat\rho(1)\) close to \(-0.5\) is a signal. The variance increases after differencing instead of falling: \(\mathrm{Var}(\Delta\varepsilon_t) = 2\sigma^2\). An estimated MA coefficient close to \(-1\) shows that the extra difference is cancelled by the MA part.

                                                                            The practical rule is simple: difference only as long as the variance of the series falls and the tests call for differencing. The first difference that makes the variance rise is, as a rule, one too many.

                                                                            In brief

                                                                            • Differencing an \(I(0)\) series creates an MA unit root: \(\Delta\varepsilon_t = \varepsilon_t - \varepsilon_{t-1}\), \(\theta = -1\) (Chapter 1)
                                                                              • the result is not invertible: no AR(\(\infty\)) representation, poor estimation Plosser and Schwert (1977)
                                                                            • Symptoms
                                                                              • \(\hat\rho(1)\) of the differenced series close to \(-0.5\)
                                                                              • the variance increases after differencing instead of decreasing
                                                                              • an estimated MA coefficient close to \(-1\)
                                                                            • Rule: difference only as long as the variance goes down and the tests ask for it

                                                                              ARIMA in practice

                                                                              In applications, ARIMA models are often chosen automatically, for many series at once. The section describes the standard automatic selection algorithm and the points at which it must be checked. It then applies the methodology to three problems: the choice of \(d\) for Romanian inflation, the comparison between an ARIMA model and the random walk for the EUR/RON rate, in the tradition of the Meese–Rogoff study, and the forecast of US GDP after the 2008 crisis.

                                                                              Automatic ARIMA

                                                                              Slide 73 in the PDF

                                                                              The algorithm of Hyndman and Khandakar (2008), implemented in the auto.arima function in R and in the Python libraries pmdarima and statsforecast, automates the Box–Jenkins cycle. The order \(d\) is chosen by repeated KPSS tests at 5%: the series is differenced as long as KPSS rejects stationarity. The orders \(p\) and \(q\) are chosen by a stepwise search by AICc, starting from a few initial models and changing \(p\) or \(q\) by one unit at each step, with \(d\) fixed. For \(d \le 1\), the algorithm also decides whether to include a constant or drift.

                                                                              The algorithm discards models whose roots are too close to the unit circle and those whose estimation fails. It is useful when many series must be modelled at once and as a starting point for an individual analysis. Evaluated by its authors on the 3,003 series of the M3 forecasting competition, the method was competitive with the best methods in the competition.

                                                                              Each step of the algorithm contains a choice, however: the test used for \(d\), the criterion, the search space. Whoever uses the result must be able to justify each choice, and the next frame shows where problems can arise.

                                                                              In brief

                                                                              • Hyndman and Khandakar (2008) (auto.arima in R; pmdarima and statsforecast in Python):
                                                                                • \(d\): repeated KPSS tests at 5%: difference while KPSS rejects
                                                                                • \(p\), \(q\): a stepwise search from a few starting models, by AICc, on the chosen \(d\); a constant or drift if \(d \le 1\)
                                                                                • rejects models with roots too close to the unit circle and fits that fail
                                                                              • Useful for many series at once and as a starting point
                                                                                • evaluated by its authors on the 3 003 series of the M3 forecasting competition, it was competitive with the best methods there
                                                                              • But each step embeds a choice that you must be able to defend (next slide)

                                                                              Automatic ARIMA: checks

                                                                              Slide 74 in the PDF

                                                                              The first check concerns the comparability of the criterion. AICc cannot be compared across different values of \(d\): with \(d = 1\), the likelihood is computed for \(\Delta y_t\), a different series from \(y_t\), and the values of the criterion are on different scales. The order \(d\) is therefore chosen by tests and judgement, and AICc compares only models with the same \(d\).

                                                                              The second check concerns roots that nearly cancel. On Romanian GDP with the sample extended back to 1995Q1, the lowest AICc, 607.5, belongs to ARIMA(2,1,2), against 611.1 for ARIMA(0,1,0), and its estimation did not converge. The MA roots have modulus 1.0002 and the AR roots modulus 1.014: the AR and MA polynomials have almost equal roots, which cancel. The second MA coefficient is estimated at 1.000, with standard error 6.96. The model describes the noise of the 1990s data, not the dynamics of the economy.

                                                                              The general rule is to check the output of automatic selection every time. The data are plotted, the convergence of the estimation and the position of the roots are checked, the residual tests are applied, and the forecasts are compared with a simple benchmark, for example the random walk (Chapter 0).

                                                                              • AICc is not comparable across \(d\): with \(d = 1\) the likelihood is computed for \(\Delta y_t\), a different series

                                                                                Case study: Meese and Rogoff (1983)

                                                                                Slide 77 in the PDF

                                                                                The study of Meese and Rogoff (1983) asked a simple question: can exchange-rate models forecast better than a random walk? The authors compared the monetary exchange-rate models of the 1970s, time-series models, ARIMA and vector autoregressions (VAR), and the random walk, on US dollar exchange rates, at horizons of 1–12 months.

                                                                                The result was negative for all models. Out of sample, no model had a lower root mean squared error (RMSE) than the random walk. The result held even when the structural models used the actual future values of the fundamental variables, information that a real forecaster does not have.

                                                                                The study left two legacies. The first is the "Meese–Rogoff puzzle": economic models of the exchange rate do not forecast it better than a model without economics. The second is the use of the random walk as a mandatory benchmark for any exchange-rate forecast. The next frame makes the same comparison for EUR/RON.

                                                                                    Key takeaways

                                                                                    Slide 85 in the PDF

                                                                                    Trending series are either trend-stationary, with shocks that die out, or difference-stationary, with permanent shocks. The treatment differs: in the first case the trend is removed, in the second the series is differenced, and the wrong treatment produces spurious cycles or over-differencing. Regressions between unrelated \(I(1)\) series are spurious: they have large \(t\) and \(R^2\) and a DW statistic close to 0.

                                                                                    ADF and PP test the null of a unit root, with Dickey–Fuller critical values, while KPSS tests the null of stationarity; the two kinds of test are used together. The tests have low power near \(\phi = 1\) and are misled by structural breaks; the Zivot–Andrews test allows for one break at an unknown date.

                                                                                    An ARIMA\((p,d,q)\) model is an ARMA for \(\Delta^d y_t\), and its forecast intervals grow with the horizon when \(d \ge 1\). Automatic selection is a starting point: its output is checked for \(d\), convergence, roots, residuals and the comparison with the random walk.

                                                                                    In brief

                                                                                    • Trending series are TS (shocks fade) or DS (shocks are permanent); the treatment differs: detrend or difference
                                                                                    • Regressions between unrelated \(I(1)\) series are spurious: high \(t\) and \(R^2\), DW near 0
                                                                                    • ADF and PP test \(H_0\): unit root with Dickey–Fuller critical values; KPSS tests \(H_0\): stationarity; use them together
                                                                                    • The tests have low power near \(\phi = 1\) and are misled by breaks; Zivot–Andrews allows one break
                                                                                    • ARIMA\((p,d,q)\) = ARMA on \(\Delta^d y_t\); its forecast intervals grow with the horizon when \(d \ge 1\)
                                                                                    • Automatic selection is a starting point: check \(d\), convergence, roots, residuals and a random-walk benchmark
