The models of Chapters 1–3 describe the conditional mean of a series: the best forecast of tomorrow's value given the past. For daily returns of financial assets this forecast is almost useless, because the direction of tomorrow's move is close to unpredictable. The size of tomorrow's move is a different matter. Large moves arrive in clusters, and a day of turmoil makes another day of turmoil more likely. A model that assumes a constant variance, as white noise does, misses this structure entirely, even when it describes the mean well.

This chapter models the conditional variance. Engle (1982) made yesterday's squared shock part of today's variance, and Bollerslev (1986) added yesterday's variance, which gives the GARCH(1,1) model, still the benchmark of the field. The chapter applies these models to four daily series with very different behaviour: the S&P 500, the BET index of the Bucharest Stock Exchange, the EUR/RON reference rate of the National Bank of Romania (BNR) and Bitcoin. The data run to 18 September 2026.

The variance model changes several practical results. Forecast intervals widen in crises and narrow in calm periods. Risk measures such as the one-day VaR 1% follow the state of the market. The persistence of volatility can be measured in days, through the half-life of a variance shock. On the S&P 500, the GARCH(1,1) model with Student-t innovations gives a half-life of 131 trading days, and the one-day VaR 1% on 18 September 2026 is 1.75%.

Stylised facts of financial returns

Stylised facts are empirical regularities that hold across markets, periods and sampling frequencies. For daily returns, three of them shape the whole chapter: heavy tails, almost no autocorrelation in the returns themselves, and strong, slowly decaying autocorrelation in their squares. The third fact is volatility clustering, and it is the reason for conditional variance models.

The section examines the four series of the chapter, first as time plots, then through summary statistics and autocorrelation functions, and finally through two formal tests for ARCH effects: the Ljung–Box test on squared residuals and the ARCH-LM test of Engle (1982).

    Interpreting the four series

    Slide 7 in the PDF

    The common pattern is that calm years alternate with storms: large changes are followed by large changes, of either sign, and small changes by small changes. Mandelbrot (1963) described this behaviour in speculative prices; it is now called volatility clustering. Clustering concerns the size of the moves, not their direction, so it is invisible to the autocorrelation of the returns themselves.

    The numbers show how large the swings are. The daily standard deviation of the S&P 500 was 0.42% in 2017, a calm year, 3.52% from September 2008 to March 2009, 8.3 times larger, and 3.70% from February to May 2020, 8.8 times larger. The EUR/RON rate behaves differently: its daily standard deviation was 0.78% in the 2008 crisis but only 0.10% in 2020, because the BNR kept the leu almost fixed during the pandemic. The volatility of a managed exchange rate reflects the policy of the central bank as much as the market.

    A model with a constant variance, such as the white noise of Chapter 1, describes none of these series. It would assign the same probability to a 3% move in 2017 and in March 2020.

      Interpreting the two ACFs

      Slide 11 in the PDF

      The returns are close to white noise. Almost all their autocorrelations lie inside the band, so the direction of tomorrow's move is almost unpredictable from past returns. The BET is the visible exception, with \(\hat\rho_1 = 0.11\): in a thinner market prices adjust more slowly to news, which creates a small positive autocorrelation of the kind modelled in Chapter 2.

      The squared returns tell another story. For the S&P 500 the autocorrelation of \(r_t^2\) is 0.31 at lag 1 and still 0.05 at lag 50, and all 50 lags lie outside the band. A large move today makes a large move tomorrow more likely, so the size of tomorrow's move is predictable. Bitcoin shows the same pattern more weakly, with \(\hat\rho_1(r_t^2) = 0.14\), because a few huge days dominate the squares and dilute the correlations.

      Together the two ACFs show that uncorrelated is not the same as independent. A series can have zero autocorrelation at every lag and still be dependent, because a nonlinear function of it, here the square, is autocorrelated. Daily returns are close to white noise but not i.i.d., which is the distinction made in Chapter 1.

      Testing for ARCH effects

      Slide 12 in the PDF

      A series has ARCH effects when its conditional variance depends on the past. The name comes from the ARCH model of Section 3, but the tests do not require that model; they only ask whether past squared shocks predict current squared shocks. Both tests are applied to the residuals \(\hat\varepsilon_t\) of a model for the mean; for returns with a constant mean the residuals are simply \(r_t - \bar r\).

      The first test is the Ljung–Box test applied to the squared residuals, as proposed by McLeod and Li (1983):

      \[Q(m) = T(T+2)\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\hat\rho_k^2(\hat\varepsilon^2)}{T-k},\]

      where \(\hat\rho_k(\hat\varepsilon^2)\) is the sample autocorrelation of the squared residuals at lag \(k\), \(m\) the number of lags and \(T\) the number of observations. Under the null hypothesis of no ARCH effects, \(Q(m)\) is approximately \(\chi^2(m)\). The statistic is the one of Ljung and Box (1978); only the series to which it is applied changes.

      The second is the ARCH-LM test of Engle (1982), a Lagrange multiplier test computed from an auxiliary regression estimated by ordinary least squares (OLS):

      \[\hat\varepsilon_t^2 = b_0 + b_1\hat\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \dots + b_q\hat\varepsilon_{t-q}^2 + u_t, \qquad \mathrm{LM} = nR^2 \sim \chi^2(q) \text{ under } H_0: b_1 = \dots = b_q = 0.\]
      (5.1)

      Here \(q\) is the number of lagged squares, \(R^2\) the coefficient of determination of the regression and \(n\) the number of observations used in it. If past squares have no predictive power, \(R^2\) is close to zero and \(nR^2\) is small. The same two tests are run again on the standardised residuals after a volatility model has been fitted; then they check whether the model has removed the ARCH effects.

      In brief

      • ARCH effects: the conditional variance depends on the past (the name comes from the ARCH model, Section 3)
      • Ljung–Box on squares (the test of McLeod and Li (1983)): \(Q(m) = T(T+2)\sum_{k=1}^{m}\hat\rho_k^2(\hat\varepsilon^2)/(T-k)\)
        • \(\hat\rho_k(\hat\varepsilon^2)\): the sample autocorrelation of the squared residuals at lag \(k\); \(m\): the number of lags; \(T\): the number of observations
        • \(\hat\varepsilon_t\): residuals of the mean model (for returns, \(r_t - \bar r\)); under \(H_0\) (no ARCH effects), \(Q(m) \sim \chi^2(m)\)
      • ARCH-LM test Engle (1982): LM (Lagrange multiplier) test from the auxiliary regression
        • \(\hat\varepsilon_t^2 = b_0 + b_1\hat\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \dots + b_q\hat\varepsilon_{t-q}^2 + u_t\), estimated by OLS (ordinary least squares)
        • \(b_0, \dots, b_q\): the coefficients; \(q\): the number of lagged squares; \(R^2\): the coefficient of determination of this regression
        • \(H_0\): \(b_1 = \dots = b_q = 0\); \(\mathrm{LM} = n R^2 \sim \chi^2(q)\), \(n\) = number of observations in the regression
      • Both tests are run on the residuals of the mean model, before and after fitting a volatility model

      Worked example: ARCH-LM on the BET

      Slide 13 in the PDF

      The example applies (5.1) to the BET. The mean model is an AR(1), the order chosen in Chapter 2, with estimated coefficient \(\hat\phi = 0.109\). Its residuals \(\hat\varepsilon_t\) are squared, and the squares are regressed on their own five lags, so \(q = 5\).

      The estimated regression is \(\hat\varepsilon_t^2 = 0.862 + 0.276\,\hat\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + 0.099\,\hat\varepsilon_{t-2}^2 + 0.088\,\hat\varepsilon_{t-3}^2 - 0.015\,\hat\varepsilon_{t-4}^2 + 0.102\,\hat\varepsilon_{t-5}^2\), with \(R^2 = 0.153\) on \(n = 6\,664\) observations. The test statistic is \(\mathrm{LM} = nR^2 = 6\,664 \times 0.153 = 1017\). The 5% critical value of the \(\chi^2(5)\) distribution is 11.07, so the hypothesis of no ARCH effects is rejected by a very wide margin: the variance of the BET depends on the last five squared shocks.

      An \(R^2\) of 0.153 would be modest for a regression in levels, but squared returns are extremely noisy measures of the variance, so even a perfect variance model explains only a small part of their variation. In this context an \(R^2\) of 0.15 is a strong signal. The coefficient on the first lag, 0.276, is the largest, and the coefficients decline with the lag, the pattern that the ARCH and GARCH models describe.

      In brief

      • Mean model: AR(1) for the BET returns (Chapter 2), \(\hat\phi = 0.109\); residuals \(\hat\varepsilon_t\); \(q = 5\)
      • Auxiliary regression (OLS)
        • \(\hat\varepsilon_t^2 = 0.862 + 0.276\,\hat\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + 0.099\,\hat\varepsilon_{t-2}^2 + 0.088\,\hat\varepsilon_{t-3}^2 + (\ensuremath{-}0.015)\,\hat\varepsilon_{t-4}^2 + 0.102\,\hat\varepsilon_{t-5}^2\)
        • \(R^2 = 0.153\), \(n = 6\,664\)
      • Step by step: \(\mathrm{LM} = n R^2 = 6\,664 \times 0.153 = 1017\); 5% critical value \(\chi^2_{0.95}(5) = 11.07\)
        • \(1017 \gg 11.07\): reject \(H_0\); the variance of the BET depends on the last five squared shocks
      • Interpretation: \(R^2 = 0.153\) is small, but for squared returns it is a strong signal; the model of the variance is the next step

      Section summary

      The section has established what a model for daily returns must reproduce. The mean is almost unpredictable, but the variance changes over time and is predictable from the recent past: a large move today signals a high variance tomorrow. The tails are much heavier than those of the Normal distribution. On all four markets the tests for ARCH effects reject the constant-variance hypothesis by a margin of hundreds or thousands of units over the critical value, so the next step is a model in which the variance itself depends on the past.

      Conditional mean and conditional variance

      Before writing a volatility model, the chapter needs a precise language for "the variance given the past". This section defines the information set, the conditional mean and the conditional variance, and the decomposition \(r_t = \mu_t + \sigma_t z_t\) on which every model of the chapter rests. It then shows, with a two-state example and with the residuals of an ARMA model for the BET, why a correct model of the mean can still leave strong dependence in the squares.

      Conditioning on the past

      Slide 15 in the PDF

      Let \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\) denote the information available at the end of day \(t-1\), that is, all past values of the series. The conditional expectation \(E[X \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\) is the best forecast of a random variable \(X\), in the mean-squared-error sense, given that information, as in Chapter 2. Applied to the return, it defines the conditional mean \(\mu_t = E[r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\). An ARMA model is a model for \(\mu_t\); for daily returns \(\mu_t\) is small and almost constant.

      The conditional variance is the expected squared deviation from the conditional mean, given the past:

      \[\sigma_t^2 = \mathrm{Var}(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}) = E\big[(r_t - \mu_t)^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big].\]

      Its square root \(\sigma_t\) is the conditional volatility. It is known at the end of day \(t-1\) and changes from one day to the next. A series whose conditional variance is not constant is called conditionally heteroskedastic.

      The unconditional variance \(\mathrm{Var}(r_t)\) is a different object: a single number for the whole sample, which ignores the information in the past. A stationary process can have a constant unconditional variance while its conditional variance moves every day. The stylised facts concern exactly this case.

      In brief

      • \(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\): the information available at the end of day \(t-1\) (all past values)
        • conditional expectation \(E[X \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\): the best forecast of \(X\) given the past (Chapter 2)
      • Conditional mean: \(\mu_t = E[r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\)
        • an ARMA model (Chapter 2) is a model for \(\mu_t\); for daily returns \(\mu_t\) is small and almost constant
      • Conditional variance: \(\sigma_t^2 = \mathrm{Var}(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}) = E[(r_t - \mu_t)^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\)
        • \(\sigma_t\) is the conditional volatility: known at \(t-1\), it changes from day to day
        • conditional heteroskedasticity: a conditional variance that is not constant
      • Unconditional variance \(\mathrm{Var}(r_t)\): one number for the whole sample; it can be constant while \(\sigma_t^2\) moves

      The basic decomposition

      Slide 16 in the PDF

      Every model of the chapter writes the return as a conditional mean plus a shock, and the shock as a conditional volatility times an innovation:

      \[r_t = \mu_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t.\]
      (5.2)

      The innovations \(z_t\) are i.i.d. with mean 0 and variance 1; their distribution can be Normal, Student-t or another standardised law. The shock \(\varepsilon_t\) is the unpredictable part of the return. Both \(\mu_t\) and \(\sigma_t\) depend only on the past, so they are known at \(t-1\), while \(z_t\) is new on day \(t\).

      Three consequences follow. First, \(E[\varepsilon_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = \sigma_t E[z_t] = 0\): the shocks form a martingale difference sequence, and a martingale difference with finite variance is uncorrelated, hence white noise. Second, \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}) = \sigma_t^2 E[z_t^2] = \sigma_t^2\), so the variance of tomorrow's shock is predictable. Third, the shocks are uncorrelated but not independent: when \(\sigma_t^2\) depends on \(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2\), the squares \(\varepsilon_t^2\) and \(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2\) are correlated.

      The decomposition separates the two modelling tasks. An ARMA model, as in Chapter 2, describes \(\mu_t\); a GARCH model, the subject of this chapter, describes \(\sigma_t^2\). Estimated together, they form an ARMA-GARCH model.

      In brief

      • Every model of this chapter: \(r_t = \mu_t + \varepsilon_t\),   \(\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t\)
        • \(z_t\): the innovations, i.i.d. with mean 0 and variance 1 (Normal, Student-t, …)
        • \(\varepsilon_t\): the shock; \(\mu_t\) and \(\sigma_t\) depend only on the past
      • Consequences
        • \(E[\varepsilon_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = \sigma_t E[z_t] = 0\): the shocks are a martingale difference, hence uncorrelated (white noise)
        • \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}) = \sigma_t^2\): the variance is predictable
        • the \(\varepsilon_t\) are uncorrelated but not independent: \(\varepsilon_t^2\) is correlated with \(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2\)
      • Two models, one for each moment: ARMA for \(\mu_t\) (Chapter 2), GARCH for \(\sigma_t^2\) (this chapter); together: ARMA-GARCH

      Worked example: conditional and unconditional variance

      Slide 17 in the PDF

      Consider a market with calm days, on which \(\sigma_t^2 = 0.5\), and stormy days, on which \(\sigma_t^2 = 4.5\), each with probability 1/2, and with mean zero. The law of total variance splits the unconditional variance into the average conditional variance and the variance of the conditional mean:

      \[\mathrm{Var}(r_t) = E\big[\mathrm{Var}(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1})\big] + \mathrm{Var}\big(E[r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\big).\]

      Here the second term is zero, so \(\mathrm{Var}(r_t) = 0.5 \times 0.5 + 0.5 \times 4.5 = 2.50\), and the unconditional standard deviation is \(\sqrt{2.5} = 1.58\%\).

      The unconditional figure is an average that describes no actual day. A calm day has \(\sigma_t = \sqrt{0.5} = 0.71\%\) and a stormy day \(\sigma_t = \sqrt{4.5} = 2.12\%\). The conditional variance tells which kind of day we are in; the unconditional variance does not. A risk manager using 1.58% would overstate the risk on calm days and understate it on stormy days.

      The mixture has another consequence. With Normal \(z_t\), the kurtosis of \(r_t\) equals \(3E[\sigma_t^4]/(E[\sigma_t^2])^2\). Here \(E[\sigma_t^4] = 0.5 \times 0.25 + 0.5 \times 20.25 = 10.25\) and \((E[\sigma_t^2])^2 = 6.25\), so the kurtosis is \(3 \times 10.25/6.25 = 4.92\). A time-varying variance alone creates heavy tails, even when every day's distribution is Normal. Part of the kurtosis in the table of the previous section is explained this way.

      In brief

      • A market has calm days (\(\sigma_t^2 = 0.5\)) and stormy days (\(\sigma_t^2 = 4.5\)), each with probability \(1/2\); the mean is 0
        • law of total variance: \(\mathrm{Var}(r_t) = E[\mathrm{Var}(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1})] + \mathrm{Var}(E[r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}])\)
        • step by step: \(0.5 \times 0.5 + 0.5 \times 4.5 + 0 = 2.50\); unconditional standard deviation \(1.58\%\)
      • The unconditional variance is an average; the conditional variance tells which day we are in
        • a calm day: \(\sigma_t = 0.71\%\); a stormy day: \(\sigma_t = 2.12\%\); the average \(1.58\%\) fits neither
      • With Normal \(z_t\), the mixture has kurtosis \(3E[\sigma_t^4]/(E[\sigma_t^2])^2 = 4.92\): a time-varying variance creates heavy tails

        The ARCH model

        The first model for the conditional variance makes today's variance a linear function of yesterday's squared shock. This section presents the ARCH(1) model of Engle (1982), derives its unconditional variance and kurtosis, compares simulated ARCH paths with i.i.d. and GARCH paths, and shows on the S&P 500 why ARCH needs many lags to fit real data.

        ARCH(1): definition

        Slide 22 in the PDF

        The ARCH(1) model specifies the variance of the shock as a function of yesterday's squared shock:

        \[\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t, \qquad \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2, \qquad \omega > 0,\ \alpha \ge 0.\]
        (5.3)

        The constant \(\omega\) is the minimum level of the variance, reached after a zero shock. The coefficient \(\alpha\) measures how strongly the variance reacts to yesterday's squared shock. The restrictions \(\omega > 0\) and \(\alpha \ge 0\) guarantee a positive variance. Because only the square of the shock enters, a large shock of either sign raises today's variance.

        The word "autoregressive" refers to the squared shocks. Write \(\varepsilon_t^2 = \sigma_t^2 + v_t\), with \(v_t = \sigma_t^2(z_t^2 - 1)\). Since \(E[z_t^2] = 1\), \(E[v_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0\), so \(v_t\) is a martingale difference. Substituting the variance equation gives

        \[\varepsilon_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + v_t,\]

        an AR(1) model for \(\varepsilon_t^2\), as in Chapter 2, with white-noise errors \(v_t\).

        The AR(1) form gives the autocorrelations of the squares directly: when the fourth moment exists, \(\rho_k(\varepsilon_t^2) = \alpha^k\). ARCH(1) therefore produces clustering, but the correlation of the squares decays geometrically at rate \(\alpha\). For moderate values of \(\alpha\) this decay is much faster than the slow decay seen in real squared returns.

        In brief

        • \(\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t\),   \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2\),   \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha \ge 0\)
          • \(\omega\): the minimum level of the variance; \(\alpha\): how strongly the variance reacts to yesterday's squared shock
          • \(\omega > 0\) and \(\alpha \ge 0\) keep the variance positive
          • a large shock yesterday, of either sign, raises today's variance
        • “Autoregressive”: \(\varepsilon_t^2\) follows an AR(1) model (Chapter 2)
          • write \(\varepsilon_t^2 = \sigma_t^2 + v_t\), with \(v_t = \sigma_t^2(z_t^2 - 1)\) and \(E[v_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0\)
          • then \(\varepsilon_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + v_t\): an AR(1) for the squared shocks, with white-noise errors \(v_t\)
        • Hence the ACF of \(\varepsilon_t^2\) is \(\rho_k = \alpha^k\) (when the fourth moment exists): clustering, but a fast decay

        ARCH(1): properties

        Slide 23 in the PDF

        The shocks of an ARCH(1) have mean zero and are uncorrelated at every lag \(k \ne 0\), so they are white noise. When \(\alpha < 1\) the process is stationary, and its unconditional variance follows from taking expectations in the variance equation: \(\bar\sigma^2 = E[\varepsilon_t^2] = \omega + \alpha\bar\sigma^2\), so

        \[\bar\sigma^2 = \frac{\omega}{1-\alpha}.\]

        With Normal innovations and \(3\alpha^2 < 1\), the kurtosis of the shocks is

        \[K = 3\,\frac{1-\alpha^2}{1-3\alpha^2} > 3.\]

        The innovations are Normal, yet the shocks have heavy tails, for the same reason as in the two-state example: a mixture of Normal distributions with different variances. If \(\alpha \ge 1/\sqrt{3} = 0.577\), the fourth moment is infinite, although the variance is still finite as long as \(\alpha < 1\).

        In the worked example \(\omega = 0.5\) and \(\alpha = 0.5\). The unconditional variance is \(0.5/0.5 = 1.00\), and the kurtosis is \(3 \times 0.75/0.25 = 9.0\). After a shock \(\varepsilon_{t-1} = 2\), two unconditional standard deviations, the next variance is \(\sigma_t^2 = 0.5 + 0.5 \times 4 = 2.50\), so \(\sigma_t = 1.58\): the variance more than doubles relative to its average level of 1.

        In brief

        • \(E[\varepsilon_t] = 0\) and \(\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon_t, \varepsilon_{t-k}) = 0\) for \(k \ne 0\): white noise
        • Unconditional variance (stationary if \(\alpha < 1\)): take expectations in \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\varepsilon_{t-1}^2\)
          • \(\bar\sigma^2 = E[\varepsilon_t^2] = \omega + \alpha\bar\sigma^2\), so \(\bar\sigma^2 = \omega/(1 - \alpha)\)
        • Kurtosis with Normal \(z_t\) (if \(3\alpha^2 < 1\)): \(K = 3\,\dfrac{1 - \alpha^2}{1 - 3\alpha^2} > 3\)
          • Normal innovations, yet heavy-tailed shocks; if \(\alpha \ge 1/\sqrt{3} = 0.577\), the fourth moment is infinite
        • Worked example: \(\omega = 0.5\), \(\alpha = 0.5\)
          • \(\bar\sigma^2 = 0.5/0.5 = 1.00\); \(K = 3 \times 0.75/0.25 = 9.0\)
          • after a shock \(\varepsilon_{t-1} = 2\): \(\sigma_t^2 = 0.5 + 0.5 \times 4 = 2.50\), \(\sigma_t = 1.58\): the variance more than doubles

          The GARCH(1,1) model

          GARCH(1,1) adds one term to ARCH(1), yesterday's conditional variance, and thereby turns a one-day memory into a geometrically decaying memory of all past shocks. This section defines the model, writes it as an ARMA(1,1) for the squared shocks, derives the long-run variance, the kurtosis and the half-life of a shock, and works through a one-step update. It then treats the boundary case \(\alpha + \beta = 1\), the IGARCH model, and its relation to the EWMA estimator used in industry, and compares GARCH, EWMA and a rolling window during the COVID-19 crash.

          GARCH(1,1): definition

          Slide 28 in the PDF

          The GARCH(1,1) model is

          \[\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t, \qquad \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2, \qquad \omega > 0,\ \alpha \ge 0,\ \beta \ge 0.\]
          (5.4)

          The general GARCH(\(p\),\(q\)) adds more lags of both kinds, but in practice the (1,1) specification is almost always enough for daily returns. The parameters have a direct reading. The coefficient \(\alpha\) is the reaction of the variance to yesterday's news, the squared shock. The coefficient \(\beta\) is the memory of the variance, the weight on yesterday's level. Their sum \(\alpha + \beta\) is the persistence, and \(\omega\) sets, together with the persistence, the long-run level.

          Substituting the equation into itself repeatedly gives

          \[\sigma_t^2 = \frac{\omega}{1-\beta} + \alpha\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\beta^j\,\varepsilon_{t-1-j}^2.\]

          GARCH(1,1) is therefore an ARCH(\(\infty\)) with geometrically declining weights \(\alpha\beta^j\). All past shocks matter, and the weights decay at rate \(\beta\), slowly when \(\beta\) is close to 1. This is how three parameters in the variance equation reproduce the slow decay of the ACF of squared returns.

          In brief

          • \(\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t\),   \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2\),   \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha \ge 0\), \(\beta \ge 0\)
            • GARCH(\(p\),\(q\)) adds more lags of both kinds; in practice (1,1) is almost always enough
          • Reading the parameters
            • \(\alpha\): the reaction to yesterday's news; \(\beta\): the memory of the variance
            • \(\alpha + \beta\): the persistence; \(\omega\) sets the long-run level
          • By recursion: an ARCH(\(\infty\)) with geometric weights
            • \(\sigma_t^2 = \dfrac{\omega}{1 - \beta} + \alpha\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\beta^j\,\varepsilon_{t-1-j}^2\): all past shocks matter, with weights that decay like \(\beta^j\)

          GARCH(1,1) as an ARMA(1,1) for the squared shocks

          Slide 29 in the PDF

          As for ARCH, write \(\varepsilon_t^2 = \sigma_t^2 + v_t\), with \(v_t\) a martingale difference, and substitute \(\sigma_t^2 = \varepsilon_t^2 - v_t\) and \(\sigma_{t-1}^2 = \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 - v_{t-1}\) into (5.4):

          \[\varepsilon_t^2 = \omega + (\alpha+\beta)\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + v_t - \beta\,v_{t-1}.\]
          (5.5)

          This is an ARMA(1,1) model for \(\varepsilon_t^2\), as in Chapter 2, with autoregressive coefficient \(\alpha + \beta\) and moving-average coefficient \(-\beta\).

          The ARMA form transfers the results of Chapter 2. The process is covariance stationary if \(\alpha + \beta < 1\), that is, if the AR root lies outside the unit circle. The ACF of \(\varepsilon_t^2\) decays like \((\alpha + \beta)^k\), slowly when the persistence is close to 1, which matches the ACF of real squared returns. In principle the model could be identified from the ACF and the partial autocorrelation function (PACF) of \(\varepsilon_t^2\), but the errors \(v_t\) are strongly heteroskedastic and heavy-tailed, so in practice GARCH models are estimated by maximum likelihood, the subject of Section 5.

          In brief

          • As for ARCH, write \(\varepsilon_t^2 = \sigma_t^2 + v_t\) and substitute \(\sigma_t^2 = \varepsilon_t^2 - v_t\) in the equation
            • \(\varepsilon_t^2 = \omega + (\alpha + \beta)\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + v_t - \beta\,v_{t-1}\)
            • an ARMA(1,1) (Chapter 2) with AR coefficient \(\alpha + \beta\) and MA coefficient \(-\beta\)
          • Consequences
            • covariance stationary if \(\alpha + \beta < 1\) (the AR root outside the unit circle)
            • the ACF of \(\varepsilon_t^2\) decays like \((\alpha + \beta)^k\): slow when the persistence is close to 1, as in the ACF of real squared returns
            • identification by the ACF and the PACF (partial autocorrelation function) of \(\varepsilon_t^2\) is possible in principle, but in practice we use maximum likelihood (Section 5)

          Long-run variance, kurtosis, persistence and half-life

          Slide 30 in the PDF

          When \(\alpha + \beta < 1\), taking expectations in (5.4) gives the long-run, or unconditional, variance

          \[\bar\sigma^2 = \frac{\omega}{1-\alpha-\beta}.\]

          For daily returns it is reported as an annualised volatility, \(\sqrt{252\,\bar\sigma^2}\), with 252 trading days per year. With Normal innovations, the kurtosis is \(K = 3\,[1-(\alpha+\beta)^2]/[1-(\alpha+\beta)^2-2\alpha^2]\), which exceeds 3; for \(\alpha = 0.10\) and \(\beta = 0.88\) it equals 6.06.

          The persistence governs how fast the variance returns to \(\bar\sigma^2\). The forecast derived in Section 9 shows that a deviation from the long-run variance shrinks by the factor \(\alpha + \beta\) each day: \(E_t[\sigma_{t+h}^2] - \bar\sigma^2 = (\alpha+\beta)^{h-1}(\sigma_{t+1}^2 - \bar\sigma^2)\), where \(E_t\) is the expectation given the information of day \(t\) and \(\sigma_{t+1}^2\) is tomorrow's variance, already known at \(t\). The half-life is the number of days after which half of a variance shock is gone:

          \[h_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 0.5}{\ln(\alpha+\beta)}.\]
          (5.6)

          The half-life is very sensitive to the persistence near 1. With \(\alpha + \beta = 0.90\) it is 6.6 days, with 0.98 it is 34.3 days, and with 0.99 it is 69.0 days. A change of 0.01 in the persistence doubles the duration of a shock, which is why the persistence estimates of the four markets in Section 6 translate into very different half-lives.

          In brief

          • Long-run (unconditional) variance: \(\bar\sigma^2 = \omega/(1 - \alpha - \beta)\); annualised volatility \(\sqrt{252\,\bar\sigma^2}\) for 252 trading days
            • kurtosis with Normal \(z_t\): \(K = 3\,\dfrac{1 - (\alpha + \beta)^2}{1 - (\alpha + \beta)^2 - 2\alpha^2} > 3\); \(\alpha = 0.10\), \(\beta = 0.88\): \(K = 6.06\)
          • A deviation from \(\bar\sigma^2\) shrinks by the factor \(\alpha + \beta\) each day (Section 9): \(E_t[\sigma_{t+h}^2] - \bar\sigma^2 = (\alpha + \beta)^{h-1}(\sigma_{t+1}^2 - \bar\sigma^2)\)
            • \(E_t[\cdot]\): the expectation given the information of day \(t\); \(\sigma_{t+1}^2\): tomorrow's variance, already known at \(t\)
            • half-life: the number of days after which half of a variance shock is gone, \(h_{1/2} = \ln 0.5/\ln(\alpha + \beta)\)
            • \(\alpha + \beta = 0.90\): 6.6 days; \(0.98\): 34.3 days; \(0.99\): 69.0 days: small changes near 1 matter a lot

          Worked example: one GARCH(1,1) step

          IGARCH and the link with EWMA

          Slide 32 in the PDF

          The boundary case \(\alpha + \beta = 1\) is the integrated GARCH (IGARCH) model of Engle and Bollerslev (1986). In the ARMA(1,1) form (5.5) the AR coefficient equals 1, so \(\varepsilon_t^2\) has a unit root, as in Chapter 3. Shocks to the variance never die out: the forecast of the variance does not return to any long-run level, the half-life is infinite and the unconditional variance is not finite.

          The exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) of the RiskMetrics methodology (J.P. Morgan/Reuters, 1996) is

          \[\sigma_t^2 = \lambda\sigma_{t-1}^2 + (1-\lambda)\,r_{t-1}^2, \qquad \lambda \in (0,1).\]

          The parameter \(\lambda\) is the weight of yesterday's variance and \(1 - \lambda\) the weight of yesterday's squared return. EWMA is simple exponential smoothing, from Chapter 0, applied to \(r_t^2\) with smoothing constant \(1 - \lambda\). It is also an IGARCH with \(\omega = 0\), \(\alpha = 1 - \lambda\) and \(\beta = \lambda\). RiskMetrics uses \(\lambda = 0.94\) for daily data, which gives the weights a half-life of \(\ln 0.5/\ln 0.94 = 11.2\) days.

          The question on the slide makes the difference concrete. Right after a crash, an analyst forecasts the volatility one year ahead with EWMA. Because EWMA has no long-run level, its forecast for every future horizon equals today's crisis level, so the one-year forecast stays at the crisis level. A stationary GARCH lets the forecast decay towards the long-run level at the rate \(\alpha + \beta\). The two estimators can give similar day-to-day volatilities and very different long-horizon forecasts.

          In brief

          • IGARCH (integrated GARCH) Engle and Bollerslev (1986): \(\alpha + \beta = 1\): a unit root in the ARMA(1,1) for \(\varepsilon_t^2\) (Chapter 3)
            • shocks to the variance never die out: no half-life, no finite unconditional variance
          • EWMA (exponentially weighted moving average) of J.P. Morgan/Reuters (1996): \(\sigma_t^2 = \lambda\sigma_{t-1}^2 + (1 - \lambda)r_{t-1}^2\)
            • \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\): the weight of yesterday's variance; \(1 - \lambda\): the weight of yesterday's squared return
            • simple exponential smoothing (Chapter 0) applied to \(r_t^2\), with smoothing constant \(1 - \lambda\)
            • an IGARCH with \(\omega = 0\), \(\alpha = 1 - \lambda\), \(\beta = \lambda\); RiskMetrics uses \(\lambda = 0.94\) for daily data (half-life of the weights 11.2 days)
          • Question for the room: right after a crash, an analyst forecasts the volatility one year ahead with EWMA. What goes wrong?
            • Answer: EWMA keeps today's crisis level for the whole year; GARCH lets it decay towards the long-run level

            Section summary

            GARCH(1,1) describes volatility with three numbers: how much the variance reacts to news, how much it remembers, and the level to which it returns. Its ARMA(1,1) form explains the slow decay of the autocorrelations of squared returns, and the persistence \(\alpha + \beta\) converts directly into a half-life: 34.3 days for a persistence of 0.98. The boundary case \(\alpha + \beta = 1\) links GARCH to the EWMA of RiskMetrics, which never returns to a long-run level. During the COVID-19 crash, GARCH reacted fastest and faded fastest, while a 63-day rolling window peaked two months late. The parameters still have to be estimated, which is the subject of the next section.

            Estimation by maximum likelihood

            The variance \(\sigma_t^2\) is not observed, so GARCH parameters cannot be estimated by regressing anything on anything. They are estimated by maximum likelihood, building the likelihood from the conditional densities implied by (5.2). This section writes the likelihood, shows its shape on simulated ARCH and GARCH data, estimates the S&P 500 model both step by step and with the arch package, explains quasi-maximum likelihood and robust standard errors, and replaces the Normal distribution of the innovations with the Student-t and skewed-t distributions.

            The likelihood of a GARCH model

            Slide 36 in the PDF

            Returns are dependent through their variance, so their joint density is not the product of the marginal densities. It is a product of conditional densities:

            \[f(r_1, \dots, r_T) = f(r_1)\prod_{t=2}^{T} f(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}).\]

            With Normal innovations and a constant mean \(\mu\), each conditional density is Normal, \(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1} \sim N(\mu, \sigma_t^2)\). Ignoring the first observation gives the conditional log-likelihood of the parameter vector \(\theta = (\mu, \omega, \alpha, \beta)\):

            \[\ell(\theta) = -\frac12\sum_{t=2}^{T}\Big[\ln(2\pi) + \ln\sigma_t^2(\theta) + \frac{(r_t-\mu)^2}{\sigma_t^2(\theta)}\Big].\]
            (5.7)

            The variance \(\sigma_t^2(\theta)\) is computed recursively from the GARCH equation, starting from a value \(\sigma_1^2\) such as the sample variance. Each term of the sum rewards a small variance through \(\ln\sigma_t^2\) and penalises a variance that is too small for the observed squared deviation through \((r_t-\mu)^2/\sigma_t^2\). The likelihood is maximised by a variance path that follows the squared returns without overreacting to them.

            The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) is \(\hat\theta = \arg\max_\theta \ell(\theta)\), subject to \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha, \beta \ge 0\) and \(\alpha + \beta < 1\). There is no closed form, so a numerical optimiser searches for the maximum. OLS is not possible, because the dependent variable of the variance equation, \(\sigma_t^2\), is not observed.

            In brief

            • The returns are dependent, so the joint density is a product of conditional densities
              • \(f(r_1, \dots, r_T) = f(r_1)\prod_{t=2}^{T} f(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1})\), with \(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1} \sim N(\mu, \sigma_t^2)\) for Normal innovations
            • Conditional log-likelihood, \(\theta = (\mu, \omega, \alpha, \beta)\):
              • \(\ell(\theta) = -\dfrac12\sum_{t=2}^{T}\Big[\ln(2\pi) + \ln\sigma_t^2(\theta) + \dfrac{(r_t - \mu)^2}{\sigma_t^2(\theta)}\Big]\)
              • \(\sigma_t^2(\theta)\) is computed recursively from a starting value \(\sigma_1^2\) (for example the sample variance)
            • MLE (maximum likelihood estimation): \(\hat\theta = \arg\max_\theta \ell(\theta)\), under \(\omega > 0\), \(\alpha, \beta \ge 0\), \(\alpha + \beta < 1\)
              • no closed form: a numerical optimiser searches for the maximum; OLS is not possible, since \(\sigma_t^2\) is not observed

                Quasi-maximum likelihood and practical rules

                Slide 40 in the PDF

                Quasi-maximum likelihood estimation (QMLE) maximises the Normal likelihood (5.7) even when the innovations are not Normal. Bollerslev and Wooldridge (1992) showed that the estimates remain consistent provided the conditional mean \(\mu_t\) and the conditional variance \(\sigma_t^2\) are correctly specified. Only the inference changes: the classic standard errors are invalid, and the robust "sandwich" standard errors, which combine the Hessian with the outer product of the scores, must be used. QMLE is the reason why the Normal GARCH remains useful for estimating volatility even though returns are heavy-tailed.

                Several practical rules prevent common failures. Returns should be in percent, not in decimals: in decimals \(\omega\) is tiny, the optimiser fails to move it, and arch warns about badly scaled data. Series with very small moves need extra scaling: the EUR/RON rate moves about 0.3% a day, so the model is estimated on \(10\,r_t\) and \(\omega\) is converted back by dividing by 100. The sample should contain at least 1000–2000 daily observations, and the convergence flag of the optimiser should always be checked.

                An estimate on the boundary of the parameter space, such as \(\hat\alpha + \hat\beta = 1\) or \(\hat\alpha = 0\), has no usual standard error, because the asymptotic theory assumes an interior point. Such estimates should be reported as boundary estimates, not with a \(t\) statistic.

                In brief

                • QMLE (quasi-maximum likelihood estimation): maximise the Normal likelihood even if \(z_t\) is not Normal

                • Practical rules
                  • returns in % (not decimals): the optimiser fails when \(\omega\) is tiny; arch warns about badly scaled data

                Student-t and skewed-t innovations

                Slide 41 in the PDF

                A GARCH model with Normal innovations explains only part of the kurtosis. The S&P 500 returns have kurtosis 13.7, and the standardised residuals \(\hat z_t = \hat\varepsilon_t/\hat\sigma_t\) of the Normal GARCH still have 4.7. The varying variance accounts for most of the heavy tails, but the remaining excess must come from the distribution of \(z_t\) itself.

                Bollerslev (1987) proposed the standardised Student-t distribution with \(\nu > 2\) degrees of freedom. If \(t_\nu\) is a Student variable, then \(z_t = t_\nu\sqrt{(\nu-2)/\nu}\) has variance 1, as the decomposition requires. Its kurtosis is \(3 + 6/(\nu-4)\) for \(\nu > 4\), and as \(\nu \to \infty\) it converges to the Normal distribution. The degrees of freedom \(\nu\) are estimated together with \((\mu, \omega, \alpha, \beta)\), with dist='t' in arch.

                The skewed t of Hansen (1994) adds a parameter \(\lambda \in (-1, 1)\) that controls asymmetry; \(\lambda < 0\) gives a longer left tail (dist='skewt'). The three distributions are nested, Normal within t within skewed t, so they can be compared with likelihood ratio (LR) tests or with AIC and BIC.

                In brief

                        ARMA-GARCH: one model for the mean and the variance

                        Slide 50 in the PDF

                        The ARMA(\(p\),\(q\))-GARCH(1,1) model joins the two parts of (5.2):

                        \[r_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i r_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t + \sum_{j=1}^{q}\theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j}, \qquad \varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t, \qquad \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2.\]

                        The mean equation is the ARMA model of Chapter 2; the only change is that its errors are no longer i.i.d. but follow a GARCH. Both parts are estimated at once by maximum likelihood. In arch the call is arch_model(r, mean='AR', lags=1, vol='GARCH', dist='t'); the package supports only autoregressive means, so models with MA terms need a two-step procedure or another package.

                        Why not estimate the mean by OLS and then fit a GARCH to the residuals? The OLS coefficients are still consistent, but their usual standard errors assume a constant variance, so the \(t\) tests of Chapter 2 are wrong. Joint maximum likelihood weights each observation by \(1/\sigma_t^2\), as weighted least squares would. Stormy days, which carry little information about the mean because their noise is large, receive less weight, and calm days receive more. The result is a more precise \(\hat\phi\) and valid intervals.

                          Asymmetry and the leverage effect

                          In the GARCH model the variance responds to the square of yesterday's shock, so a fall and a rise of the same size have the same effect. The QQ plots and the negative skewness parameter have already shown that the left tail of the S&P 500 is the problem. This section introduces the leverage effect, the news impact curve that displays it, and the two standard asymmetric models, GJR-GARCH and EGARCH, and measures the asymmetry on the four markets.

                          The leverage effect and the news impact curve

                          Slide 55 in the PDF

                          The leverage effect is the empirical observation, documented by Christie (1982), that falls in stock prices raise volatility more than rises of the same size. The name comes from one explanation: a lower share price raises the ratio of debt to equity, so the equity of the firm becomes riskier. A second mechanism runs in the opposite direction: news of higher future risk lowers prices at once, because investors demand a higher return. Both produce a negative relation between today's return and tomorrow's volatility.

                          GARCH(1,1) cannot represent this effect, because \(\sigma_t^2\) depends on \(\varepsilon_{t-1}^2\) and not on the sign of \(\varepsilon_{t-1}\). The QQ plot of the GARCH-t residuals and the skewed-t estimate \(\hat\lambda < 0\) already pointed to the left tail.

                          The news impact curve (NIC) of Engle and Ng (1993) makes the asymmetry visible. It plots \(\sigma_t^2\) as a function of yesterday's shock \(\varepsilon_{t-1}\), with yesterday's variance \(\sigma_{t-1}^2\) held fixed at the unconditional variance. For GARCH(1,1) the NIC is \(\omega + \alpha\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\bar\sigma^2\), a symmetric parabola centred at zero. An asymmetric model gives a curve that is steeper on the left.

                          GJR-GARCH and EGARCH

                          Slide 56 in the PDF

                          The GJR-GARCH model of Glosten, Jagannathan and Runkle (1993) gives negative shocks an extra slope:

                          \[\sigma_t^2 = \omega + (\alpha + \gamma\,I_{t-1})\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2, \qquad I_{t-1} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \varepsilon_{t-1} < 0,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\]
                          (5.8)

                          Good news has slope \(\alpha\) and bad news slope \(\alpha + \gamma\), so the leverage effect corresponds to \(\gamma > 0\). In arch the asymmetric term is requested with o=1. For a symmetric innovation distribution half of the shocks are negative, so the persistence is \(\alpha + \beta + \gamma/2\).

                          The EGARCH model of Nelson (1991) works with the logarithm of the variance:

                          \[\ln\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\big(|z_{t-1}| - E|z_{t-1}|\big) + \gamma z_{t-1} + \beta\ln\sigma_{t-1}^2.\]
                          (5.9)

                          Here \(z_{t-1}\) is yesterday's standardised shock and \(E|z_{t-1}|\) its mean absolute value, \(\sqrt{2/\pi}\) for the Normal distribution. The term with \(\alpha\) is the size effect: a shock larger than usual in absolute value raises the variance. The term with \(\gamma\) is the sign effect, and the leverage effect corresponds to \(\gamma < 0\). Because the equation is in logarithms, \(\sigma_t^2\) is positive for any parameter values, so no sign restrictions are needed. The persistence of EGARCH is \(\beta\).

                          Both models have one parameter more than GARCH(1,1), and in both \(\gamma = 0\) removes the asymmetry. The hypothesis \(\gamma = 0\) can be tested with the \(t\) statistic of \(\hat\gamma\) or with an LR test against GARCH, compared with the \(\chi^2(1)\) distribution.

                          In brief

                          • GJR-GARCH Glosten, Jagannathan and Runkle (1993): \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + (\alpha + \gamma\,I_{t-1})\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2\), with \(I_{t-1} = 1\) if \(\varepsilon_{t-1} < 0\), else 0
                            • good news: slope \(\alpha\); bad news: \(\alpha + \gamma\); leverage effect: \(\gamma > 0\); in arch: o=1
                            • persistence \(\alpha + \beta + \gamma/2\) for symmetric \(z_t\) (half of the shocks are negative)
                          • EGARCH (exponential GARCH) Nelson (1991): \(\ln\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\big(|z_{t-1}| - E|z_{t-1}|\big) + \gamma z_{t-1} + \beta\ln\sigma_{t-1}^2\)
                            • \(z_{t-1}\): yesterday's standardised shock; \(E|z_{t-1}|\): its mean absolute value (\(\sqrt{2/\pi}\) for the Normal distribution); \(\alpha\): size effect; \(\gamma\): sign effect
                            • the logarithm keeps \(\sigma_t^2 > 0\) without sign restrictions; leverage effect: \(\gamma < 0\); persistence: \(\beta\)
                          • Both have one parameter more than GARCH(1,1); test \(\gamma = 0\) with its \(t\) statistic or with an LR test, \(\chi^2(1)\)

                            Diagnostics and model choice

                            Fitting a model is not the end of the analysis. This section lists the checks a fitted volatility model must pass, applies them to the S&P 500 and the other markets, shows with the EUR/RON rate how a single extreme day can invalidate a test, and compares nine combinations of variance equation and innovation distribution by BIC.

                            Checking a fitted model

                            Slide 60 in the PDF

                            If the model is right, the standardised residuals \(\hat z_t = (r_t - \hat\mu_t)/\hat\sigma_t\) are approximately i.i.d. with mean 0 and variance 1. Each property can be checked. The Ljung–Box \(Q(10)\) on \(\hat z_t\) checks that no autocorrelation is left in the mean, as in Chapter 2. The Ljung–Box \(Q(10)\) on \(\hat z_t^2\) and the ARCH-LM test on \(\hat z_t\) check that no ARCH effects are left. The QQ plot of \(\hat z_t\) checks the assumed distribution.

                            The sign-bias test of Engle and Ng (1993) checks whether asymmetry is missing. It regresses \(\hat z_t^2\) on three variables: the indicator \(I_{t-1}\) of a negative shock, the negative part \(I_{t-1}\hat\varepsilon_{t-1}\) and the positive part \((1 - I_{t-1})\hat\varepsilon_{t-1}\). The joint test statistic \(TR^2\) is approximately \(\chi^2(3)\); a rejection means that the sign or the size of past shocks still predicts the variance after the model has been fitted.

                            Diagnostics are asymmetric in what they can show. A passed diagnostic does not prove that the model is right, because the tests have limited power against many alternatives. A failed diagnostic shows where the model is wrong and points to the extension that is needed.

                            • If the model is right, the standardised residuals \(\hat z_t = (r_t - \hat\mu_t)/\hat\sigma_t\) are close to i.i.d. with mean 0 and variance 1

                              Variance forecasts and their evaluation

                              A volatility model is ultimately used to forecast. This section derives the multi-step variance forecast of GARCH(1,1), computes it for the S&P 500 on the last day of the sample, plots the term structure of volatility on three dates, and then asks how a volatility forecast can be evaluated when the true variance is never observed. The answer, the QLIKE loss and the Diebold–Mariano test, is applied to an out-of-sample comparison of GARCH and EWMA from 2015 to 2026.

                              Multi-step forecasts

                              Slide 66 in the PDF

                              At the end of day \(t\), the one-step variance \(\sigma_{t+1}^2 = \omega + \alpha\varepsilon_t^2 + \beta\sigma_t^2\) is known exactly. Two steps ahead, \(\varepsilon_{t+1}^2\) is not yet known, but its conditional expectation is \(E_t[\varepsilon_{t+1}^2] = \sigma_{t+1}^2\). Hence \(E_t[\sigma_{t+2}^2] = \omega + \alpha\sigma_{t+1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t+1}^2 = \omega + (\alpha+\beta)\sigma_{t+1}^2\). Repeating the argument and using \(\omega = (1-\alpha-\beta)\bar\sigma^2\) gives, by induction,

                              \[E_t[\sigma_{t+h}^2] = \bar\sigma^2 + (\alpha+\beta)^{h-1}\big(\sigma_{t+1}^2 - \bar\sigma^2\big).\]
                              (5.10)

                              The forecast converges geometrically to the long-run variance, just as the forecast of an AR(1) converges to the mean of the process in Chapter 2. From a high level it decreases, from a low level it increases.

                              For risk over several days, what matters is the variance of the \(H\)-day return. Because returns are uncorrelated, the variance of their sum is the sum of the daily variances:

                              \[\mathrm{Var}_t(r_{t+1} + \dots + r_{t+H}) = \sum_{h=1}^{H} E_t[\sigma_{t+h}^2],\]

                              not \(H\sigma_{t+1}^2\). The square-root-of-time rule, \(\sigma_{t+1}\sqrt{H}\), assumes that tomorrow's variance persists unchanged for \(H\) days. In a crisis the variance is expected to fall, so the rule is too high; in a calm period it is expected to rise, so the rule is too low.

                              In brief

                              • One step: \(\sigma_{t+1}^2 = \omega + \alpha\varepsilon_t^2 + \beta\sigma_t^2\) is known at the end of day \(t\)
                                • two steps: \(E_t[\sigma_{t+2}^2] = \omega + \alpha E_t[\varepsilon_{t+1}^2] + \beta\sigma_{t+1}^2 = \omega + (\alpha + \beta)\sigma_{t+1}^2\), since \(E_t[\varepsilon_{t+1}^2] = \sigma_{t+1}^2\)
                              • By induction: \(E_t[\sigma_{t+h}^2] = \bar\sigma^2 + (\alpha + \beta)^{h-1}(\sigma_{t+1}^2 - \bar\sigma^2)\)
                                • the forecast converges to the long-run variance, like an AR(1) forecast converges to its mean (Chapter 2)
                              • Variance of the \(H\)-day return: the sum of the daily forecasts (the returns are uncorrelated)
                                • \(\mathrm{Var}_t(r_{t+1} + \dots + r_{t+H}) = \sum_{h=1}^{H} E_t[\sigma_{t+h}^2]\), not \(H\sigma_{t+1}^2\)
                                • the square-root-of-time rule \(\sigma_{t+1}\sqrt{H}\) is too high in a crisis and too low in a calm period

                                How to evaluate a volatility forecast

                                Slide 69 in the PDF

                                The true variance \(\sigma_t^2\) is never observed, so a forecast \(h_t\) can only be compared with a noisy proxy, here the squared return \(r_t^2\). The proxy is conditionally unbiased, \(E_{t-1}[r_t^2] = \sigma_t^2\) when \(\mu \approx 0\), but very noisy: \(r_t^2 = \sigma_t^2 z_t^2\), and \(z_t^2\) varies a lot from day to day. Andersen and Bollerslev (1998) showed that this noise makes the \(R^2\) of a regression of \(r_t^2\) on the forecast low even for a perfect forecast, so a low \(R^2\) is not evidence of a bad model.

                                The noise also distorts many loss functions: with a noisy proxy, some losses rank forecasts incorrectly. Patton (2011) identified the loss functions that rank forecasts correctly even with a noisy but unbiased proxy. One of them is the QLIKE loss:

                                \[L(r_t^2, h_t) = \frac{r_t^2}{h_t} + \ln h_t,\]
                                (5.11)

                                up to a constant; the smaller its mean over the evaluation period, the better the forecast. QLIKE is minus the Normal log-likelihood of (5.7) with \(\sigma_t^2\) replaced by the forecast. It is asymmetric: as \(h_t\) approaches zero, \(r_t^2/h_t\) explodes, so forecasts that are too low are punished far more than forecasts that are too high.

                                The evaluation is out of sample, as in Chapter 0: from 2015, each forecast uses only data up to the previous day, and the models are re-estimated every 250 days. Two models A and B are compared with the test of Diebold and Mariano (1995). Let \(d_t = L_t^A - L_t^B\) be the loss difference and \(\bar d\) its mean. Under \(H_0: E[d_t] = 0\), the statistic \(\mathrm{DM} = \bar d/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\bar d)\) is approximately \(N(0,1)\), where \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}\) is a heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) standard error. \(\mathrm{DM} < -1.96\) means that A has a significantly smaller loss at 5%.

                                In brief

                                • The true \(\sigma_t^2\) is never observed: we compare the forecast \(h_t\) with a noisy proxy, here \(r_t^2\)
                                  • \(E_{t-1}[r_t^2] = \sigma_t^2\) (with \(\mu \approx 0\)), but \(r_t^2\) is very noisy: a low \(R^2\) even for a perfect forecast Andersen and Bollerslev (1998)
                                • QLIKE loss Patton (2011): \(L(r_t^2, h_t) = \dfrac{r_t^2}{h_t} + \ln h_t\) (up to a constant); the smaller the mean, the better
                                  • it ranks forecasts correctly even with a noisy proxy; it is minus the Normal log-likelihood; it punishes forecasts that are too low
                                • Out of sample (Chapter 0): from 2015, each forecast uses only data up to the day before; the models are re-estimated every 250 days
                                • DM test Diebold and Mariano (1995): \(H_0\): \(E[d_t] = 0\), with \(d_t = L_t^A - L_t^B\) the loss difference of two models A and B
                                  • \(\mathrm{DM} = \bar d/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\bar d)\), approximately \(N(0, 1)\) under \(H_0\); \(\bar d\): the mean of \(d_t\); \(\mathrm{DM} < -1.96\): A is better
                                  • \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}\): HAC (heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent) standard error

                                  An application: VaR 1% from a GARCH model

                                  Value at risk is the most common use of volatility models in practice. This section builds a one-day conditional VaR 1% from the GARCH model and the quantile of the innovation distribution, computes it for the S&P 500 on 18 September 2026, follows it through the COVID-19 crash and counts its exceedances from 2015 to 2026 on the four markets.

                                  Conditional VaR

                                  Slide 74 in the PDF

                                  The value at risk (VaR) at level \(\alpha\) is the loss exceeded with probability \(\alpha\): \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha = -q_\alpha(r_{t+1})\), where \(q_\alpha\) is the \(\alpha\)-quantile of tomorrow's return. In this section \(\alpha\) denotes the VaR level, not the GARCH parameter. Here \(\alpha = 1\%\), so the VaR 1% is a loss exceeded on one day in a hundred.

                                  With \(r_{t+1} = \mu + \sigma_{t+1}z_{t+1}\), the quantile of the return is the quantile of the innovation scaled by tomorrow's volatility and shifted by the mean, so

                                  \[\mathrm{VaR}_{t+1} = -\big(\mu + \sigma_{t+1}\,q_\alpha(z)\big).\]
                                  (5.12)

                                  Here \(q_\alpha(z)\) is the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the innovation \(z\). For the Normal distribution, \(q_{0.01} = -2.326\). For the standardised t, \(q_{0.01} = t_\nu^{-1}(0.01)\sqrt{(\nu-2)/\nu}\), where \(t_\nu^{-1}\) is the quantile function of the Student distribution with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom. The VaR moves every day with \(\sigma_{t+1}\): it is high in storms and low in calm periods.

                                  For the S&P 500 on 18 September 2026, the GARCH(1,1)-t model gives \(\hat\mu = 0.079\), \(\sigma_{t+1} = 0.714\) and \(\hat\nu = 6.26\). The Student quantile is \(t_{6.26}^{-1}(0.01) = -3.100\) and the scaling factor \(\sqrt{(6.26-2)/6.26} = 0.825\), so \(q_{0.01} = -3.100 \times 0.825 = -2.557\). From (5.12), the VaR 1% is \(-(0.079 + 0.714 \times (-2.557)) = 1.75\%\). With Normal innovations the same volatility would give 1.58%. Over 10 days, using the 10-day variance of 5.67 from the forecasting section and ignoring \(\mu\), the VaR 1% is \(2.557 \times \sqrt{5.67} = 6.09\%\).

                                  In brief

                                  • VaR (value at risk) at level \(\alpha\): the loss exceeded with probability \(\alpha\), \(\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha = -q_\alpha(r_{t+1})\), with \(q_\alpha\) the \(\alpha\)-quantile
                                    • here \(\alpha = 1\%\): VaR 1%, a loss exceeded on one day in a hundred
                                  • With \(r_{t+1} = \mu + \sigma_{t+1}z_{t+1}\): \(\mathrm{VaR}_{t+1} = -(\mu + \sigma_{t+1}\,q_\alpha(z))\)
                                    • \(q_\alpha(z)\): the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the innovation \(z\); Normal: \(q_{0.01} = \ensuremath{-}2.326\)
                                    • standardised t: \(q_{0.01} = t_\nu^{-1}(0.01)\sqrt{(\nu - 2)/\nu}\), \(t_\nu^{-1}\): the quantile function of the Student distribution with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom
                                    • the VaR moves every day with \(\sigma_{t+1}\): high in storms, low in calm periods
                                  • Worked example: S&P 500 on 18 September 2026, GARCH(1,1)-t: \(\hat\mu = 0.079\), \(\sigma_{t+1} = 0.714\), \(\hat\nu = 6.26\)
                                    • \(t_{\hat\nu}^{-1}(0.01) = \ensuremath{-}3.100\), \(\sqrt{(\hat\nu - 2)/\hat\nu} = 0.825\), so \(q_{0.01} = \ensuremath{-}3.100 \times 0.825 = \ensuremath{-}2.557\)
                                    • VaR 1% \(= -(0.079 + 0.714 \times (\ensuremath{-}2.557)) = 1.75\%\)

                                    Checks you must run

                                    Slide 79 in the PDF

                                    The checks follow the errors that this chapter has shown to be common. Returns must be in percent, not decimals, and a series with a very small variance, such as the EUR/RON rate, must be rescaled, with \(\omega\) converted back afterwards.                                     Returns have an almost unpredictable mean and a predictable variance, which the decomposition \(r_t = \mu_t + \sigma_t z_t\) separates. ARCH effects are detected with the ACF of \(r_t^2\) and the ARCH-LM test. GARCH(1,1) is an ARMA(1,1) for \(\varepsilon_t^2\); on daily data its persistence \(\alpha + \beta\) is close to 1, and the boundary case \(\alpha + \beta = 1\) gives IGARCH and EWMA.

                                    The parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood with arch, with robust standard errors and Student-t innovations for the tails. ARMA-GARCH estimates the mean of Chapter 2 and the variance of this chapter together. Equity indices need asymmetry, through GJR-GARCH or EGARCH, and every fit must be followed by checks on \(\hat z_t\) and \(\hat z_t^2\). Variance forecasts revert to the long-run level; they are compared out of sample with QLIKE and the Diebold–Mariano test, and the one-day VaR 1% is \(-(\mu + \sigma_{t+1}q_{0.01}(z))\).

                                    In brief

                                    • Returns: an almost unpredictable mean and a predictable variance, \(r_t = \mu_t + \sigma_t z_t\); test with the ACF of \(r_t^2\) and ARCH-LM
                                    • GARCH(1,1) is an ARMA(1,1) for \(\varepsilon_t^2\); \(\alpha + \beta\) close to 1; IGARCH and EWMA when \(\alpha + \beta = 1\)
                                    • Estimate by maximum likelihood with arch, report robust SE; Student-t innovations for the tails
                                    • ARMA-GARCH: the mean of Chapter 2 and the variance of this chapter estimated together
                                    • Equity indices need asymmetry (GJR, EGARCH); check \(\hat z_t\) and \(\hat z_t^2\) after every fit
                                    • Forecasts revert to the long-run level; compare them out of sample with QLIKE and DM; VaR 1% \(= -(\mu + \sigma_{t+1}q_{0.01}(z))\)

                                    Self-assessment

                                    Slide 82 in the PDF

                                    The first question applies the long-run variance and the half-life: with \(\omega = 0.05\), \(\alpha = 0.05\) and \(\beta = 0.90\), the long-run variance is \(0.05/(1 - 0.95) = 1\) and the half-life is \(\ln 0.5/\ln 0.95 = 13.5\) days. The second applies the ARCH-LM test: with \(q = 5\), 1000 residuals and \(R^2 = 0.004\), the statistic is \(1000 \times 0.004 = 4\), below the 5% critical value of 11.07, so the test does not detect ARCH effects at 5%.

                                    The third question applies the GJR equation (5.8) with \(\hat\alpha = 0\) and \(\hat\gamma = 0.2\). After a rise of 3%, \(I_{t-1} = 0\) and the news term is \(0 \times 9 = 0\), so the variance changes only through the decay \(\beta\sigma_t^2\) and the constant: in this model only negative shocks raise the variance. The next chapter, Chapter 6, moves from one series to several: VAR models and Granger causality.

                                    In brief

                                    • Question: \(\omega = 0.05\), \(\alpha = 0.05\), \(\beta = 0.90\). What are the long-run variance and the half-life?
                                      • Answer: \(0.05/0.05 = 1\); \(\ln 0.5/\ln 0.95 = 13.5\) days
                                    • Question: an ARCH-LM regression with \(q = 5\) on 1000 residuals gives \(R^2 = 0.004\). Are there ARCH effects at 5%?
                                      • Answer: no: \(\mathrm{LM} = 1000 \times 0.004 = 4 < 11.07\)
                                    • Question: GJR gives \(\hat\alpha = 0\) and \(\hat\gamma = 0.2\). What happens to the variance after a rise of 3%?
                                      • Answer: nothing beyond the decay \(\beta\sigma_t^2\): only negative shocks raise the variance

                                    Section summary

                                    The chapter has turned volatility clustering into a model. GARCH(1,1), estimated by maximum likelihood with Student-t innovations, captures the persistence of volatility in all four markets, and asymmetric versions capture the leverage effect of equity indices. The models are checked on standardised residuals, compared out of sample with QLIKE and the Diebold–Mariano test, and used to build a VaR 1% that follows the state of the market.
