Today's question and route

Slide 2 in the PDF

The chapter asks how to measure a quantity that cannot be observed directly: the true level of a noisy series, the deviation of GDP from potential, a beta coefficient that changes over time, or a recession. The common answer is to write the unobserved quantity as a state and to let every new observation correct its estimate. The estimate is built recursively: in each period, the old estimate and the new observation are combined into an updated estimate.

The route has four stages. The first defines the state space form and shows that the local level, local linear trend and ARMA models and the regression with time-varying parameters are special cases of it. The second builds the Kalman filter, applies it by hand and uses it for smoothing, likelihood, missing values and forecasting. The third applies the method to the trend and cycle of Romanian GDP, discusses the Hodrick–Prescott filter and introduces dynamic factors. The fourth moves to discrete regimes with Markov-switching models. The chapter builds on the exponential smoothing of Chapter 0, the ARMA models of Chapter 2, the GARCH models of Chapter 5 and the long memory of Chapter 8.

Many of the quantities that matter in economics and finance cannot be observed directly. The "true" level of a noisy series, the potential output of an economy, the sensitivity of one stock market to another, or whether the economy is in a recession are quantities we infer from data rather than read off it. Published statistics are imperfect measurements of these quantities: they contain noise, arrive with a delay and get revised.

This chapter treats the unobserved quantity as a state that evolves over time according to a known law, and the data as noisy measurements of it. The state space form writes both relations in one framework, and the Kalman filter updates the estimate of the state period by period as observations arrive. The same framework contains the ARMA models of Chapter 2, the exponential smoothing of Chapter 0, trend–cycle models, regressions with moving coefficients and models with common factors.

The last part of the chapter moves to a discrete state: a regime, such as recession or expansion, a calm market or a turbulent one. The Markov-switching models of Hamilton (1989) estimate in every period the probability of each regime. The applications use the flow of the Nile, the GDP of Romania and of the US, the BET index and the S&P 500.

Hidden states

A hidden state is a quantity that drives the behaviour of the data but does not itself appear in the data. The idea comes from control engineering, where the position and velocity of a spacecraft are inferred from imperfect measurements. The section presents the origin of the Kalman filter and then the inventory of hidden states estimated in the chapter: the level of a series, the trend and cycle of GDP, a beta coefficient, a common factor and a regime.

Rudolf Kálmán and the Apollo navigation

Slide 5 in the PDF

Kalman (1960) proposed a recursive method for estimating a hidden state from noisy measurements. The method alternates two steps. First, the dynamic model predicts where the state should be in the next period. Then a new measurement arrives, and the prediction is corrected by a fraction of the difference between the measurement and what the model expected. The weight of this correction is called the Kalman gain, and it balances two sources of uncertainty: how uncertain the model's prediction is and how noisy the measurement is.

The first large-scale use was in space navigation. At NASA Ames, the filter was adapted for the navigation of the Apollo spacecraft: the state was the position and velocity of the craft, and the measurements were star sightings and radar data. The 1968 photograph shows Jim Lovell at the guidance and navigation station of Apollo 8.

In economics the logic is the same, but the state has another meaning. It may be the trend or the cycle of a series, a regression coefficient that changes, or the regime the economy is in, and the measurements are the published data. As in navigation, no single measurement reveals the state; it is revealed by the combination of the model and the sequence of measurements.

Hidden states in economics and finance

Slide 6 in the PDF

The table lists the observed series, the hidden state and the model used in this chapter. The annual flow of the Nile and monthly inflation have a background level that changes slowly, covered by period-to-period fluctuations; the model is the local level. Real GDP contains a trend and a deviation from trend, estimated with an unobserved components model. The returns of a stock index depend on the returns of another market through a beta coefficient that may change over time, modelled with a regression with time-varying parameters. Several indicators of economic activity share an unobserved factor, estimated with a dynamic factor model. GDP growth and stock returns pass through regimes, recession or expansion, calm or turbulent, described by Markov-switching models.

The essential difference between the first four examples and the last is the nature of the state. In the first four the state is continuous: a level, a trend or a coefficient can take any real value. For a continuous state with linear dynamics and Gaussian errors, the optimal filter is the Kalman filter. In the last example the state is discrete: the economy is either in one regime or in the other. For a discrete state that evolves as a Markov chain, the corresponding filter is the Hamilton filter.

The two filters share the same structure. Each predicts today's state from yesterday's information, then updates the prediction using today's observation \(y_t\). The Kalman filter carries a mean and a variance; the Hamilton filter carries a probability for each regime.

The state space form

The state space form writes a time series model with two equations: one that says how the state generates the data and one that says how the state evolves. The section defines the general form, then builds the local level and local linear trend models in turn, writes ARMA models and the regression with time-varying parameters in this form, and shows the link with exponential smoothing. All these models differ only in the choice of the system matrices.

The state space form

Slide 7 in the PDF

The state space form has two equations:

\[\begin{aligned} y_t &= Z_t\alpha_t + \varepsilon_t,\\ \alpha_{t+1} &= T\alpha_t + R\eta_t,\\ \varepsilon_t &\sim N(0, H), \quad \eta_t \sim N(0, Q). \end{aligned}\]
(10.1)

The first is the measurement equation. It links the observation \(y_t\), here a scalar, to the state vector \(\alpha_t\), of dimension \(m \times 1\), which is not observed. The row \(Z_t\), of dimension \(1 \times m\), holds known numbers that say which combination of the state is measured, and \(\varepsilon_t\) is the measurement noise, with variance \(H\). The second is the transition equation: the state evolves as a vector autoregression of order 1, VAR(1), of the kind studied in Chapter 6, with the \(m \times m\) matrix \(T\). The matrix \(R\) says how the state shocks \(\eta_t\) enter each component of the state, and \(Q\) is their variance matrix.

The model is completed by the initial state, \(\alpha_1 \sim N(a_1, P_1)\), where \(a_1\) and \(P_1\) are its mean and variance, and by the assumption that \(\varepsilon_t\), \(\eta_s\) and \(\alpha_1\) are mutually independent. The system matrices \(Z_t\), \(H\), \(T\), \(R\) and \(Q\) depend on a parameter vector \(\theta\), usually variances and autoregressive coefficients, which is estimated by maximum likelihood.

The advantage of this form is its generality. The same two equations, with other matrices, describe ARMA models, exponential smoothing, trend–cycle models, regressions with moving coefficients and factor models (Durbin and Koopman, 2012). Once a model is written in this form, the same Kalman filter computes the state estimate, the likelihood, the forecasts and the missing values.

In brief

  • Measurement equation: \(y_t = Z_t\alpha_t + \varepsilon_t\),   \(\varepsilon_t \sim N(0, H)\)
    • \(y_t\): the observation (here a scalar); \(\alpha_t\): the state vector (\(m \times 1\)), not observed; \(Z_t\): a \(1 \times m\) row of known numbers
  • Transition equation: \(\alpha_{t+1} = T\alpha_t + R\eta_t\),   \(\eta_t \sim N(0, Q)\)
    • the state evolves as a first-order vector autoregression (VAR(1), Chapter 6); \(T\) is \(m \times m\)
    • \(R\): maps the shocks \(\eta_t\) into the state; \(H\), \(Q\): the variances of the measurement and of the state shocks
    • initial state: \(\alpha_1 \sim N(a_1, P_1)\), \(a_1\), \(P_1\): its mean and variance; \(\varepsilon_t\), \(\eta_s\) and \(\alpha_1\) mutually independent
  • The system matrices \(Z_t, H, T, R, Q\) contain the parameters \(\theta\); they are estimated by maximum likelihood
    • the same form covers ARMA, exponential smoothing, trend–cycle models, regressions with moving coefficients and factor models Durbin and Koopman (2012)

The local level model

Slide 8 in the PDF

The simplest state space model is the local level model, also called the random walk plus noise:

\[y_t = \mu_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \mu_{t+1} = \mu_t + \eta_t.\]
(10.2)

The state is the level \(\mu_t\), and all matrices are scalars: \(Z = T = R = 1\), \(H = \sigma^2_\varepsilon\) and \(Q = \sigma^2_\eta\). The two shocks play different roles. The measurement shock \(\varepsilon_t\) affects only the current observation and is gone in the next period. The level shock \(\eta_t\) moves the level permanently, because the level is a random walk.

The signal-to-noise ratio \(q = \sigma^2_\eta/\sigma^2_\varepsilon\) measures how much of the movement of the series is permanent. For \(q = 0\) the level is constant, and the series is white noise around a mean. As \(q\) grows without bound, the measurement noise becomes negligible and the series is a pure random walk. Between these extremes, the series has a moving level observed through noise.

The reduced form of the model shows the link with ARIMA models. The first difference is \(\Delta y_t = \eta_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t - \varepsilon_{t-1}\), a sum of white noises with no autocorrelation beyond lag 1, hence an MA(1) process. Its variance is \(\gamma(0) = \sigma^2_\eta + 2\sigma^2_\varepsilon\), and its first-order autocovariance is \(\gamma(1) = -\sigma^2_\varepsilon\), because \(\varepsilon_{t-1}\) enters \(\Delta y_t\) with a minus sign and \(\Delta y_{t-1}\) with a plus sign. This gives \(\rho(1) = -1/(q+2)\), a value between \(-1/2\) and 0. The local level model is therefore an ARIMA(0,1,1), of the kind studied in Chapter 3, with a negative MA coefficient.

In brief

  • Local level (random walk plus noise): \(y_t = \mu_t + \varepsilon_t\),   \(\mu_{t+1} = \mu_t + \eta_t\)
    • state \(\alpha_t = \mu_t\), the level; \(Z = T = R = 1\), \(H = \sigma^2_\varepsilon\), \(Q = \sigma^2_\eta\)
    • \(\varepsilon_t\): measurement noise that disappears next period; \(\eta_t\): a permanent shift of the level
  • Signal-to-noise ratio \(q = \sigma^2_\eta/\sigma^2_\varepsilon\): how much of the movement is permanent
    • \(q = 0\): a constant level (white noise around a mean); \(q \to \infty\): a pure random walk
  • Reduced form: \(\Delta y_t = \eta_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t - \varepsilon_{t-1}\) is an MA(1) with \(\rho(1) = -1/(q+2)\)
    • so the local level model is an ARIMA(0,1,1) (Chapter 3) with a negative MA coefficient
    • \(\gamma(0) = \sigma^2_\eta + 2\sigma^2_\varepsilon\) and \(\gamma(1) = -\sigma^2_\varepsilon\) give \(\rho(1)\)

        The local linear trend

        Slide 11 in the PDF

        The local linear trend model adds to the level a slope that also evolves over time:

        \[\begin{aligned} y_t &= \mu_t + \varepsilon_t,\\ \mu_{t+1} &= \mu_t + \beta_t + \eta_t,\\ \beta_{t+1} &= \beta_t + \zeta_t. \end{aligned}\]

        The state now has two components, \(\alpha_t = (\mu_t, \beta_t)'\): the level and the slope, that is, the growth rate per period. The measurement equation reads only the level, so \(Z = (1,\ 0)\). The transition matrix \(T\) has first row \((1,\ 1)\), because tomorrow's level is today's level plus today's slope, and second row \((0,\ 1)\), because tomorrow's slope is today's slope plus a shock. The level and slope shocks are independent, with \(Q = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma^2_\eta, \sigma^2_\zeta)\).

        Three special cases link the model to earlier chapters. If both state variances are zero, the level and slope are fixed, and the trend is linear and deterministic, \(\mu_1 + \beta_1 t\), as in Chapter 3. If only \(\sigma^2_\zeta = 0\), the slope is constant and the level is a random walk with drift \(\beta\). If only \(\sigma^2_\eta = 0\), the level receives no shocks of its own and the trend changes only its slope; this is the smooth trend, also called the integrated random walk, the model behind the Hodrick–Prescott filter of Section 5.

        The model's forecast extends the last estimate of the level with the last estimate of the slope: \(\hat y_{T+h} = \hat\mu_T + h\hat\beta_T\). This is the state space version of Holt's method from Chapter 0, except that the smoothing weights are not chosen ad hoc but follow from the estimated variances.

        In brief

        • Local linear trend: \(y_t = \mu_t + \varepsilon_t\),   \(\mu_{t+1} = \mu_t + \beta_t + \eta_t\),   \(\beta_{t+1} = \beta_t + \zeta_t\)
          • state \(\alpha_t = (\mu_t, \beta_t)'\): the level and the slope (the growth rate)
          • \(Z = (1,\ 0)\),   \(T = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\),   \(Q = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma^2_\eta, \sigma^2_\zeta)\)
        • Special cases
          • \(\sigma^2_\eta = \sigma^2_\zeta = 0\): a deterministic linear trend \(\mu_1 + \beta_1 t\) (Chapter 3)
          • \(\sigma^2_\zeta = 0\): a random walk with drift \(\beta\)
          • \(\sigma^2_\eta = 0\): the smooth trend (integrated random walk), the model behind the HP filter (Section 5)
        • Forecasts: \(\hat y_{T+h} = \hat\mu_T + h\hat\beta_T\), the state space version of Holt's method (Chapter 0)

        ARMA models in state space form

        Slide 12 in the PDF

        Every ARMA model can be written in state space form. For an AR(2), \(y_t = \phi_1y_{t-1} + \phi_2y_{t-2} + u_t\), the state keeps the last two values, \(\alpha_t = (y_t, y_{t-1})'\). The measurement equation reads the first component, \(y_t = (1,\ 0)\,\alpha_t\), without measurement noise (\(H = 0\)). The transition equation is

        \[\alpha_{t+1} = \begin{pmatrix}\phi_1 & \phi_2\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\alpha_t + \begin{pmatrix}1\\ 0\end{pmatrix}u_{t+1}.\]

        The first row reproduces the AR(2) equation, and the second only moves \(y_t\) into the "yesterday's value" slot. The matrix \(T\) is the companion matrix of Chapter 2; its eigenvalues are the inverses of the roots of the AR polynomial, so the process is stationary exactly when the eigenvalues lie inside the unit circle.

        For an ARMA(1,1), \(y_t = \phi y_{t-1} + u_t + \theta u_{t-1}\), a convenient choice of state is \(\alpha_t = (y_t, \theta u_t)'\), with \(T\) having rows \((\phi,\ 1)\) and \((0,\ 0)\) and with \(R = (1,\ \theta)'\). The first row gives \(y_{t+1} = \phi y_t + \theta u_t + u_{t+1}\), exactly the ARMA(1,1) equation. In general, an ARMA\((p,q)\) needs a state of dimension \(m = \max(p, q+1)\).

        The practical importance is that the Kalman filter computes the exact Gaussian likelihood of an ARMA model, even with missing values. For this, the initial state starts from the stationary distribution: \(P_1\) solves \(P = TPT' + RQR'\), with solution \(\mathrm{vec}(P_1) = (I - T \otimes T)^{-1}\mathrm{vec}(RQR')\), where \(\mathrm{vec}\) stacks the columns of a matrix and \(\otimes\) is the Kronecker product. This is how SARIMAX in statsmodels estimates the ARIMA models of Chapters 2–4.

        In brief

        • AR(2): \(y_t = \phi_1y_{t-1} + \phi_2y_{t-2} + u_t\); state \(\alpha_t = (y_t, y_{t-1})'\)

        Regression with time-varying parameters

        Slide 14 in the PDF

        An ordinary regression assumes that the effect of \(x_t\) on \(y_t\) is the same over the whole period. The regression with time-varying parameters (TVP) drops this assumption and lets the coefficient evolve as a random walk:

        \[y_t = \alpha + \beta_t x_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \beta_{t+1} = \beta_t + \eta_t.\]

        The state is \((\alpha, \beta_t)'\). What is new compared with the earlier models is that the measurement row \(Z_t = (1,\ x_t)\) changes over time, because it contains the regressor. The state variance matrix is \(Q = \mathrm{diag}(0, \sigma^2_\eta)\): the intercept is fixed and the coefficient moves. If \(\sigma^2_\eta = 0\), the coefficient is constant, and the Kalman filter computes the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimator recursively, updating it with each new observation.

        Economic examples abound: a market beta that rises in crises, an exchange-rate pass-through to inflation that changes with the monetary regime, an inflation persistence that falls after the adoption of inflation targeting. The usual alternative is a rolling-window regression. The TVP regression is better for three reasons: it does not require an arbitrary choice of window length, it uses all observations with weights set by the likelihood, and it gives a standard error for every \(\beta_t\).

        In brief

        • Time-varying parameter (TVP) regression: \(y_t = \alpha + \beta_t x_t + \varepsilon_t\),   \(\beta_{t+1} = \beta_t + \eta_t\)
          • state \((\alpha, \beta_t)'\); the row \(Z_t = (1,\ x_t)\) changes over time; \(Q = \mathrm{diag}(0, \sigma^2_\eta)\)
          • \(\sigma^2_\eta = 0\): ordinary least squares (OLS), computed recursively
        • Examples
          • a market beta that rises in crises; the pass-through of the exchange rate to inflation; the persistence of inflation
        • Better than a rolling window: no window length to choose, every observation is used, and \(\beta_t\) comes with a standard error

        Exponential smoothing as a state space model

        Slide 15 in the PDF

        Chapter 0 introduced simple exponential smoothing (SES): a level \(\ell_t\) is updated by the rule \(\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1}\), and the forecast for the next period is \(\hat y_{t+1} = \ell_t\). Rewritten as \(\ell_t = \ell_{t-1} + \alpha(y_t - \ell_{t-1})\), the rule says that the new level is the old level plus a fraction \(\alpha\) of the surprise, that is, of the difference between the observation and the forecast.

        This is exactly the form of a Kalman update. Muth (1960) showed that SES gives the optimal forecasts when the data follow the local level model. Section 3 shows that the weight \(\alpha\) is the steady-state Kalman gain of this model, so choosing \(\alpha\) amounts to an assumption about the signal-to-noise ratio.

        The ETS models (error, trend, seasonality) of Hyndman et al. (2008) are also state space models, but with a single source of error: the same shock enters the observation and all the components. The models in this chapter have separate shocks for each component, such as \(\varepsilon_t\) and \(\eta_t\) in the local level model. Both families give the same point forecasts in the simple cases, but they differ in the interpretation of the components and in how they are estimated.

        In brief

        • Chapter 0: simple exponential smoothing (SES) updates a level \(\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1}\) and forecasts \(\hat y_{t+1} = \ell_t\)
          • equivalently \(\ell_t = \ell_{t-1} + \alpha(y_t - \ell_{t-1})\): old level plus a share \(\alpha\) of the surprise
        • Muth (1960): SES gives the optimal forecasts when the data follow the local level model
          • Section 3 shows it: \(\alpha\) is the steady-state Kalman gain
        • ETS models (error, trend, seasonal) are state space models with a single source of error Hyndman et al. (2008); the models here have separate shocks for each component

        Section summary

        The state space form reduces a wide variety of models to the same pair of equations. The local level separates a permanent level from transitory noise, the local linear trend adds a slowly changing growth rate, ARMA models enter through the companion matrix, and the TVP regression lets a coefficient move. Exponential smoothing, in turn, is the updating rule of a local level model. The next step is the algorithm that estimates the state in all these models: the Kalman filter.

        The Kalman filter

        The Kalman filter computes, period by period, the best estimate of the state given the observations so far, together with the uncertainty of that estimate. The section presents the idea of the filter, the formulas of its two steps, the role of the Kalman gain, an example computed by hand and the steady state, which links the filter to exponential smoothing. The application is the flow of the Nile.

        The idea of the filter

        Slide 17 in the PDF

        Write \(Y_t = (y_1, \dots, y_t)\) for all observations up to time \(t\). The filter tracks two estimates of the state. The prediction \(a_t = E(\alpha_t \mid Y_{t-1})\) is the estimate of today's state from the data up to yesterday, with variance matrix \(P_t\). The filtered estimate \(a_{t|t} = E(\alpha_t \mid Y_t)\) also uses today's observation, with variance \(P_{t|t}\).

        Each period has two steps. In the update step, \(y_t\) arrives and the prediction \(a_t\) is corrected by a fraction of the surprise, that is, of the prediction error \(v_t = y_t - Z_ta_t\). In the prediction step, the filtered estimate passes through the transition equation and gives the prediction for the next period, \(a_{t+1}\). The cycle repeats until the end of the sample.

        If the errors are Gaussian, \(a_{t|t}\) is the exact conditional mean of the state, hence the best estimate in the mean squared error sense. Without normality, the filter remains the best estimator that is linear in the data (Durbin and Koopman, 2012). The filter needs a starting point, \(a_1\) and \(P_1\). For a stationary state, the stationary distribution is used. For a nonstationary state, such as the level of a random walk, a diffuse initialisation is used: a very large \(P_1\), which means we know nothing about the initial state, so that the first observation determines the estimate almost entirely.

        In brief

        • Notation: \(Y_t = (y_1, \dots, y_t)\); we track two numbers (vectors) for the state
          • prediction: \(a_t = E(\alpha_t \mid Y_{t-1})\) with variance \(P_t\)
          • filtered estimate: \(a_{t|t} = E(\alpha_t \mid Y_t)\) with variance \(P_{t|t}\)
        • Each period has two steps
          • update: \(y_t\) arrives; correct \(a_t\) by a share of the surprise \(v_t = y_t - Z_ta_t\)
          • prediction: push the filtered state through the transition equation to get \(a_{t+1}\)
        • With Gaussian errors, \(a_{t|t}\) is the exact conditional mean; without normality it is still the best linear estimate Durbin and Koopman (2012)
        • Start: \(a_1, P_1\); for a non-stationary state we use a diffuse start (\(P_1\) very large: we know nothing)

        The two steps in formulas

        Slide 18 in the PDF

        The update step applies when \(y_t\) is observed:

        \[\begin{aligned} v_t &= y_t - Z_ta_t,\\ F_t &= Z_tP_tZ_t' + H,\\ K_t &= P_tZ_t'F_t^{-1},\\ a_{t|t} &= a_t + K_tv_t,\\ P_{t|t} &= P_t - K_tZ_tP_t. \end{aligned}\]
        (10.3)

        The prediction error \(v_t\) has variance \(F_t\), which adds the uncertainty about the state, \(Z_tP_tZ_t'\), to the measurement noise, \(H\). The Kalman gain \(K_t\) turns the surprise into a correction of the state, and the state variance falls after the observation. The prediction step then passes the filtered state through the transition equation:

        \[\begin{aligned} a_{t+1} &= Ta_{t|t},\\ P_{t+1} &= TP_{t|t}T' + RQR'. \end{aligned}\]
        (10.4)

        For the local level model all quantities are scalars: \(F_t = P_t + \sigma^2_\varepsilon\), \(K_t = P_t/(P_t + \sigma^2_\varepsilon)\), and the two steps combine into \(a_{t+1} = a_t + K_t(y_t - a_t)\) and \(P_{t+1} = P_t(1 - K_t) + \sigma^2_\eta\). The level predicted for tomorrow is the level predicted for today plus a fraction \(K_t\) of today's surprise. If \(y_t\) is missing, the update step is skipped, \(a_{t|t} = a_t\) and \(P_{t|t} = P_t\), and the filter goes straight to the prediction.

        In brief

        • Update (when \(y_t\) is observed)
          • prediction error \(v_t = y_t - Z_ta_t\), with variance \(F_t = Z_tP_tZ_t' + H\)
          • Kalman gain \(K_t = P_tZ_t'F_t^{-1}\)
          • \(a_{t|t} = a_t + K_tv_t\),   \(P_{t|t} = P_t - K_tZ_tP_t\)
        • Prediction
          • \(a_{t+1} = Ta_{t|t}\),   \(P_{t+1} = TP_{t|t}T' + RQR'\)
        • Local level: \(F_t = P_t + \sigma^2_\varepsilon\),   \(K_t = P_t/(P_t + \sigma^2_\varepsilon)\)
          • \(a_{t+1} = a_t + K_t(y_t - a_t)\),   \(P_{t+1} = P_t(1 - K_t) + \sigma^2_\eta\)
        • Missing \(y_t\): skip the update (\(a_{t|t} = a_t\), \(P_{t|t} = P_t\)) and predict

        The Kalman gain

        Slide 19 in the PDF

        In the local level model, the gain \(K_t = P_t/(P_t + \sigma^2_\varepsilon)\) is a number between 0 and 1, and the filtered estimate can be written as a weighted average: \(a_{t|t} = (1 - K_t)\,a_t + K_t\,y_t\). The gain is the weight given to the new observation, and \(1 - K_t\) is the weight of the model's prediction.

        Two uncertainties compete. If the measurements are very noisy, \(\sigma^2_\varepsilon\) is large, the gain is small, and the filter trusts the model more than any single observation. If the state is very uncertain, \(P_t\) is large, for example at the start of the sample or after a run of missing values, the gain approaches 1, and the filter relies almost entirely on the data. The formula is the same as for combining two independent estimates of the same quantity, each weighted by its precision: the precision of the prediction is \(1/P_t\) and that of the observation is \(1/\sigma^2_\varepsilon\).

        The state variance always falls after an observation, because \(P_{t|t} = (1 - K_t)P_t \le P_t\): any new information reduces uncertainty. In the prediction step the variance rises by \(\sigma^2_\eta\), because the level receives a new shock. The two forces balance: after a number of periods, the variance and the gain settle into a steady state.

        In brief

        • Local level: \(K_t = \dfrac{P_t}{P_t + \sigma^2_\varepsilon}\) is the weight of the new observation, between 0 and 1
          • \(a_{t|t} = (1 - K_t)\,a_t + K_t\,y_t\): a weighted average of the prediction and the observation
        • Two uncertainties compete
          • noisy measurements (large \(\sigma^2_\varepsilon\)): small \(K_t\), trust the model
          • uncertain state (large \(P_t\), e.g. at the start or after missing data): large \(K_t\), trust the data
        • The variance always falls with an observation: \(P_{t|t} = (1 - K_t)P_t \le P_t\)
          • and grows by \(\sigma^2_\eta\) in the prediction step: the two forces settle at a steady state
        • The same formula as the weighting of two independent estimates by their precisions \(1/P_t\) and \(1/\sigma^2_\varepsilon\)

        Steady state and exponential smoothing

        Slide 22 in the PDF

        In the local level model, the prediction variance \(P_t\) converges to a fixed value \(\bar P\) at which a full step of the filter no longer changes the variance: \(\bar P = \bar P(1 - \bar K) + \sigma^2_\eta\), with \(\bar K = \bar P/(\bar P + \sigma^2_\varepsilon)\). Substituting \(\bar K\) gives a quadratic equation in \(\bar P\), whose positive solution is

        \[\begin{aligned} \bar P &= \sigma^2_\varepsilon\,\frac{q + \sqrt{q^2 + 4q}}{2},\\ \bar K &= \frac{\bar P}{\bar P + \sigma^2_\varepsilon}. \end{aligned}\]
        (10.5)

        For the previous example, \(q = 0.25\) gives \(\bar P/\sigma^2_\varepsilon = (0.25 + \sqrt{1.0625})/2 = 0.64\), so \(\bar P = 2.56\) and \(\bar K = 2.56/6.56 = 0.390\).

        In the steady state the updating rule becomes \(a_{t+1} = a_t + \bar K(y_t - a_t) = \bar K y_t + (1 - \bar K)a_t\). This is exactly simple exponential smoothing with weight \(\alpha = \bar K\): exponential smoothing is the steady-state Kalman filter of a local level model (Muth, 1960). The relation can be inverted. An SES weight \(\alpha\) corresponds to the signal-to-noise ratio \(q = \alpha^2/(1 - \alpha)\), so a large weight indicates a level that moves a lot relative to the noise.

        The state space version adds what exponential smoothing lacks: a likelihood from which \(q\) is estimated, forecast intervals based on estimated variances, and a natural treatment of missing values. In addition, at the start of the sample the filter's time-varying gain is larger than \(\bar K\) and adapts faster than a fixed SES weight.

        In brief

        • For the local level model, \(P_t\) converges to the solution of \(\bar P = \bar P(1 - \bar K) + \sigma^2_\eta\) with \(\bar K = \bar P/(\bar P + \sigma^2_\varepsilon)\)
          • solution: \(\bar P = \sigma^2_\varepsilon\,\dfrac{q + \sqrt{q^2 + 4q}}{2}\),   \(\bar K = \dfrac{\bar P}{\bar P + \sigma^2_\varepsilon}\)
          • example: \(q = 0.25\) gives \(\bar P/\sigma^2_\varepsilon = (0.25 + \sqrt{1.0625})/2 = 2.56/4\) and \(\bar K = 0.390\)
        • In the steady state: \(a_{t+1} = a_t + \bar K(y_t - a_t) = \bar K y_t + (1 - \bar K)a_t\)
          • this is SES with \(\alpha = \bar K\): exponential smoothing is the Kalman filter of a local level model Muth (1960)
        • Inverse map: an SES weight \(\alpha\) corresponds to \(q = \alpha^2/(1 - \alpha)\)
          • the state space version adds what SES lacks: a likelihood for \(q\), forecast intervals, missing values

                        Section summary

                        The Kalman filter alternates prediction and update, and its gain splits each surprise between signal and noise according to the relative variances of the state and the measurement. In the local level model the gain converges quickly to a steady-state value, and the steady-state filter is simple exponential smoothing. For the Nile, each new flow moves the level by about a quarter of the surprise, and the filter needs several years to accept the fall in level around 1900.

                        Smoothing, likelihood and missing data

                        The Kalman filter is only the first step. The same recursion, completed by a backward pass, gives the estimate of the state based on the whole sample. The filter's prediction errors give the likelihood, and hence the parameter estimates, and the residuals for diagnostics. Missing values and forecasts are handled by skipping the update step. The section presents these tools and applies them to the Nile series.

                        Smoothing: using the whole sample

                        Slide 28 in the PDF

                        Filtering uses the information \(Y_t\), the past and the present: it is the estimate an analyst can make in real time. Smoothing uses the information \(Y_n\), the whole sample of size \(n\): the smoothed estimate \(\hat\alpha_t = E(\alpha_t \mid Y_n)\) also takes into account the observations after \(t\). Its variance \(V_t\) cannot exceed the variance of the filtered estimate, \(V_t \le P_{t|t}\), because it uses more information.

                        The Rauch–Tung–Striebel smoother (Rauch, Tung and Striebel, 1965) is a backward pass, run after the filter has gone through the whole sample, from \(t = n-1\) down to \(t = 1\):

                        \[\begin{aligned} J_t &= P_{t|t}T'P_{t+1}^{-1},\\ \hat\alpha_t &= a_{t|t} + J_t(\hat\alpha_{t+1} - a_{t+1}),\\ V_t &= P_{t|t} + J_t(V_{t+1} - P_{t+1})J_t'. \end{aligned}\]

                        The matrix \(J_t\) is the smoother gain: the weight with which the revision coming from the future, \(\hat\alpha_{t+1} - a_{t+1}\), corrects the filtered estimate. If the smoothed estimate of tomorrow's state differs from the prediction made today, the future data contain information about today's state. At \(t = n\) there is no future, so the smoothed and filtered estimates coincide.

                        The two estimates have different uses. The filtered state is used for real-time decisions and forecasting, because it contains only the information available at the time. The smoothed state is used for history: dating breaks, estimating past output gaps or reconstructing a series.

                        In brief

                        • Filtering uses \(Y_t\) (the past and the present): the real-time estimate
                          • smoothing uses \(Y_n\) (the whole sample): \(\hat\alpha_t = E(\alpha_t \mid Y_n)\), with variance \(V_t \le P_{t|t}\)
                        • Rauch–Tung–Striebel smoother Rauch, Tung and Striebel (1965): one backward pass after the filter, from \(t = n-1\) to 1
                          • \(n\): the sample size; \(J_t\): the smoother gain, the weight of the revision coming from the future
                          • \(J_t = P_{t|t}T'P_{t+1}^{-1}\),   \(\hat\alpha_t = a_{t|t} + J_t(\hat\alpha_{t+1} - a_{t+1})\)
                          • \(V_t = P_{t|t} + J_t(V_{t+1} - P_{t+1})J_t'\); at \(t = n\) smoothed and filtered coincide
                        • Use: the filtered state for decisions in real time and forecasting; the smoothed state for history (dating breaks, the past output gap)

                              The likelihood: prediction-error decomposition

                              Slide 31 in the PDF

                              The joint density of the observations can be written as a product of one-step conditional densities: \(p(y_1, \dots, y_n) = \prod_t p(y_t \mid Y_{t-1})\). With Gaussian errors, each conditional density is Normal, \(y_t \mid Y_{t-1} \sim N(Z_ta_t, F_t)\), and its mean and variance are exactly the quantities computed by the filter. This gives the prediction error decomposition:

                              \[\begin{aligned} \log L(\theta) = &-\frac{n}{2}\log 2\pi\\ &-\frac12\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(\log F_t + \frac{v_t^2}{F_t}\right). \end{aligned}\]
                              (10.6)

                              Here \(\theta\) collects the model's parameters, for example the variances, and \(v_t\) and \(F_t\) are the prediction errors and their variances, computed by the filter for that value of \(\theta\). The term \(v_t^2/F_t\) penalises prediction errors that are large relative to their variance, and \(\log F_t\) penalises large variances: a good model predicts well and with justified confidence.

                              The computation is simple: one run of the filter gives \(\log L\) for one value of \(\theta\), and a numerical optimiser searches for the value that maximises it. With a diffuse initialisation, the first \(d\) terms of the sum are dropped, because there \(F_t\) is huge; for the local level model \(d = 1\), because a single observation pins down the level (Durbin and Koopman, 2012). The idea is the same as for the exact ARMA likelihood of Chapter 2, now valid for any state space model.

                              In brief

                              • The joint density factorises into one-step conditional densities: \(p(y_1, \dots, y_n) = \prod_t p(y_t \mid Y_{t-1})\)
                                • with Gaussian errors, \(y_t \mid Y_{t-1} \sim N(Z_ta_t, F_t)\): the filter delivers the mean and the variance
                              • Prediction-error decomposition
                                • \(\log L(\theta) = -\dfrac{n}{2}\log 2\pi - \dfrac12\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(\log F_t + \dfrac{v_t^2}{F_t}\right)\)
                                • \(\theta\): the parameters (the variances of the model); \(v_t\), \(F_t\): the prediction errors and their variances, from the filter
                                • one run of the filter gives \(\log L\) for one value of \(\theta\); a numerical optimiser searches over \(\theta\)
                              • Diffuse start: the first \(d\) terms (here \(d = 1\) for the level) are left out, since \(F_t\) is huge there Durbin and Koopman (2012)

                              Maximum likelihood in practice

                              Slide 32 in the PDF

                              Variances must be positive, so the optimisation is carried out over \(\log\sigma^2\), which can take any real value. The likelihood surface can be flat in some directions, so it is prudent to start the optimisation from several initial values. A variance estimated at zero is common and has a clear interpretation: that component is deterministic. For example, a slope variance equal to zero means that the slope of the trend is constant.

                              For the local level model the likelihood can be concentrated. Write \(\sigma^2_\eta = q\sigma^2_\varepsilon\) and run the filter with \(\sigma^2_\varepsilon = 1\); for fixed \(q\), the noise variance then has a closed-form estimate, \(\hat\sigma^2_\varepsilon = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_t v_t^2/F^*_t\), with the sum over the \(n - 1\) non-diffuse terms. Substituting this estimate into the likelihood, the profile log-likelihood depends only on \(q\) and can be plotted, as in the next frame.

                              The standard errors of the estimates come from the numerical Hessian of the log-likelihood. Competing models are compared with the AIC and BIC information criteria, and the adequacy of a model is checked on the standardised prediction errors \(e_t = v_t/\sqrt{F_t}\). The UnobservedComponents, SARIMAX and DynamicFactor classes of statsmodels all use this mechanism.

                              • Optimise over \(\log\sigma^2\) (variances stay positive); several starting values, since the surface can be flat
                                • a variance estimated at zero is common and meaningful: that component is deterministic
                              • Concentrating: for the local level, write \(\sigma^2_\eta = q\sigma^2_\varepsilon\); for fixed \(q\), \(\hat\sigma^2_\varepsilon = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_t v_t^2/F^*_t\) in closed form, with \(v_t\), \(F^*_t\) from the filter run with \(\sigma^2_\varepsilon = 1\), \(\sigma^2_\eta = q\)
                                • the profile log-likelihood is then a function of \(q\) alone (next chart)

                                    Diagnostics

                                    Slide 35 in the PDF

                                    If the model is correctly specified, the standardised prediction errors \(e_t = v_t/\sqrt{F_t}\) are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with the standard Normal distribution. The checks are those of Chapters 1–2: the ACF and the Ljung–Box test for independence, the Jarque–Bera test for normality, and the time plot for extreme values.

                                    State space models also offer a specific tool: the auxiliary residuals of Durbin and Koopman (2012). These are the smoothed disturbances \(\hat\varepsilon_t\) and \(\hat\eta_t\), that is, the estimates of the measurement shocks and the level shocks based on the whole sample, standardised by their standard deviations. Each type of residual detects a different problem. A large \(\hat\varepsilon_t\) points to an outlier in a single observation. A large \(\hat\eta_t\) points to a structural break in the level, a permanent shift.

                                    The auxiliary residuals are computed by a backward recursion after the filter, called the disturbance smoother. They answer a question the prediction errors cannot separate: a large prediction error may come from an extreme year or from the start of a level shift.

                                    • If the model is right, the standardised prediction errors \(e_t = v_t/\sqrt{F_t}\) are i.i.d. \(N(0, 1)\)

                                      Missing values and forecasting

                                      Slide 38 in the PDF

                                      Missing values are simple to handle in the Kalman filter. If \(y_t\) is missing, the update step is skipped and the prediction is made directly from the previous prediction: \(a_{t+1} = Ta_t\) and \(P_{t+1} = TP_tT' + RQR'\). The state variance grows in every period without data. In the likelihood, the term of the missing period is simply dropped. No interpolation by hand is needed.

                                      Forecasting is the same mechanism. The \(h\)-step forecast from the end of the sample means treating \(y_{n+1}, \dots, y_{n+h}\) as missing values. For the local level model the forecast is the same at every horizon, \(\hat y_{n+h} = a_{n+1}\), because the best estimate of a future random walk is its current level. The forecast variance is \(P_{n+1} + (h-1)\sigma^2_\eta + \sigma^2_\varepsilon\): the uncertainty about today's level, plus the level shocks accumulated up to the horizon, plus the noise of the future observation.

                                      The mechanism has many practical uses: irregular data, for example because of holidays, series that start at different dates, mixed frequencies such as monthly and quarterly data in one model, and the ragged edge of a nowcast. The smoother fills the gaps with the estimate \(\hat\alpha_t\) and the corresponding standard error.

                                      In brief

                                      • A missing \(y_t\): no update; \(a_{t+1} = Ta_t\), \(P_{t+1} = TP_tT' + RQR'\)
                                        • the likelihood simply skips that term; nothing is interpolated by hand
                                      • Forecasting \(h\) steps ahead = treating \(y_{n+1}, \dots, y_{n+h}\) as missing
                                        • local level: \(\hat y_{n+h} = a_{n+1}\) for every \(h\), with variance \(P_{n+1} + (h-1)\sigma^2_\eta + \sigma^2_\varepsilon\)
                                      • Uses: irregular data (holidays), series that start at different dates, mixed frequencies, the ragged edge of a nowcast
                                        • the smoother fills the gaps with \(\hat\alpha_t\) and the corresponding standard error

                                        Unobserved components models

                                        Slide 42 in the PDF

                                        The structural time series model, also called the unobserved components (UC) model, of Harvey (1989) decomposes the series into components with a direct interpretation:

                                        \[y_t = \mu_t + \psi_t + \gamma_t + \varepsilon_t.\]

                                        The components are the trend \(\mu_t\), modelled as a local level or a local linear trend, the cycle \(\psi_t\), the seasonal \(\gamma_t\) and the irregular component \(\varepsilon_t\). The decomposition resembles the one in Chapter 0, but there the components were extracted with fixed rules; here each component is a stochastic process with its own variance, estimated from the data.

                                        The cycle can be modelled as a stationary AR(2), \(\psi_t = \phi_1\psi_{t-1} + \phi_2\psi_{t-2} + \kappa_t\), where \(\phi_1\) and \(\phi_2\) are the autoregressive coefficients and \(\kappa_t\) is the cycle shock. An alternative is a damped stochastic cycle with frequency \(\lambda_c\), in radians, hence with period \(2\pi/\lambda_c\). An AR(2) with complex roots also produces oscillations, which makes it suitable for business cycles.

                                        For log GDP, the cycle \(\psi_t\) is the output gap: the percentage deviation of output from trend, interpreted as potential output. A positive gap indicates an economy producing above potential, with the risk of overheating; a negative one indicates idle resources. In Python the model is estimated with UnobservedComponents, with the specification level='smooth trend' and autoregressive=2, and the whole state is estimated with the Kalman filter and smoother.

                                        In brief

                                        • Structural time series or unobserved components (UC) model Harvey (1989): \(y_t = \mu_t + \psi_t + \gamma_t + \varepsilon_t\)
                                          • trend \(\mu_t\) (local level or local linear trend), cycle \(\psi_t\), seasonal \(\gamma_t\), irregular \(\varepsilon_t\)
                                          • the decomposition of Chapter 0, but each component is a stochastic process with its own variance
                                        • The cycle: a stationary AR(2) \(\psi_t = \phi_1\psi_{t-1} + \phi_2\psi_{t-2} + \kappa_t\), or a damped stochastic cycle with period \(2\pi/\lambda_c\)
                                          • \(\phi_1\), \(\phi_2\): the AR coefficients of the cycle; \(\kappa_t\): the cycle shock; \(\lambda_c\): the frequency of the cycle, in radians
                                          • for log GDP, \(\psi_t\) is the output gap: the percentage deviation of output from its trend (potential)
                                        • In Python: UnobservedComponents(y, level='smooth trend', autoregressive=2); the whole state is estimated by the Kalman filter and smoother

                                        The HP filter is a state space smoother

                                        Slide 43 in the PDF

                                        The Hodrick–Prescott (HP) filter of Hodrick and Prescott (1997) chooses the trend \(\tau_t\) that minimises

                                        \[\begin{aligned} &\sum_t(y_t - \tau_t)^2\\ &\quad + \lambda\sum_t(\Delta^2\tau_t)^2, \end{aligned}\]
                                        (10.7)

                                        where \(\Delta^2\tau_t = \tau_t - 2\tau_{t-1} + \tau_{t-2}\) is the change in the slope of the trend and \(\lambda \ge 0\) is the smoothing parameter. The first term asks the trend to stay close to the data, the second asks its slope to change little. For \(\lambda = 0\) the trend is the series itself; as \(\lambda\) grows without bound, the slope can no longer change and the trend becomes a straight line. For quarterly data the conventional value is \(\lambda = 1600\).

                                        Harvey and Jaeger (1993) showed that the HP trend is exactly the smoothed trend of a particular UC model: a smooth trend whose second difference is white noise with variance \(\sigma^2_\zeta\), plus a white-noise irregular with variance \(\sigma^2_\varepsilon\), with \(\lambda = \sigma^2_\varepsilon/\sigma^2_\zeta\). The HP filter thus fixes two things that a UC model would estimate: the ratio of the variances and the assumption that the deviation from trend is white noise, without dynamics of its own.

                                        Being a smoother, the HP filter uses future data. The trend in a given quarter depends on the observations after it, so the last values of the trend and the gap are revised as new quarters arrive. The problem is most serious precisely at the end of the sample, where economic policy decisions are made.

                                        In brief

                                        • Hodrick and Prescott (1997): the trend \(\tau_t\) minimises \(\sum_t(y_t - \tau_t)^2 + \lambda\sum_t(\Delta^2\tau_t)^2\); \(\lambda = 1600\) for quarterly data
                                          • \(\Delta^2\tau_t = \tau_t - 2\tau_{t-1} + \tau_{t-2}\): the change in the slope of the trend; \(\lambda \ge 0\): the smoothing parameter
                                          • large \(\lambda\): a straight-line trend; \(\lambda = 0\): the trend is the series itself
                                        • This is exactly the smoothed trend of a UC model: smooth trend (\(\Delta^2\mu_{t+1} = \zeta_{t-1}\)) plus white noise, with \(\lambda = \sigma^2_\varepsilon/\sigma^2_\zeta\) Harvey and Jaeger (1993)
                                          • the HP filter imposes the signal-to-noise ratio and a white-noise gap; the UC model estimates both
                                        • Being a smoother, HP uses future data: its last values are revised as new quarters arrive

                                        Hamilton (2018): why you should never use the HP filter

                                        Slide 44 in the PDF

                                        Hamilton (2018) raises three objections. First: applied to a random walk, which has no cycles, the HP filter produces a gap with regular cycles. The dynamics of the gap are created by the filter, not by the data. Second: the end of the sample is treated differently from the middle, because at the end the filter has no future data; hence the large revisions and the misleading real-time gaps. Third: the value \(\lambda = 1600\) is a convention, far from the value a likelihood would choose for typical macroeconomic series.

                                        Hamilton's alternative is the regression filter. One estimates by OLS the regression of \(y_{t+h}\) on a constant and on \(y_t, y_{t-1}, y_{t-2}, y_{t-3}\), with \(h = 8\) quarters for quarterly data. The cycle is the regression residual: the part of today's value that could not be predicted two years earlier. The filter is one-sided by construction, because it uses only earlier data, so today's estimate is not revised when new data arrive, apart from re-estimation of the coefficients.

                                        The output gap is never observed, so no method can be checked against the true value. What can be done is a comparison between methods, with the assumptions of each in plain view. The following frames compare the UC model, the HP filter and the Hamilton filter on Romanian GDP.

                                        In brief

                                        • Hamilton (2018) lists three problems
                                          • the HP gap of a random walk has cycles that are not in the data: spurious dynamics
                                          • the end of the sample is treated differently from the middle: large revisions, misleading real-time gaps
                                          • \(\lambda = 1600\) is a convention, far from the value a likelihood would choose
                                        • His alternative, the regression filter: OLS of \(y_{t+h}\) on \(1, y_t, y_{t-1}, y_{t-2}, y_{t-3}\) with \(h = 8\) quarters
                                          • the cycle is the residual: what could not be predicted two years earlier; one-sided by construction
                                        • No method is the truth: we compare the UC model, HP and the Hamilton filter on Romanian GDP

                                                    Dynamic factor models and nowcasting

                                                    Slide 53 in the PDF

                                                    A dynamic factor model describes many series \(y_{1t}, \dots, y_{Nt}\) driven by an unobserved common factor \(f_t\):

                                                    \[\begin{aligned} y_{it} &= \lambda_if_t + u_{it},\\ f_t &= \phi_1f_{t-1} + \phi_2f_{t-2} + \eta_t. \end{aligned}\]

                                                    The factor \(f_t\) is the state. The loading \(\lambda_i\) says how strongly series \(i\) responds to the factor, \(u_{it}\) is the part of the series not explained by the factor, and \(\phi_1\) and \(\phi_2\) are the autoregressive coefficients of the factor. Stock and Watson (1989) built in this way a coincident index of US economic activity from four monthly indicators: instead of choosing a single indicator or an arbitrary average, the model extracts the common movement.

                                                    Nowcasting (Giannone, Reichlin and Small, 2008) uses the same idea to estimate the current quarter before GDP is published. The practical problem is that monthly data are released at different dates: at any given moment, the last months of some series are missing. This structure is called the ragged edge. The Kalman filter treats the values not yet published as missing values and updates the factor with all the data that have already arrived. Central banks use such models to nowcast GDP, and statsmodels provides them through DynamicFactor and DynamicFactorMQ.

                                                    In brief

                                                    • Dynamic factor model: \(y_{it} = \lambda_if_t + u_{it}\),   \(f_t = \phi_1f_{t-1} + \phi_2f_{t-2} + \eta_t\)
                                                      • many series \(y_{1t}, \dots, y_{Nt}\) driven by one unobserved common factor \(f_t\) (the state) with loadings \(\lambda_i\)
                                                      • \(u_{it}\): the part of series \(i\) not explained by the factor; \(\phi_1\), \(\phi_2\): the AR coefficients of the factor
                                                      • Stock and Watson (1989): a coincident index of US activity from four monthly indicators

                                                      Markov switching

                                                      Until now the state has been continuous. In this section the state is a discrete regime, for example recession or expansion, that switches according to a Markov chain. The section defines the model, derives the expected durations of the regimes and their long-run probabilities, builds the Hamilton filter and the Kim smoother, and applies the model to US and Romanian GDP growth and to S&P 500 returns. The last part shows that regimes can imitate GARCH persistence and long memory.

                                                      Hamilton (1989) and the dating of recessions

                                                      Slide 57 in the PDF

                                                      In the US, recessions are dated by a committee of the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The committee announces the start and end of a recession months after the event, based on a judgement over several indicators. The idea behind the dating is that a recession is a regime: while it lasts the economy behaves differently, and GDP grows more slowly or falls.

                                                      Hamilton (1989) turned this idea into a statistical model. The mean of GDP growth depends on an unobserved regime \(S_t \in \{1, 2\}\), and the regime follows a Markov chain: the probability of tomorrow's regime depends only on today's regime. The model estimates, for every quarter, the probability that the economy was in the recession regime, without a committee's judgement.

                                                      The state is now discrete, so the Kalman filter, built for continuous states with linear dynamics, no longer applies. Its place is taken by the Hamilton filter, which tracks the probabilities of the regimes instead of a mean and a variance.

                                                      The Markov-switching model

                                                      Slide 58 in the PDF

                                                      The simplest Markov-switching model has a regime-dependent mean:

                                                      \[\begin{aligned} y_t &= \mu_{S_t} + \varepsilon_t,\\ \varepsilon_t &\sim N(0, \sigma^2_{S_t}),\\ S_t &\in \{1, \dots, k\}. \end{aligned}\]

                                                      In each regime \(j\), the observation has mean \(\mu_j\) and variance \(\sigma^2_j\). The regime follows a Markov chain with transition probabilities \(p_{ij} = \Pr(S_t = j \mid S_{t-1} = i)\), constant over time. The probabilities in each row of the transition matrix sum to 1, because from any regime the process has to go somewhere.

                                                      Hamilton's original model adds autoregressive dynamics, in an MS-AR(4): \(y_t - \mu_{S_t} = \sum_{j=1}^{4}\phi_j(y_{t-j} - \mu_{S_{t-j}}) + \varepsilon_t\). The deviations from the mean of the current regime follow an AR(4) with the same coefficients in both regimes; only the mean switches. The usual variants also let the variance depend on the regime, for calm and turbulent markets, let the AR coefficients depend on the regime, or use \(k = 3\) regimes. In Python the models are estimated with MarkovRegression and MarkovAutoregression from statsmodels.

                                                      The model is nonlinear. The forecast for the next period is an average of the forecasts from each regime, weighted by the regime probabilities, so it depends on how sure the model is that the economy is in one regime or the other.

                                                      In brief

                                                      • Switching mean: \(y_t = \mu_{S_t} + \varepsilon_t\),   \(\varepsilon_t \sim N(0, \sigma^2_{S_t})\),   \(S_t \in \{1, \dots, k\}\)
                                                        • Markov chain: \(p_{ij} = \Pr(S_t = j \mid S_{t-1} = i)\), constant over time; the rows of the transition matrix sum to 1
                                                      • Hamilton's MS-AR(4): \(y_t - \mu_{S_t} = \sum_{j=1}^{4}\phi_j(y_{t-j} - \mu_{S_{t-j}}) + \varepsilon_t\)
                                                        • the AR dynamics stay the same; only the mean switches
                                                      • Variants: switching variance (calm and turbulent markets), switching AR coefficients, \(k = 3\) regimes
                                                        • in Python: MarkovRegression, MarkovAutoregression (statsmodels)
                                                      • A nonlinear model: the forecast depends on the probability of each regime

                                                      Transition matrix, durations and ergodic probabilities

                                                      Slide 59 in the PDF

                                                      With two regimes, the transition matrix is

                                                      \[\mathbf{P} = \begin{pmatrix}p_{11} & 1 - p_{11}\\ 1 - p_{22} & p_{22}\end{pmatrix},\]

                                                      and the \(h\)-step transition probabilities are the elements of \(\mathbf{P}^h\). The diagonal holds the probabilities of staying in the same regime, which determine how persistent the regimes are.

                                                      The duration of a spell in regime \(i\) has a geometric distribution: the process stays in the regime with probability \(p_{ii}\) in each period and leaves with probability \(1 - p_{ii}\), so \(\Pr(D = d) = p_{ii}^{d-1}(1 - p_{ii})\). The mean of this distribution gives the expected duration:

                                                      \[E(D_i) = \frac{1}{1 - p_{ii}}.\]
                                                      (10.8)

                                                      For example, with \(p_{11} = 0.75\) recessions last on average \(1/0.25 = 4\) quarters; with \(p_{22} = 0.95\) expansions last on average 20 quarters.

                                                      The ergodic probabilities are the long-run fractions of time spent in each regime. For regime 1, \(\pi_1 = (1 - p_{22})/(2 - p_{11} - p_{22})\). With the values of the example, \(\pi_1 = 0.05/0.30 = 1/6\): in the long run, one quarter in six is a recession quarter. The result has a simple interpretation: recessions last 4 quarters and expansions 20, so in each cycle of 24 quarters, 4 are recession quarters.

                                                      In brief

                                                      • Two regimes: \(\mathbf{P} = \begin{pmatrix}p_{11} & 1 - p_{11}\\ 1 - p_{22} & p_{22}\end{pmatrix}\)
                                                        • \(h\)-step transitions: \(\mathbf{P}^h\)
                                                      • Expected duration of regime \(i\): the time spent there is geometric, \(\Pr(D = d) = p_{ii}^{d-1}(1 - p_{ii})\), so \(E(D) = 1/(1 - p_{ii})\)
                                                        • \(p_{11} = 0.75\): recessions last \(1/0.25 = 4\) quarters on average; \(p_{22} = 0.95\): expansions last 20 quarters
                                                      • Ergodic (long-run) probabilities: \(\pi_1 = \dfrac{1 - p_{22}}{2 - p_{11} - p_{22}}\), the share of time spent in regime 1
                                                        • example: \(\pi_1 = 0.05/0.30 = 1/6\): one quarter in six is a recession quarter

                                                      The Hamilton filter

                                                      Slide 60 in the PDF

                                                      The Hamilton filter tracks the filtered probabilities \(\Pr(S_t = j \mid Y_t)\) for each regime \(j\). As with the Kalman filter, each period has a prediction step and an update step. The prediction uses the transition matrix: the probability of being in regime \(j\) today, given the past, is \(\Pr(S_t = j \mid Y_{t-1}) = \sum_i p_{ij}\Pr(S_{t-1} = i \mid Y_{t-1})\).

                                                      The update uses Bayes' rule. The density of the observation in regime \(j\) is \(f_j(y_t) = \varphi\bigl((y_t - \mu_j)/\sigma_j\bigr)/\sigma_j\), where \(\varphi\) is the density of the standard Normal distribution and \(\mu_j\) and \(\sigma_j\) are the mean and standard deviation of the regime. The updated probability is proportional to the predicted probability multiplied by this density:

                                                      \[\begin{aligned} &\Pr(S_t = j \mid Y_t)\\ &\quad = \frac{\Pr(S_t = j \mid Y_{t-1})\,f_j(y_t)}{\sum_i\Pr(S_t = i \mid Y_{t-1})\,f_i(y_t)}. \end{aligned}\]
                                                      (10.9)

                                                      A regime gains probability when today's observation is plausible in that regime. A strongly negative GDP growth rate has a high density in the recession regime and a low one in the expansion regime, so it shifts probability towards recession.

                                                      The denominator of (10.9) is the predictive density \(p(y_t \mid Y_{t-1})\), so the log-likelihood is \(\sum_t\log p(y_t \mid Y_{t-1})\), the same construction as with the Kalman filter. The prediction–update cycle is the same; only the quantities carried are probabilities rather than means and variances. The filter starts from the ergodic probabilities \(\pi_j\).

                                                      In brief

                                                      • Track \(\xi_{t|t} = \Pr(S_t = j \mid Y_t)\), the filtered probabilities, for each regime \(j\)
                                                        • prediction: \(\Pr(S_t = j \mid Y_{t-1}) = \sum_i p_{ij}\Pr(S_{t-1} = i \mid Y_{t-1})\)
                                                        • densities: \(f_j(y_t) = \varphi\bigl((y_t - \mu_j)/\sigma_j\bigr)/\sigma_j\), the likelihood of \(y_t\) in each regime
                                                        • \(\varphi\): the standard Normal density; \(\mu_j\), \(\sigma_j\): the mean and standard deviation of regime \(j\)
                                                        • update (Bayes): \(\Pr(S_t = j \mid Y_t) = \dfrac{\Pr(S_t = j \mid Y_{t-1})\,f_j(y_t)}{\sum_i\Pr(S_t = i \mid Y_{t-1})\,f_i(y_t)}\)
                                                      • The denominator is \(p(y_t \mid Y_{t-1})\): the log-likelihood is \(\sum_t\log p(y_t \mid Y_{t-1})\), as for the Kalman filter
                                                        • the same predict–update cycle, with probabilities instead of means and variances
                                                      • Start: the ergodic probabilities \(\pi_j\)

                                                      Smoothed probabilities and estimation

                                                      Slide 61 in the PDF

                                                      The smoothed probabilities \(\Pr(S_t = j \mid Y_n)\) use the whole sample. They are computed by a backward pass after the filter, proposed by Kim (1994), the analogue of the RTS smoother for discrete states. The smoothed probabilities are used for the historical dating of regimes, while the filtered probabilities show what a forecaster knew at time \(t\).

                                                      The parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood, either by direct numerical optimisation or by the EM (expectation–maximisation) algorithm of Hamilton (1990). The expectation step computes the smoothed probabilities for the current parameters. The maximisation step re-estimates the means, variances and transition probabilities as averages weighted by these probabilities; for example, \(p_{11}\) becomes the expected number of transitions from regime 1 to regime 1 divided by the expected number of periods in regime 1. The two steps are repeated until convergence.

                                                      The likelihood of a Markov-switching model often has several local maxima, so estimation must be started from many random initial values and the solutions compared. A second problem is label switching: the likelihood does not change if regimes 1 and 2 swap names, so the label "regime 1" has no meaning in itself. Regimes must be named after their characteristics, for example the lower mean or the higher variance.

                                                      In brief

                                                      • Smoothed probabilities \(\Pr(S_t = j \mid Y_n)\): a backward pass Kim (1994), the analogue of the RTS smoother
                                                        • used to date regimes in history; the filtered probabilities are what a forecaster knew at time \(t\)
                                                      • Estimation: maximum likelihood by numerical optimisation, or the EM algorithm Hamilton (1990)
                                                        • EM: expectation (smoothed probabilities given \(\theta\)) and maximisation (weighted means, variances, transition counts), repeated
                                                      • The likelihood has several local maxima: use many random starting values and compare the solutions
                                                        • label switching: regime 1 and regime 2 can swap; name them by their means or variances

                                                                          Regimes, GARCH persistence and long memory

                                                                          Slide 72 in the PDF

                                                                          Lamoureux and Lastrapes (1990) showed that shifts in the level of the variance make a GARCH model look nearly integrated, with \(\alpha + \beta\) close to 1. A GARCH fitted to a series with two variance levels interprets the move from one level to the other as a very persistent shock. The near-IGARCH estimates of Chapter 5 may therefore reflect regimes rather than a single, very persistent process. Hamilton and Susmel (1994) proposed an ARCH model within each regime, SWARCH, and found that the estimated persistence falls substantially once the regimes are modelled explicitly.

                                                                          Diebold and Inoue (2001) showed a related result for long memory. Rare regime switches produce a slowly decaying autocorrelation function and a positive estimate of the fractional differencing parameter \(\hat d\), exactly the signs of long memory from Chapter 8, even though the process has no long memory.

                                                                          Three models, highly persistent GARCH, long memory and regime switching, thus describe the same fact: volatility shocks persist. The next frame simulates the regime model estimated for the S&P 500, without GARCH and without long memory, to see how much of this persistence the regimes alone can explain.

                                                                          In brief

                                                                          • Lamoureux and Lastrapes (1990): shifts in the level of variance make a GARCH look almost integrated (\(\alpha + \beta \approx 1\))
                                                                            • the near-IGARCH estimates of Chapter 5 may reflect regimes, not one very persistent process
                                                                            • Hamilton and Susmel (1994): ARCH within regimes (SWARCH) lowers the persistence
                                                                          • Diebold and Inoue (2001): rare regime switches produce a slowly decaying ACF and \(\hat d > 0\) (Chapter 8, spurious long memory)
                                                                            • three models, one fact: volatility shocks persist; the next chart simulates the fitted regime model to see how far regimes alone go

                                                                              Section summary

                                                                              Markov-switching models replace the continuous state with a discrete regime, and the Hamilton filter replaces the Kalman filter. For the US, the model dates the deep recessions without false alarms, but in real time it recognises the 2008 recession only at the end of the year. For Romania, the regimes combine weak growth with large fluctuations, and for the S&P 500 they separate calm markets from turbulent ones. Interpreting the regimes requires checks: multiple maxima, outliers and variance breaks can change the meaning of a regime completely, and regimes can imitate GARCH persistence and long memory.

                                                                              Key takeaways

                                                                              Slide 78 in the PDF

                                                                              The state space form describes a hidden state \(\alpha_t\) that follows a VAR(1) and is measured with noise; ARMA, ETS, UC, TVP and factor models can all be written this way. The Kalman filter alternates prediction and update, and its gain weighs the data against the model. The steady-state filter of the local level model is simple exponential smoothing.

                                                                              Smoothing is for history and filtering for real time. The likelihood is built from the filter's prediction errors, and missing values and forecasts are handled by skipping the update step. Output gaps depend on the method and are heavily revised at the end of the sample. In Markov-switching models, the regime probabilities and the durations \(1/(1 - p_{ii})\) are read only after checking what the regimes mean.

                                                                              Section summary

                                                                              The chapter treated unobserved quantities as hidden states. The state space form and the Kalman filter estimate continuous states, such as the level of the Nile, the trend and cycle of GDP, a time-varying beta or a common factor, provide the likelihood and handle missing values and forecasts.
