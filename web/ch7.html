Today's question and route

Slide 2 in the PDF

The question of the chapter starts from two prices that both move like random walks. Each of them, taken alone, cannot be forecast over long horizons. A combination of them, for example their difference or a ratio, may nonetheless be stable over time. If so, the series share a stochastic trend and a long-run equilibrium, and a regression in levels estimates a real relation, not a spurious one.

The route has three parts. The first defines cointegration and tests it with the Engle–Granger method, using the critical values that fit it, then builds the error correction model. The second moves to systems: the VECM, the Granger representation theorem, the Johansen tests, the interpretation of the coefficients and forecasting. The third applies the tools: purchasing power parity, interest rates, three Central European currencies and pairs trading on the Bucharest Stock Exchange. The chapter builds on Chapter 3, for unit roots and spurious regression, and on Chapter 6, for VAR models.

Chapter 3 established two facts that seem to close the discussion of series with a stochastic trend. Many economic and financial series are integrated of order 1, \(I(1)\): they become stationary only after one difference. And the regression of one \(I(1)\) series on another, unrelated \(I(1)\) series often produces a spurious result, with large \(t\)-statistics and a large \(R^2\). The cautious conclusion would be to always work in differences. This chapter shows why that conclusion is too hasty.

There is an important case in which two or more \(I(1)\) series are tied together in the long run: some linear combination of them is stationary. The series can drift apart for a while, but the distance between them keeps returning towards an equilibrium level. This case is called cointegration. Granger (1981) formulated the idea, and Engle and Granger (1987) gave the definition, the two-step estimation method and the link to error correction models. Differencing the data would erase exactly this information about the long run.

The chapter builds three tools: the residual-based Engle–Granger test, the single-equation error correction model, and the vector error correction model (VECM), with the Johansen tests for the number of equilibrium relations. It applies them to US Treasury yields, to US consumption and income, to purchasing power parity for the leu, to ROBOR and Euribor, to three Central European currencies and to a pairs-trading strategy on the Bucharest Stock Exchange. The results are mixed, and learning to read a negative result is one of the lessons of the chapter.

The idea of cointegration

The section starts from the problem of spurious regression and shows the case in which a regression between \(I(1)\) series makes sense. The intuition comes from a well-known story, a drunk walking her dog, checked afterwards by simulation. Then come the formal definition of cointegration, its representation through common stochastic trends, the examples from economic theory and the first tests on four pairs of real series.

From spurious regression to cointegration

Slide 5 in the PDF

Granger and Newbold (1974) showed, by simulation, that regressing one random walk on another, independent random walk mostly gives a large \(t\)-statistic, a large \(R^2\) and a Durbin–Watson statistic close to 0. The result is a spurious regression, discussed in Chapter 3. The cause is the regression error: whatever the slope \(b\), the combination \(y_t - a - bx_t\) still contains the stochastic trend of \(y_t\) and that of \(x_t\), so it is itself \(I(1)\). Nothing ties the two series together, and the usual tests, which assume stationary errors, are no longer valid.

The exception is the case in which, for one particular value of \(b\), the combination \(u_t = y_t - a - bx_t\) is stationary. The stochastic trends of the two series then cancel exactly in this combination, and the series are called cointegrated (Granger, 1981; Engle and Granger, 1987). The regression in levels is no longer spurious: it estimates a real long-run relation. The two series can drift apart for a while, but \(u_t\), being stationary, returns towards its mean and brings them back towards each other. In this sense, \(u_t = 0\) describes an equilibrium.

The rest of the chapter answers three questions about this case: how to recognise it in the data, how to test formally whether a combination is stationary, and how to build a model that uses the information in the equilibrium.

  • Chapter 3: regressing one \(I(1)\) series on another, unrelated \(I(1)\) series gives large \(t\), high \(R^2\) and a Durbin–Watson statistic near 0 Granger and Newbold (1974)
    • the error \(y_t - a - b x_t\) is itself \(I(1)\) for every \(b\): nothing ties the two series together
  • The exception: for one value of \(b\), the combination \(u_t = y_t - a - b x_t\) is stationary

A drunk and her dog

Slide 6 in the PDF

Murray (1994) gave an illustration that has become a classic. A drunk leaves the pub and wanders aimlessly: her position \(x_t\) is a random walk, each step being unpredictable. Her dog wanders too, so its position \(y_t\) is also a random walk. If the two were independent passers-by, the distance between them would be the difference of two random walks, hence again a random walk, and its standard deviation would grow without bound, in proportion to \(\sqrt{t}\).

The drunk and the dog are not independent, however. When they drift apart, the dog hears her call and runs back, and she hears the dog and turns towards it. Each closes part of the distance at every step: \(\Delta x_t = c\,(y_{t-1} - x_{t-1}) + u_t\) and \(\Delta y_t = d\,(x_{t-1} - y_{t-1}) + w_t\), where \(c, d > 0\) are the fractions of the distance closed at each step and \(u_t\) and \(w_t\) are independent shocks. The terms in \(c\) and \(d\) are error correction terms.

The result combines two properties that seem contradictory. Each path remains unpredictable, because the shocks accumulate, but the distance \(y_t - x_t\) stays bounded, because it is corrected at every step. This is, in a single picture, the idea of the chapter: cointegration and error correction are two sides of the same phenomenon.

    Interpreting the simulation: the distances

    Slide 8 in the PDF

    Unit root tests confirm what the chart shows. The augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test applied to the drunk's path gives a p-value of 0.21, so the unit root is not rejected, as expected for a random walk. The distance to her own dog, however, is an AR(1) process: subtracting the two equations, \(y_t - x_t = (1 - c - d)(y_{t-1} - x_{t-1}) + (w_t - u_t)\), with coefficient \(\rho = 1 - 0.15 - 0.25 = 0.6\). The ADF test on this distance gives a p-value below 0.001, and its standard deviation is 1.9. The distance to the stray dog has an ADF p-value of 0.81 and a standard deviation of 14.1, so it behaves like a random walk.

    The coefficient \(\rho = 0.6\) also tells how fast a distance closes. After \(h\) steps without new shocks a fraction \(0.6^h\) of it remains, so half of it has gone after \(h = \ln 0.5/\ln 0.6 = 1.4\) steps. This quantity is the half-life, which returns in Section 3 for the estimated models.

    The practical consequence matters. Neither the drunk's path nor the dog's can be forecast over long horizons, but knowing where the drunk is tells us where to look for the dog. Cointegration does not make the series predictable; it makes the relation between them predictable.

    In brief

    • Each path is \(I(1)\): ADF on \(x_t\) gives p = 0.21
      • the distance \(y_t - x_t\) is an AR(1) with \(\rho = 1 - c - d = 0.6\): ADF p \(<\) 0.001, standard deviation 1.9
      • the distance to the stray dog wanders: ADF p = 0.81, standard deviation 14.1
    • Half of a gap disappears in \(\ln 0.5/\ln 0.6 = 1.4\) steps: the half-life (Section 3)

    Definition of cointegration

    Slide 9 in the PDF

    The definition of Engle and Granger (1987) applies to a vector of \(n\) series, \(\mathbf y_t = (y_{1t}, \dots, y_{nt})^\top\). Its components are cointegrated of order (1,1), written \(\mathbf y_t \sim CI(1,1)\), if two conditions hold. First, each component is \(I(1)\), that is, it becomes stationary after one difference, as in Chapter 3. Second, there is a vector \(\beta \neq 0\) for which the linear combination \(\beta^\top\mathbf y_t\) is \(I(0)\), that is, stationary. The first 1 in the notation gives the order of integration of the series, the second by how much this order falls in the combination.

    The vector \(\beta\) is called the cointegrating vector, and \(u_t = \beta^\top\mathbf y_t - \mu\), where \(\mu\) is the mean of the combination, is called the equilibrium error. The vector is not unique: if \(\beta^\top\mathbf y_t\) is stationary, so is \(2\beta^\top\mathbf y_t\). One coefficient is therefore normalised to 1; with two variables one usually writes \(\beta = (1, -b)^\top\), which corresponds to the relation \(y_t = a + bx_t + u_t\).

    With \(n\) variables there can be several linearly independent cointegrating vectors, but at most \(n - 1\); their number \(r\) is called the cointegrating rank. Cointegration is a property of the levels: if all series are differenced before modelling, the combination \(\beta^\top\mathbf y_t\) disappears from the data and, with it, the information about the equilibrium.

    In brief

    • Definition Engle and Granger (1987): the components of \(\mathbf y_t = (y_{1t}, \dots, y_{nt})^\top\) are cointegrated of order (1,1), \(\mathbf y_t \sim CI(1,1)\), if
      • every component is \(I(1)\) (Chapter 3: stationary after one difference), and
      • some linear combination \(\beta^\top \mathbf y_t\), with \(\beta \neq 0\), is \(I(0)\): stationary
    • \(\beta\) is the cointegrating vector; \(u_t = \beta^\top \mathbf y_t - \mu\) is the equilibrium error (\(\mu\): its mean; \(\top\): transpose)
      • \(\beta\) is unique only up to scale: \(2\beta\) works as well; we normalise one coefficient to 1, e.g. \(\beta = (1, -b)^\top\)
      • with \(n\) variables there can be up to \(r = n - 1\) independent cointegrating vectors; \(r\) is the cointegration rank
    • Cointegration is a property of the levels: differencing the data destroys the information in \(\beta^\top \mathbf y_t\)

    Common stochastic trends

    Slide 10 in the PDF

    A simple example shows the mechanism. Let \(x_t = x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) be a random walk and \(y_t = bx_t + u_t\), with \(u_t\) stationary. Both series contain the same stochastic trend, the sum of past shocks \(\sum_{s \le t}\varepsilon_s\): \(x_t\) contains it with weight 1, \(y_t\) with weight \(b\). The combination \(y_t - bx_t = u_t\) removes it completely and stays stationary. Cointegration therefore means that the series are driven by shared trends, not each by its own.

    Stock and Watson (1988) turned this observation into a general common trends representation: \(n\) variables with \(r\) cointegrating vectors are driven by \(n - r\) common stochastic trends. The two extremes are instructive. With \(r = 0\) there are \(n\) separate trends and no cointegration. With \(r = n - 1\) there is a single trend, shared by all the series. The Treasury yields at 1, 5 and 10 years, with \(r = 2\), have a single common trend, the general level of interest rates, as Section 5 shows.

    The worked example uses three series: \(x_t\) a random walk, \(y_t = 2x_t + u_t\) and \(z_t = -x_t + v_t\), with \(u_t\) and \(v_t\) stationary. All three are driven by the same trend, so \(n = 3\), \(n - r = 1\) and \(r = 2\). In the order \((x_t, y_t, z_t)\), two cointegrating vectors are \(\beta_1 = (-2, 1, 0)^\top\), because \(y_t - 2x_t = u_t\), and \(\beta_2 = (0, 1, 2)^\top\), because \(y_t + 2z_t = 2x_t + u_t - 2x_t + 2v_t = u_t + 2v_t\). Any linear combination of the two vectors is again a cointegrating vector; the rank says how many independent directions exist.

    In brief

    • Write \(x_t = x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) (a random walk) and \(y_t = b\,x_t + u_t\), with \(u_t\) stationary
      • both series contain the same stochastic trend \(\sum_{s \le t} \varepsilon_s\); \(y_t - b x_t = u_t\) removes it
    • Common trends representation Stock and Watson (1988): \(n\) variables with \(r\) cointegrating vectors are driven by \(n - r\) common stochastic trends
      • \(r = 0\): \(n\) separate trends, no cointegration; \(r = n - 1\): one trend shared by all
      • example: three yields (1, 5 and 10 years) with \(r = 2\) share one trend, the level of interest rates (Section 5)
    • Worked example: \(x_t\) a random walk, \(y_t = 2x_t + u_t\), \(z_t = -x_t + v_t\) (\(u_t\), \(v_t\) stationary)
      • \(n = 3\), one common trend, so \(r = 2\): in the order \((x_t, y_t, z_t)\), e.g. \(\beta_1 = (-2, 1, 0)^\top\) and \(\beta_2 = (0, 1, 2)^\top\) (since \(y_t - 2x_t = u_t\) and \(y_t + 2z_t = u_t + 2v_t\))

    Where economics predicts cointegration

    Slide 11 in the PDF

    Many economic theories state that some combination of non-stationary variables must be stable. Purchasing power parity (PPP) writes the log exchange rate as \(s_t = p_t - p_t^* + q_t\), where \(s_t\) is the log exchange rate (lei per euro), \(p_t\) and \(p_t^*\) are the logs of the domestic and foreign price levels, and \(q_t\) is the real exchange rate. The theory requires \(q_t\) to be stationary, which means cointegration of \(s_t\), \(p_t\) and \(p_t^*\); Section 8 tests this requirement for the leu.

    The term structure of interest rates gives the second example. Under the expectations hypothesis, a long-term yield is the average of expected short-term yields plus a premium; the difference (the spread) between the long and the short yield is then stationary (Campbell and Shiller, 1987). The third example is the "great ratios": if consumption follows permanent income, the ratio of consumption to income, \(c_t - y_t\) in logs, is stable.

    The last examples come from financial markets. Arbitrage keeps small the difference between the spot price and the futures price of the same asset, or between an index and a fund that tracks it. The shares of similar firms are exposed to the same sector shocks and may share a trend; this is the basis of the pairs-trading strategy in Section 9. In every case, theory proposes a relation and the data must confirm it.

    In brief

    • Purchasing power parity: \(s_t = p_t - p^*_t + q_t\), with the real exchange rate \(q_t\) stationary (Section 8)
      • \(s_t\): log exchange rate (lei per euro); \(p_t\), \(p^*_t\): log price levels at home and abroad
    • Term structure of interest rates: by the expectations hypothesis, the spread between a long and a short yield is stationary Campbell and Shiller (1987)
    • Consumption and income: the “great ratio” \(c_t - y_t\) (logs) is stable if consumption follows permanent income
    • Spot and futures prices, an index and a fund that tracks it: arbitrage keeps the gap small
    • Shares of similar firms: the same sector shocks; the basis of pairs trading (Section 9)

          Interpreting the four pairs

          Slide 13 in the PDF

          Each of the eight series is \(I(1)\) according to the tests of Chapter 3. The question is whether the two series of each pair move together in the long run, and the answer comes from the Engle–Granger test of Section 2. Its statistic, \(\tau\), is compared with a 5% critical value of about \(-3.34\); a more negative value rejects the null hypothesis of no cointegration.

          For the 10-year yield on the 3-month yield, \(\tau = -3.35\) with p-value 0.048, a borderline result. For consumption and income, \(\tau = -3.54\) with p-value 0.029, and for the two banks \(\tau = -4.33\) with p-value 0.002: these are cointegrated in the sample since 2014.           Recap: The idea of cointegration

          Slide 15 in the PDF

          \(I(1)\) series are cointegrated if a linear combination \(\beta^\top\mathbf y_t\) is stationary; the combination describes a long-run equilibrium, and deviations from it are corrected. An equivalent statement is that the series share stochastic trends: \(n\) variables with \(r\) cointegrating vectors are driven by \(n - r\) trends. Economic theory suggests cointegration in many places, from parity conditions to spreads and "great ratios", but the data must confirm it through a test.

          In brief

          • \(I(1)\) series are cointegrated if a combination \(\beta^\top \mathbf y_t\) is stationary: a long-run equilibrium
          • Equivalently, they share common stochastic trends: \(n\) variables, \(r\) vectors, \(n - r\) trends
          • Theory suggests cointegration (parity conditions, spreads, great ratios), but the data must confirm it

          Section summary

          Cointegration is the exception to the rule of spurious regression: series that, taken alone, are unpredictable random walks can be tied by a stationary combination. The simulation of the drunk and her dog shows that a distance corrected at every step stays bounded although both paths wander. On real data, the Engle–Granger test finds cointegration for consumption and income and for two Romanian banks, a borderline result for two US yields and no evidence for EUR/RON and EUR/HUF.           The Engle–Granger two-step method

          The method of Engle and Granger (1987) is the simplest way to test two series for cointegration: a regression in levels, followed by a unit root test on the residuals. The simplicity hides a trap: the critical values are no longer the Dickey–Fuller ones. The section explains why, obtains them by simulation, presents the alternative Phillips–Ouliaris test, applies both tests to real data and closes with the limits of the method.

          Two steps

          Slide 16 in the PDF

          Step 1 estimates the long-run relation by ordinary least squares (OLS) in levels, \(y_t = a + bx_t + u_t\), and keeps the residuals \(\hat u_t = y_t - \hat a - \hat bx_t\). If the series are cointegrated, the estimator \(\hat b\) has an unusual property: it is super-consistent, meaning that its error shrinks like \(1/T\), faster than the usual rate \(1/\sqrt{T}\). The intuition is that any wrong value of \(b\) leaves a stochastic trend in the residuals, whose variance then grows with the sample; OLS, which minimises this variance, finds the right value very quickly.

          Step 2 applies an ADF test, as in Chapter 3, to the step-1 residuals, without a constant, because OLS residuals already have mean zero:

          \[\begin{gathered} \Delta\hat u_t = \gamma\,\hat u_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{k}\delta_j\,\Delta\hat u_{t-j} \\ + e_t, \\ H_0: \gamma = 0 \ \text{(no cointegration)}, \\ H_1: \gamma < 0 \ \text{(cointegration)}. \end{gathered}\]
          (7.1)

          Here \(\delta_j\) are the coefficients of the \(k\) lagged differences, which absorb the autocorrelation of \(\hat u_t\), and \(e_t\) is white noise. The null hypothesis \(\gamma = 0\) states that the residuals have a unit root, so there is no cointegration; the alternative \(\gamma < 0\) states that the residuals revert towards zero. The test statistic is \(\tau = \hat\gamma/\mathrm{SE}(\hat\gamma)\), where SE is the standard error, and the null is rejected when \(\tau\) is below the critical value.

          The test has a precondition: each series must be \(I(1)\). An \(I(0)\) series and an \(I(1)\) series cannot be cointegrated, because any combination with a non-zero coefficient on the \(I(1)\) series remains \(I(1)\). That is why every cointegration analysis starts with unit root tests on each series.

          In brief

          • Step 1: estimate the long-run relation by OLS (ordinary least squares) in levels: \(y_t = a + b\,x_t + u_t\)
            • keep the residuals \(\hat u_t = y_t - \hat a - \hat b x_t\)
            • if the series are cointegrated, \(\hat b\) is super-consistent: its error shrinks like \(1/T\), faster than the usual \(1/\sqrt{T}\)
          • Step 2: an ADF test (Chapter 3) on the residuals, without a constant: \(\Delta\hat u_t = \gamma\,\hat u_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{k}\delta_j\Delta\hat u_{t-j} + e_t\)
            • \(H_0\): \(\gamma = 0\), the residuals have a unit root: no cointegration; \(H_1\): \(\gamma < 0\): cointegration
            • \(\delta_j\): coefficients of the \(k\) lagged differences, which absorb the autocorrelation of \(\hat u_t\); \(e_t\): white noise
            • statistic \(\tau = \hat\gamma/\mathrm{SE}(\hat\gamma)\), SE: standard error; reject \(H_0\) when \(\tau\) is below the critical value
          • Pretest: each series must be \(I(1)\); cointegration between an \(I(0)\) and an \(I(1)\) series is impossible

          The reason the Dickey–Fuller table is wrong here

          Slide 17 in the PDF

          The step-1 residuals are not an observed series but the result of an optimisation. OLS chooses \(\hat b\) so that the variance of the residuals is as small as possible, that is, so that \(\hat u_t\) looks as "calm" as possible. Even when the series are not cointegrated, \(\hat u_t\) therefore looks more stationary than the true error \(u_t\). Under the null, the statistic \(\tau\) is pushed towards negative values, and its distribution lies to the left of the Dickey–Fuller distribution.

          The right critical values depend on the number of variables \(n\) in the regression and on the deterministic terms in step 1 (a constant, or a constant and a trend). Engle and Granger (1987) obtained them by simulation; MacKinnon (1994) and MacKinnon (2010) then gave accurate approximations, for any sample size, which the coint function of statsmodels uses.

          There is one case in which the Dickey–Fuller table is the right one: when the cointegrating vector is known from theory. If theory says that the spread between two rates is stationary, that is \(\beta = (1, -1)^\top\), the spread is computed directly, without any estimation, and the ordinary ADF test, with its own table, is applied to this observed series.

          In brief

          • OLS chooses \(\hat b\) to make the residual variance as small as possible
            • so \(\hat u_t\) looks more stationary than the true \(u_t\), even when there is no cointegration
            • under \(H_0\) the statistic \(\tau\) is shifted to the left of the Dickey–Fuller distribution
          • The critical values depend on the number of variables \(n\) and on the deterministic terms of step 1
            • tables: Engle and Granger (1987) (by simulation), then MacKinnon (1994) and MacKinnon (2010), used by statsmodels (coint)
          • With \(\beta\) known from theory (e.g. a spread, \(\beta = (1, -1)^\top\)), nothing is estimated: the plain ADF test and its table apply

              The Phillips–Ouliaris test

              Slide 21 in the PDF

              Phillips and Ouliaris (1990) proposed a test with the same step 1 and the same null hypothesis, no cointegration. The difference is in step 2: the Dickey–Fuller regression on the residuals is estimated without lagged differences, and the autocorrelation of the residuals is handled by correcting the \(t\)-statistic with the long-run variance of the residuals, as in the Phillips–Perron test of Chapter 3. The test has two versions: \(Z_t\), the corrected \(t\)-ratio, and \(Z_\alpha\), the corrected statistic \(T\hat\gamma\). The asymptotic critical values of \(Z_t\) are the same as for the Engle–Granger test.

              Using both tests has a practical reason. The Engle–Granger test depends on the number of lags \(k\), usually chosen by the Akaike information criterion (AIC), while the Phillips–Ouliaris test depends on the bandwidth used for the long-run variance. The two choices handle the same autocorrelation in different ways. When the tests agree, the verdict is robust; when they disagree, the residual plot and the sample must be examined.

              In Python, the Engle–Granger test is coint(y, x) from statsmodels, and the Phillips–Ouliaris test is phillips_ouliaris(y, x, test_type="Zt") from arch.

              In brief

              • Phillips and Ouliaris (1990): the same step 1 and the same \(H_0\) (no cointegration)
                • step 2: a Dickey–Fuller regression without lagged differences; the \(t\)-statistic is corrected with the long-run variance of the residuals (as in the Phillips–Perron test of Chapter 3)
                • two versions: \(Z_t\) (corrected \(t\)-ratio) and \(Z_\alpha\) (corrected \(T\hat\gamma\)); the critical values of \(Z_t\) are those of Engle–Granger
              • Reasons for two tests
                • Engle–Granger depends on the number of lags \(k\) chosen by AIC; Phillips–Ouliaris depends on the bandwidth of the long-run variance
                • when they agree, the verdict is robust; when they disagree, look at the residual plot and at the sample

                        Limits of the Engle–Granger method

                        Slide 27 in the PDF

                        The method has four limits. First, it can find at most one cointegrating vector. With two variables this is not a problem, but with \(n \ge 3\) variables there may be several vectors, and a single regression estimates only one combination of them (Sections 4–5). Second, the results depend on the variable placed on the left, as the bank pair showed.

                        The third limit concerns inference on \(\hat b\). The estimator is super-consistent but biased in small samples, and the \(t\)-statistics reported by OLS in step 1 are not valid, because the errors are autocorrelated and correlated with the changes in \(x_t\). One remedy is the dynamic OLS (DOLS) estimator of Stock and Watson (1993), which adds leads and lags of \(\Delta x_t\) to the regression and gives valid standard errors.

                        The fourth limit comes from the two-step structure: estimation errors in step 1 carry over into step 2, and the power of the test is low when adjustment to equilibrium is slow. The Johansen method of Section 5 avoids these problems by estimating everything at once, by maximum likelihood, in a system.

                        In brief

                        • At most one cointegrating vector: with \(n \ge 3\) variables there may be several (Sections 4–5)
                        • Normalisation: the results depend on the variable put on the left
                        • Inference on \(\hat b\): super-consistent, but biased in small samples, and its OLS \(t\)-statistics are not valid
                          • remedy: dynamic OLS (DOLS) Stock and Watson (1993), adding leads and lags of \(\Delta x_t\), gives valid standard errors
                        • Two-step errors: mistakes in step 1 carry over to step 2; the power is low against slow adjustment
                          • the Johansen method (Section 5) estimates everything at once, by maximum likelihood

                        The Engle–Granger test is simple, but it needs the right table: because OLS makes the residuals look stationary, the Dickey–Fuller critical values would find cointegration between independent series three to six times more often than they promise.                         Error correction models

                        If two series are cointegrated, yesterday's equilibrium error contains information about today's changes. The error correction model (ECM) puts this information into a regression in differences. The section defines the model, translates the adjustment coefficient into a half-life, estimates ECMs for US consumption and for the 10-year yield and presents the DHSY consumption function, the first famous application of this form.

                        The error correction model

                        Slide 30 in the PDF

                        Let \(u_{t-1} = y_{t-1} - a - bx_{t-1}\) be the equilibrium error of the previous period. The error correction model explains the change in \(y_t\) by the current and past changes of the variables and by this error:

                        \[\begin{aligned} \Delta y_t = {} & c + \gamma\,u_{t-1} + \delta_0\,\Delta x_t \\ & + \sum_{j \ge 1}\left(\phi_j\,\Delta y_{t-j} + \delta_j\,\Delta x_{t-j}\right) \\ & + \varepsilon_t. \end{aligned}\]
                        (7.2)

                        Here \(c\) is the intercept, \(\phi_j\) and \(\delta_j\) are the short-run coefficients of the lagged differences, and \(\varepsilon_t\) is white noise. If the series are cointegrated, all the terms of the equation are stationary: the differences because the series are \(I(1)\), and \(u_{t-1}\) by the definition of cointegration. That is why OLS and the usual \(t\)-tests are valid, and \(\hat u_{t-1}\) from step 1 of the Engle–Granger method can be used in place of \(u_{t-1}\), thanks to the super-consistency of \(\hat b\).

                        The coefficients have distinct interpretations. The slope \(b\) of the equilibrium relation is the long-run effect of \(x\) on \(y\); \(\delta_0\) is the short-run effect, within the same period. The coefficient \(\gamma\) is the speed of adjustment. If \(y_{t-1}\) was above equilibrium, \(u_{t-1} > 0\), and \(\gamma < 0\), then \(\gamma u_{t-1}\) is negative and \(y_t\) tends to fall: a fraction \(|\gamma|\) of the distance is closed in each period. Error correction requires \(-2 < \gamma < 0\); for \(\gamma\) between \(-1\) and 0 the adjustment is monotone.

                        In brief

                        • With \(u_{t-1} = y_{t-1} - a - b x_{t-1}\) (last period's equilibrium error), the error correction model (ECM) is
                          • \(\Delta y_t = c + \gamma\,u_{t-1} + \delta_0\,\Delta x_t + \sum_{j \ge 1}(\phi_j\Delta y_{t-j} + \delta_j\Delta x_{t-j}) + \varepsilon_t\)
                        • Every term is stationary: OLS and the usual \(t\)-tests are valid (with \(\hat u_{t-1}\) from step 1)
                          • \(b\): the long-run effect of \(x\) on \(y\); \(\delta_0\): the short-run (same-period) effect
                          • \(\gamma\): the speed of adjustment; error correction requires \(-2 < \gamma < 0\)
                          • \(c\): constant; \(\phi_j\), \(\delta_j\): short-run coefficients of the lagged differences; \(\varepsilon_t\): white noise
                        • Reading: if \(y_{t-1}\) was above equilibrium (\(u_{t-1} > 0\)) and \(\gamma < 0\), then \(y_t\) tends to fall: a share \(|\gamma|\) of the gap is closed each period

                        Speed of adjustment and half-life

                        Slide 31 in the PDF

                        The speed \(\gamma\) is hard to read directly; a time is easier to communicate. In a two-variable system in which only \(y\) adjusts, without new shocks, the equilibrium error evolves as \(u_t = (1 + \gamma)\,u_{t-1}\), so after \(h\) periods \(u_h = (1 + \gamma)^h u_0\) remains. The half-life is the number of periods after which half of a deviation has gone:

                        \[(1 + \gamma)^{h} = 0.5 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 0.5}{\ln(1 + \gamma)}.\]
                        (7.3)

                        The worked example uses \(\gamma = -0.2\): \(h_{1/2} = \ln 0.5/\ln 0.8 = 3.1\) periods. On monthly data, half of a deviation disappears in about three months; on quarterly data, in about three quarters, that is, nine months. The same value of \(\gamma\) therefore means very different real speeds.

                        That is why half-lives are compared only in the same time unit. A \(\gamma\) estimated on monthly data and one estimated on quarterly data are not directly comparable; they must first be turned into half-lives and expressed in the same unit.

                        In brief

                        • Without new shocks, the equilibrium error evolves as \(u_t = (1 + \gamma)\,u_{t-1}\), so \(u_h = (1 + \gamma)^h u_0\)
                          • (in a bivariate system where only \(y\) adjusts)
                        • Half-life: the number of periods after which half of a deviation is gone

                                      Case study: the consumption function of DHSY (1978)

                                      Slide 35 in the PDF

                                      The question arose in the United Kingdom of the 1970s: consumption functions estimated in levels or in differences forecast consumers' expenditure poorly. Davidson et al. (1978), the paper by Davidson, Hendry, Srba and Yeo, known by its initials as DHSY, analysed quarterly British data on consumers' expenditure and income.

                                      The method started from a large dynamic model and reduced it step by step, through a general-to-specific search. The final model is written in differences but also contains the lagged log ratio of expenditure to income, that is, an error correction term. This term pulls consumption back towards a stable share of income and explains why the models in differences alone went wrong.

                                      The legacy of the paper is that the ECM form appeared before the theory that justifies it. Granger (1981) and Engle and Granger (1987) then showed why it works: expenditure and income are cointegrated, and the representation theorem guarantees an error correction form. The \(t\)-statistic of \(\gamma\) in an ECM can even serve as a cointegration test, with its own critical values (Banerjee, Dolado and Mestre, 1998).

                                      The VECM and the Granger representation theorem

                                      The section moves from one equation to a system. It starts from the VAR model of Chapter 6 and rewrites it as a vector error correction model (VECM). The rank of the long-run matrix \(\Pi\) says whether and how many cointegrating relations exist, and the Granger representation theorem links cointegration to error correction. Then come a two-variable example, the five cases of deterministic terms and the notion of weak exogeneity.

                                      From a VAR to a VECM

                                      Slide 37 in the PDF

                                      A VAR(\(p\)) in levels, as in Chapter 6, is \(\mathbf y_t = \mathbf c + A_1\mathbf y_{t-1} + \dots + A_p\mathbf y_{t-p} + \mathbf u_t\). Subtracting \(\mathbf y_{t-1}\) from both sides and regrouping the lags, the same model is written in vector error correction form:

                                      \[\begin{aligned} \Delta\mathbf y_t &= \mathbf c + \Pi\,\mathbf y_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\Gamma_i\,\Delta\mathbf y_{t-i} \\ &\quad + \mathbf u_t, \\ \Pi &= A_1 + \dots + A_p - I, \\ \Gamma_i &= -(A_{i+1} + \dots + A_p). \end{aligned}\]
                                      (7.4)

                                      The matrix \(\Pi\) is the long-run matrix: it multiplies the lagged levels. The matrices \(\Gamma_i\) are the short-run matrices, which multiply the lagged differences, and \(I\) is the identity matrix.

                                      The derivation for \(p = 2\) shows how the regrouping works. Start from \(\mathbf y_t - \mathbf y_{t-1} = (A_1 - I)\mathbf y_{t-1} + A_2\mathbf y_{t-2} + \mathbf u_t\), add and subtract \(A_2\mathbf y_{t-1}\), and obtain \(\Delta\mathbf y_t = (A_1 + A_2 - I)\mathbf y_{t-1} - A_2\Delta\mathbf y_{t-1} + \mathbf u_t\), so \(\Pi = A_1 + A_2 - I\) and \(\Gamma_1 = -A_2\).

                                      The VECM form is a rewriting, not a new model: it has the same parameters and the same likelihood as the VAR in levels. Its advantage is that it separates the short-run dynamics, in \(\Gamma_i\), from the long-run structure, which is concentrated entirely in the matrix \(\Pi\).

                                      In brief

                                      • Chapter 6: a VAR(\(p\)) in levels, \(\mathbf y_t = \mathbf c + A_1\mathbf y_{t-1} + \dots + A_p\mathbf y_{t-p} + \mathbf u_t\)
                                        • subtract \(\mathbf y_{t-1}\) and regroup the lags: the same model in vector error correction form (VECM)
                                      • \(\Delta\mathbf y_t = \mathbf c + \Pi\,\mathbf y_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\Gamma_i\,\Delta\mathbf y_{t-i} + \mathbf u_t\),   \(\Pi = A_1 + \dots + A_p - I\),   \(\Gamma_i = -(A_{i+1} + \dots + A_p)\)
                                        • \(\Pi\): the long-run matrix (it multiplies the levels); \(\Gamma_i\): the short-run matrices; \(I\): the identity matrix
                                      • Derivation for \(p = 2\): \(\mathbf y_t - \mathbf y_{t-1} = (A_1 - I)\mathbf y_{t-1} + A_2\mathbf y_{t-2} + \mathbf u_t\); add and subtract \(A_2\mathbf y_{t-1}\):
                                        • \(\Delta\mathbf y_t = (A_1 + A_2 - I)\,\mathbf y_{t-1} - A_2\,\Delta\mathbf y_{t-1} + \mathbf u_t\): \(\Pi = A_1 + A_2 - I\), \(\Gamma_1 = -A_2\)
                                      • The VECM is a rewriting, not a new model: all the information about the long run sits in the matrix \(\Pi\)

                                      The rank of \(\Pi\)

                                      Slide 38 in the PDF

                                      In (7.4) the left-hand side \(\Delta\mathbf y_t\) is stationary, because the series are \(I(1)\). The right-hand side must then be stationary too, hence also \(\Pi\mathbf y_{t-1}\), although \(\mathbf y_{t-1}\) is not. This condition restricts the matrix \(\Pi\), and the restriction is expressed through its rank \(r = \mathrm{rank}(\Pi)\), with three cases.

                                      If \(r = 0\), then \(\Pi = 0\), there is no cointegration, and the right model is a VAR in differences. If \(r = n\), the matrix \(\Pi\) has full rank and is invertible; then \(\mathbf y_{t-1} = \Pi^{-1}(\Pi\mathbf y_{t-1})\) is itself stationary, and the right model is a VAR in levels, as in Chapter 6. The interesting case is \(0 < r < n\): the series are cointegrated with \(r\) vectors, and the matrix factors as \(\Pi = \alpha\beta^\top\), with \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) of dimension \(n \times r\).

                                      In this factorisation, \(\beta^\top\mathbf y_{t-1}\) are the \(r\) equilibrium errors, and \(\alpha\) contains the adjustment coefficients (loadings): the element \(\alpha_{ij}\) says how strongly variable \(i\) reacts to equilibrium error \(j\). Only the product is unique: for any invertible \(r \times r\) matrix \(Q\), \((\alpha Q)(\beta Q^{-\top})^\top = \alpha QQ^{-1}\beta^\top = \alpha\beta^\top\). That is why \(\beta\) is normalised, as in the single-equation case. Testing for cointegration thus becomes testing the rank of \(\Pi\), the subject of Section 5.

                                      In brief

                                      • \(\Delta\mathbf y_t\) is stationary; so \(\Pi\mathbf y_{t-1}\) must be stationary too. Three cases, by \(r = \mathrm{rank}(\Pi)\):
                                      • \(r = 0\): \(\Pi = 0\); no cointegration; the right model is a VAR in differences
                                      • \(r = n\): \(\Pi\) has full rank; \(\mathbf y_t\) is already stationary; a VAR in levels (Chapter 6)
                                      • \(0 < r < n\): cointegration with \(r\) vectors; \(\Pi = \alpha\beta^\top\) with \(\alpha\), \(\beta\) of size \(n \times r\)
                                        • \(\beta^\top\mathbf y_{t-1}\): the \(r\) equilibrium errors; \(\alpha\): the adjustment coefficients (loadings), how strongly each variable reacts to each error
                                        • only the product is unique: \(\alpha\beta^\top = (\alpha Q)(\beta Q^{-\top})^\top\) for any invertible \(Q\); we normalise \(\beta\)
                                      • Testing for cointegration = testing the rank of \(\Pi\) (Section 5)

                                      The Granger representation theorem

                                      Slide 39 in the PDF

                                      The Granger representation theorem, proved in Engle and Granger (1987), states that if \(\mathbf y_t\) is \(I(1)\) and cointegrated with rank \(r\), then it has a VECM representation with \(\Pi = \alpha\beta^\top\), and conversely. Cointegration and error correction are therefore equivalent: if an equilibrium exists, the deviation from it must influence the changes of at least one variable; otherwise nothing would pull them back towards equilibrium.

                                      The theorem has three practical consequences. First, a VAR in differences omits the term \(\alpha\beta^\top\mathbf y_{t-1}\), so it is misspecified when the series are cointegrated. Second, if \(\alpha \neq 0\), at least one variable adjusts to the equilibrium error, which depends on the other variables, so there is Granger causality, in the sense of Chapter 6, in at least one direction. Third, the levels are driven by \(n - r\) common stochastic trends, and the effects of shocks on the levels do not die out.

                                      A VAR in levels is not misspecified either: its estimators are consistent, since the VECM is only a rewriting of it. It ignores, however, the restriction \(\mathrm{rank}(\Pi) = r\), so it estimates more parameters than necessary, and some of its tests have non-standard distributions.

                                      In brief

                                      • Theorem Engle and Granger (1987): if \(\mathbf y_t\) is \(I(1)\) and cointegrated with rank \(r\), then it has a VECM representation with \(\Pi = \alpha\beta^\top\), and conversely
                                        • cointegration \(\Leftrightarrow\) error correction: the equilibrium error must feed back into the changes
                                      • Consequences
                                        • a VAR in differences omits \(\alpha\beta^\top\mathbf y_{t-1}\): it is misspecified when the series are cointegrated
                                        • \(\alpha \neq 0\): at least one variable adjusts, so there is Granger causality (Chapter 6) in at least one direction
                                        • the levels are driven by \(n - r\) common stochastic trends; the effects of shocks on the levels do not die out
                                      • A VAR in levels is not wrong either: it is consistent, but it ignores the restriction \(\mathrm{rank}(\Pi) = r\) and its tests are non-standard

                                      Worked example: a bivariate VECM

                                      Slide 41 in the PDF

                                      The example uses the system \(\Delta y_{1t} = -0.10\,(y_{1,t-1} - y_{2,t-1}) + u_{1t}\) and \(\Delta y_{2t} = 0.10\,(y_{1,t-1} - y_{2,t-1}) + u_{2t}\). The equilibrium error is the distance \(y_1 - y_2\), so \(\beta = (1, -1)^\top\), and the adjustment coefficients are \(\alpha = (-0.10;\ 0.10)^\top\). The long-run matrix is

                                      \[\Pi = \alpha\beta^\top = \begin{pmatrix} -0.10 & 0.10 \\ 0.10 & -0.10 \end{pmatrix},\]

                                      with \(\det\Pi = 0.01 - 0.01 = 0\); its columns are proportional, so the rank is 1, as in the case \(0 < r < n\).

                                      The signs of \(\alpha\) say who moves. If \(y_1\) is above \(y_2\), then \(y_1\) falls, because \(\alpha_1 < 0\), and \(y_2\) rises, because \(\alpha_2 > 0\): both variables close the distance, like the drunk and her dog. Subtracting the two equations, the distance \(z_t = y_{1t} - y_{2t}\) follows \(z_t = (1 + \beta^\top\alpha)\,z_{t-1} + (u_{1t} - u_{2t})\), with \(\beta^\top\alpha = -0.10 - 0.10 = -0.2\), so \(z_t = 0.8\,z_{t-1} + (u_{1t} - u_{2t})\). This is a stationary AR(1), with half-life \(\ln 0.5/\ln 0.8 = 3.1\) periods; the stationarity condition is \(-2 < \beta^\top\alpha < 0\).

                                      Adding the two equations, the correction terms cancel, so the average \((y_{1t} + y_{2t})/2\) changes only by \((u_{1t} + u_{2t})/2\): it is a random walk. This is the common trend, and \(n - r = 2 - 1 = 1\) confirms that there is only one.

                                      In brief

                                      • \(\Delta y_{1t} = -0.10\,(y_{1,t-1} - y_{2,t-1}) + u_{1t}\),   \(\Delta y_{2t} = 0.10\,(y_{1,t-1} - y_{2,t-1}) + u_{2t}\)
                                        • \(\beta = (1, -1)^\top\), \(\alpha = (-0.10, 0.10)^\top\); \(\Pi = \alpha\beta^\top = \begin{pmatrix} -0.10 & 0.10 \\ 0.10 & -0.10 \end{pmatrix}\), \(\det\Pi = 0\), rank 1
                                      • If \(y_1\) is above \(y_2\): \(y_1\) falls (\(\alpha_1 < 0\)) and \(y_2\) rises (\(\alpha_2 > 0\)): both close the gap
                                      • The gap \(z_t = y_{1t} - y_{2t}\): \(z_t = (1 + \beta^\top\alpha)\,z_{t-1} + (u_{1t} - u_{2t}) = 0.8\,z_{t-1} + \dots\)

                                      Slide 42 in the PDF

                                      A VECM can contain a constant and a linear trend, and their place matters: inside the cointegrating relations, \(\beta^\top\mathbf y_{t-1}\), or outside them, unrestricted.                                       Case 1, with no constant and no trend, means series without drift and relations through the origin; it is rarely realistic. Case 2 puts a constant only inside the cointegrating relations: the series have no drift, but the equilibrium errors have a non-zero mean, the right situation for yields and spreads.                                       Weak exogeneity

                                      Slide 43 in the PDF

                                      If a row of \(\alpha\) is zero, \(\alpha_i = 0\), variable \(y_i\) does not react to any equilibrium error. We say that \(y_i\) is weakly exogenous for \(\beta\): the other variables do all the adjusting. Such a variable drives the common trend, and the others follow it. An example is Euribor for Romanian interest rates, analysed in Seminar 7 (B4): it is plausible that Romanian rates adjust to euro-area rates, not the other way round.

                                      Weak exogeneity can be tested. With a single cointegrating vector, a \(t\)-test on \(\alpha_i\) suffices; in general a likelihood ratio (LR) test of the restriction \(\alpha_i = 0\) is used (Johansen, 1991). Once the rank \(r\) has been fixed, the statistic is asymptotically \(\chi^2\) with \(r\) degrees of freedom, one for each element of the row \(\alpha_i\).

                                      The notion also has a practical use. If \(x_t\) is weakly exogenous, a single-equation ECM for \(y_t\), as in Section 3, loses no information about \(\beta\): all the error correction takes place in the equation of \(y_t\), and the equation of \(x_t\) adds nothing about the equilibrium.

                                      In brief

                                      • If a row of \(\alpha\) is zero, \(\alpha_i = 0\), the variable \(y_i\) does not react to the equilibrium errors
                                        • \(y_i\) is weakly exogenous for \(\beta\): the other variables do all the adjusting
                                        • \(y_i\) pushes the common trend; e.g. Euribor for Romanian interest rates (Seminar 7, B4)
                                      • Test: a \(t\)-test on \(\alpha_i\) (one cointegrating vector) or a likelihood-ratio (LR) test of \(\alpha_i = 0\) Johansen (1991)

                                      Recap: The VECM

                                      Slide 44 in the PDF

                                      A VECM, \(\Delta\mathbf y_t = \alpha\beta^\top\mathbf y_{t-1} + \sum\Gamma_i\Delta\mathbf y_{t-i} + \mathbf u_t\), is a rewritten VAR, and the rank \(r\) of the matrix \(\Pi\) gives the number of cointegrating relations. The Granger representation theorem says that cointegration and error correction are equivalent, so a VAR in differences omits the long-run term. The matrix \(\beta\) contains the equilibria, \(\alpha\) shows who adjusts, the deterministic terms have five cases, and a zero row of \(\alpha\) means weak exogeneity.

                                      In brief

                                      • \(\Delta\mathbf y_t = \alpha\beta^\top\mathbf y_{t-1} + \sum\Gamma_i\Delta\mathbf y_{t-i} + \mathbf u_t\): a VAR rewritten; \(\mathrm{rank}(\Pi) = r\)
                                      • Granger representation: cointegration \(\Leftrightarrow\) error correction; a VAR in differences omits the long run
                                      • \(\beta\): equilibria; \(\alpha\): who adjusts; deterministic terms: five cases; \(\alpha_i = 0\): weak exogeneity

                                      Section summary

                                      The VECM form separates the short-run dynamics of a VAR from its long-run structure: all the information about the equilibria lies in \(\Pi = \alpha\beta^\top\), and its rank counts the cointegrating relations. The Granger representation theorem explains why a VAR in differences is misspecified for cointegrated series and why shocks have permanent effects on the levels. What remains is to estimate the rank from the data; this is the role of the Johansen tests.

                                      The Johansen tests

                                      Johansen (1988) and Johansen (1991) gave the method for estimating a VECM by maximum likelihood and, with it, tests for the cointegrating rank. Unlike the Engle–Granger method, the approach works directly with the system, can find several cointegrating vectors and does not depend on the choice of the left-hand variable. The section explains the idea of reduced-rank regression, defines the two test statistics, computes them on an example and applies them to three real systems.

                                      The idea: reduced-rank regression

                                      Slide 45 in the PDF

                                      The method of Johansen (1988) and Johansen (1991) estimates a VECM by maximum likelihood, assuming Gaussian errors. The idea is a reduced-rank regression. First the short-run dynamics are removed: \(\Delta\mathbf y_t\) and \(\mathbf y_{t-1}\) are each regressed on the lagged differences, and the residuals are denoted \(R_{0t}\) and \(R_{1t}\). What remains is the relation between current changes and lagged levels, cleaned of short-run effects.

                                      Then one looks for the linear combinations of \(R_{1t}\) that are most strongly correlated with \(R_{0t}\); these are the canonical correlations. Their squares are the estimated eigenvalues \(1 > \hat\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \hat\lambda_n \ge 0\). A large eigenvalue corresponds to a combination of the levels that predicts the changes well, that is, to a stationary combination acting as an equilibrium error. An eigenvalue close to 0 corresponds to a combination with no predictive power that remains \(I(1)\).

                                      The eigenvectors associated with the \(r\) largest eigenvalues give the estimator \(\hat\beta\); then \(\hat\alpha\) follows by OLS, regressing \(R_{0t}\) on \(\hat\beta^\top R_{1t}\). The method was developed at the University of Copenhagen by Søren Johansen and Katarina Juselius.

                                      Trace and maximum-eigenvalue statistics

                                      Slide 46 in the PDF

                                      The two Johansen tests are likelihood ratio tests built from the eigenvalues, where \(T\) is the number of observations:

                                      \[\begin{gathered} \lambda_{\mathrm{trace}}(r) = -T\sum_{i=r+1}^{n}\ln(1 - \hat\lambda_i), \\ \lambda_{\max}(r) = -T\ln(1 - \hat\lambda_{r+1}). \end{gathered}\]
                                      (7.5)

                                      The trace test has the null hypothesis "the rank is at most \(r\)" against the alternative "the rank is \(n\)". It sums the information in the smallest \(n - r\) eigenvalues: if all of them are close to 0, each \(-\ln(1 - \hat\lambda_i)\) is close to 0, the statistic is small and the null is not rejected. The maximum-eigenvalue test has the null hypothesis "the rank is \(r\)" against the alternative "the rank is \(r + 1\)" and uses only the next eigenvalue, \(\hat\lambda_{r+1}\).

                                      The procedure is sequential. First \(r = 0\) is tested; if it is rejected, \(r \le 1\) is tested, and so on. The estimated rank is the first \(r\) whose null hypothesis is not rejected. The null distributions of the statistics are non-standard, functionals of Brownian motions, and the critical values depend on \(n - r\) and on the case of deterministic terms; they are tabulated by Osterwald-Lenum (1992) and MacKinnon, Haug and Michelis (1999). The two tests usually agree; when they do not, most applied work prefers the trace test.

                                      In brief

                                      • Trace test: \(H_0\): rank \(\le r\), against rank \(= n\);   \(\lambda_{\mathrm{trace}}(r) = -T\sum_{i=r+1}^{n}\ln(1 - \hat\lambda_i)\)
                                        • it sums the information in the \(n - r\) smallest eigenvalues: if they are all close to 0, the statistic is small and \(H_0\) is kept
                                      • Maximum-eigenvalue test: \(H_0\): rank \(= r\), against rank \(= r + 1\);   \(\lambda_{\max}(r) = -T\ln(1 - \hat\lambda_{r+1})\)
                                        • it uses only the next eigenvalue, \(\hat\lambda_{r+1}\); \(T\): the number of observations
                                      • Sequential procedure: test \(r = 0\), then \(r \le 1\), …; the rank is the first \(r\) whose \(H_0\) is not rejected
                                        • the statistics are likelihood ratios, but their distributions are non-standard (functions of Brownian motions)
                                        • critical values depend on \(n - r\) and on the deterministic case: Osterwald-Lenum (1992), MacKinnon, Haug and Michelis (1999)
                                      • The two tests usually agree; if not, the trace test is preferred in most applied work

                                      Interpreting the three systems

                                      Slide 49 in the PDF

                                      For the yields, rank 2 means a single common trend, \(n - r = 3 - 2 = 1\). This trend is the level of interest rates, which moves all maturities together, and the two cointegrating relations are, approximately, the two spreads, which are stationary. The result agrees with the expectations hypothesis of the term structure (Campbell and Shiller, 1987) in its weak form, which requires only that the spreads be stationary.

                                      For GDP, consumption and investment, the trace statistic for \(r = 0\) is 28.9 against the critical value of 29.80: the rank is 0 at 5%, but the result is very close to rejection. Balanced-growth theory predicts rank 2, because the shares of consumption and investment in GDP should be stable. Moreover, the Engle–Granger test found consumption and income cointegrated, with p-value 0.029. The non-rejection in the Johansen test reflects low power over 203 quarters, not proof that the relations are absent.

                                      For the three currencies, the trace statistic of 24.2 is clearly below the critical value. The test finds no cointegrating relation, and the data are compatible with three separate stochastic trends; Section 8 returns to this case.

                                      Case study: Johansen and Juselius (1990)

                                      Slide 51 in the PDF

                                      The question of the paper was whether there is a stable money demand in Denmark and Finland. Johansen and Juselius (1990) analysed quarterly data on real money, real income, interest rates and, for one of the systems, inflation. A stable money demand means exactly a cointegrating relation among these variables.

                                      The method had two stages. The first estimated a VECM by maximum likelihood and chose the rank with the trace and maximum-eigenvalue tests. The second tested economic restrictions on \(\beta\) and \(\alpha\) as likelihood ratio tests: for example a unit income elasticity of money demand, \(\beta = (1, -1, \cdot)\), or the weak exogeneity of income and interest rates.

                                      The legacy of the paper is the "cointegrated VAR" methodology, set out at length by Juselius (2006): first test the rank, then test the economic hypotheses, formulated as restrictions on \(\beta\) and \(\alpha\). The paper is one of the most cited in applied econometrics.

                                                      Forecasting: VECM or VAR in differences?

                                                      The representation theorem says that a VAR in differences is misspecified for cointegrated series. The practical question is whether the correctly specified VECM also forecasts better. The section presents the theoretical arguments, then an out-of-sample forecasting experiment on US yields, for the levels and for a spread.

                                                      The theoretical arguments

                                                      Slide 59 in the PDF

                                                      VECM forecasts preserve the equilibria. As the horizon \(h\) grows, the forecast of the equilibrium error, \(\hat\beta^\top\hat{\mathbf y}_{T+h}\), returns to its mean, because the correction term pushes the forecasts towards equilibrium. A VAR in differences does not use the information in the levels: its forecasts of the spreads stay, over long horizons, close to the current value, however far from the mean it is.

                                                      Engle and Yoo (1987) showed, in simulated systems, that imposing cointegration improves long-horizon forecasts. Christoffersen and Diebold (1998) specified where these gains arise: in the cointegrating combinations. For the individual levels, the long-horizon forecast error is dominated by the common trend, which no model can predict, so a VECM and a VAR in differences have similar errors.

                                                      There is also an argument the other way. A VECM has more parameters to estimate: \(\alpha\), \(\beta\) and the constants. Their estimation error can make a VECM forecast worse in practice, especially at short horizons. The answer can only come from an out-of-sample check, with the models re-estimated at every forecast origin.

                                                      In brief

                                                      • VECM forecasts keep the equilibria: as the horizon grows, \(\hat\beta^\top\hat{\mathbf y}_{T+h}\) returns to its mean
                                                        • a VAR in differences forgets the levels: its forecasts of the spreads stay where they are
                                                      • Engle and Yoo (1987): imposing cointegration improves long-horizon forecasts in simulated systems
                                                      • Christoffersen and Diebold (1998): the gains are concentrated in the cointegrating combinations
                                                        • for the individual levels, the forecast error is dominated by the common trend, which no model can predict
                                                        • estimation error in \(\alpha\), \(\beta\) and in the constants can make a VECM worse in practice
                                                      • Test it out of sample, with re-estimation at each forecast origin

                                                        Economic examples: parity and interest rates

                                                        The section applies the tools of the chapter to three questions about the Romanian economy and the region: whether the leu exchange rate obeys purchasing power parity, whether ROBOR and Euribor are tied in the long run, and whether three Central European currencies share a trend. All three are cases in which theory suggests a cointegrating relation, and the data give a weak or negative answer.

                                                        Purchasing power parity

                                                        Slide 64 in the PDF

                                                        Relative purchasing power parity states that the change in the exchange rate offsets the inflation difference between two countries. With \(s_t = 100\ln(\mathrm{EUR/RON})\) and with \(p_t\) and \(p_t^*\) equal to \(100\ln\) of the harmonised index of consumer prices (HICP) of Romania and of the euro area, the real exchange rate is \(q_t = s_t - p_t + p_t^*\). A rise in \(q_t\) means a real depreciation of the leu, a fall a real appreciation. PPP holds in the long run if \(q_t\) is stationary, that is, if \((s_t, p_t, p_t^*)\) are cointegrated with the vector \(\beta = (1, -1, 1)\).

                                                        There are two reasons why PPP may fail. The first is the Balassa–Samuelson effect (Balassa, 1964): in a converging economy, productivity grows quickly in the tradable-goods sector, wages rise across the economy, and the prices of non-tradable services go up. The result is a trend real appreciation, hence a non-stationary \(q_t\).

                                                        The second reason is speed. Even where PPP holds, deviations from it die out slowly; the survey by Taylor and Taylor (2004) reports half-lives of several years. In a short sample, a relation with such slow correction is hard to tell apart from no relation.

                                                              An application: pairs trading

                                                              Pairs trading is the most direct financial application of cointegration: if the spread of two shares reverts to its mean, its large deviations are trading opportunities. The section describes the idea, builds a rule that uses no future information, applies it to the Banca Transilvania – BRD pair and to two groups of shares, in Bucharest and in the US, and closes with the pitfalls of the method.

                                                              The idea of pairs trading

                                                              Slide 72 in the PDF

                                                              Two shares exposed to the same shocks, for example two banks or two oil companies, may be cointegrated: their spread, the difference of the log prices adjusted by a coefficient, reverts to its mean. When the spread is unusually large, the strategy sells the relatively expensive share and buys the relatively cheap one; the position is closed when the spread has reverted.

                                                              It is a relative-value trade: because the position is long one share and short the other, the direction of the market largely cancels out, and the profit comes from error correction. Gatev, Goetzmann and Rouwenhorst (2006) studied the distance method, a variant without a cointegration test, on US shares and found profits over 1962–2002. Do and Faff (2010) showed that profits declined in the following years.

                                                              Pairs trading is a good test for any time-series idea, because it requires three things that an in-sample analysis can hide: the rule must work out of sample, after costs and on pairs chosen without looking ahead.

                                                              In brief

                                                              • Two shares exposed to the same shocks (two banks, two oil companies) may be cointegrated: their spread mean-reverts
                                                                • when the spread is unusually wide, sell the expensive share and buy the cheap one; close when the spread is back
                                                              • A relative-value trade: the market direction cancels out; the profit comes from the error correction
                                                                • Gatev, Goetzmann and Rouwenhorst (2006): the distance method on US shares, profitable over 1962–2002
                                                                • Do and Faff (2010): the profits declined in later years
                                                              • A good test of a time-series idea: it must work out of sample, after costs, and on pairs chosen without looking ahead

                                                              A rule without look-ahead

                                                              Slide 73 in the PDF

                                                              The rule has two windows that follow each other. In the formation window, the previous 252 trading days, about one year, each pair of shares is tested with Engle–Granger, and the pairs with a p-value below 0.05 are kept. For a kept pair, with prices \(P_{A,t}\) and \(P_{B,t}\), the hedge ratio \(\hat b\) is estimated by OLS of \(\ln P_A\) on \(\ln P_B\). The spread is \(s_t = \ln P_{A,t} - \hat a - \hat b\ln P_{B,t}\), and its mean \(\bar s\) and standard deviation \(\hat\sigma_s\) are also computed in the formation window.

                                                              In the trading window, the next 126 days, about six months, the formation parameters stay fixed, and the rule tracks the standardised score \(z_t = (s_t - \bar s)/\hat\sigma_s\). If \(z_t > 2\), A is relatively expensive: the strategy short-sells A and buys \(\hat b\) units of B. If \(z_t < -2\), it does the reverse. The position is closed when \(z_t\) returns to 0 or at the end of the window. The daily return per unit of gross exposure is \(\pm(r_A - \hat b\,r_B)/(1 + |\hat b|)\), where \(r_A\) and \(r_B\) are the daily log returns of the two shares, and each selected pair receives the same capital.

                                                              Trading costs are 0.20% of the gross exposure traded in Bucharest and 0.05% in the US, at every opening and every closing of a position. The key point is that every decision uses only data available at the time; a rule that estimated \(\hat b\) or \(\hat\sigma_s\) on the whole sample would have an advantage impossible in practice, called look-ahead bias.

                                                              In brief

                                                              • Formation (the previous 252 trading days): test every pair with Engle–Granger; keep the pairs with p \(<\) 0.05
                                                                • \(P_{A,t}\), \(P_{B,t}\): the prices of shares A and B; \(r_A\), \(r_B\): their daily log returns
                                                                • hedge ratio \(\hat b\) by OLS of \(\ln P_A\) on \(\ln P_B\); mean \(\bar s\) and standard deviation \(\hat\sigma_s\) of the spread \(s_t = \ln P_{A,t} - \hat a - \hat b\ln P_{B,t}\)
                                                              • Trading (the next 126 days), with the formation parameters fixed: \(z_t = (s_t - \bar s)/\hat\sigma_s\)
                                                                • \(z_t > 2\): short A, long \(\hat b\) of B; \(z_t < -2\): the opposite; close at \(z_t = 0\) or at the end of the window
                                                                • daily return per unit of gross exposure: \(\pm(r_A - \hat b\,r_B)/(1 + |\hat b|)\); equal capital per selected pair
                                                              • Costs: 0.20% (Bucharest) and 0.05% (US) of the gross exposure traded, at each opening and closing

                                                                  Pitfalls of pairs trading

                                                                  Slide 78 in the PDF

                                                                  The first pitfall is multiple testing. With 8 shares there are 28 pairs; if none of them is cointegrated, a test at the 5% level still lets through, on average, \(28 \times 0.05 = 1.4\) pairs in each window, purely by chance. Some of the selected pairs are therefore false from the start.

                                                                  The second pitfall is look-ahead: choosing the pairs, the start dates or the thresholds after seeing the whole sample, as in the TLV–BRD example. The third is breaks: mergers, recapitalisations, regulatory changes and crises change the vector \(\beta\), and the spread may no longer return to its old mean.

                                                                  The fourth pitfall is market frictions: short selling is limited on the Bucharest Stock Exchange, borrowing shares costs a fee, and bid-ask spreads are wide for small companies. The fifth is slow error correction: a half-life of several months means capital tied up for months and, sometimes, a long wait in a losing position.

                                                                  In brief

                                                                  • Multiple testing: with 28 pairs and a 5% test, about 1.4 pairs pass by chance in each window
                                                                  • Look-ahead: choosing pairs, start dates or thresholds after seeing the whole sample
                                                                  • Breaks: mergers, recapitalisations, regulation and crises change \(\beta\); the spread may never come back

                                                                  Key takeaways

                                                                  Slide 82 in the PDF

                                                                  Cointegrated \(I(1)\) series share stochastic trends, and a combination \(\beta^\top\mathbf y_t\) of them is stationary. The Engle–Granger method estimates the relation by OLS in levels and applies an ADF test to the residuals, with the MacKinnon critical values, not the Dickey–Fuller ones; the Phillips–Ouliaris test serves as a check. The error correction model explains the changes by the lagged equilibrium error, and the coefficient \(\gamma\) gives the speed of adjustment and the half-life.

                                                                  For systems, the VECM writes the long-run matrix as \(\Pi = \alpha\beta^\top\), and the Granger representation theorem says that cointegration and error correction are equivalent; the Johansen tests choose the rank \(r\). Cointegration helps to forecast the relations between series, such as spreads, not the levels, and applications, from parity to pairs trading, are evaluated out of sample and after costs.

                                                                  • Cointegrated \(I(1)\) series share common stochastic trends; a combination \(\beta^\top\mathbf y_t\) is stationary

                                                                  • ECM: changes respond to the lagged equilibrium error; \(\gamma\) gives the speed and the half-life
                                                                  • VECM: \(\Pi = \alpha\beta^\top\); Granger representation: cointegration \(\Leftrightarrow\) error correction; Johansen tests choose \(r\)
                                                                  • Cointegration helps forecast relations (spreads), not levels; applications must be judged out of sample and after costs
